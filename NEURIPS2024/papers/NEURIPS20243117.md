## Problem

Bayesian optimization algorithms almost universally terminate when a fixed evaluation budget is exhausted, leaving practitioners without an interpretable, data-adaptive criterion for deciding when a solution is 'good enough.' This paper addresses that gap by introducing a probabilistic regret bound (PRB) stopping rule that halts search once a point is within ε of the optimum with probability at least 1−δ under the GP posterior, and provides the first convergence and correctness guarantees for such a model-based stopping rule in BO.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a GP posterior (mean μ_t and covariance k_t), a set of candidate points C ⊆ X, a regret bound ε > 0, and a risk tolerance δ > 0 that is split into δ_mod (model-level risk) and δ_est (Monte Carlo estimation risk) with δ_mod + δ_est ≤ δ. It outputs a binary stopping decision and, upon stopping, the candidate point whose estimated probability of ε-optimality exceeds 1 − δ_mod. The key operational detail is that δ_est is further scheduled across BO steps (e.g., δ_est^t = δ_est/(T−T_0)) and across sequential confidence-interval tests within each step, so the total false-positive probability is controlled by a union bound.

## Method

The PRB rule estimates Ψ_t(x) = P(r_t(x) ≤ ε) by drawing approximate GP posterior samples via random-feature parameterization and Matheron's rule, then jointly maximizing each draw to obtain f_t* and evaluating the Bernoulli indicator 1(f_t* − f_t(x) ≤ ε). Rather than marginalizing f_t(x) conditional on f_t*, the method samples f_t(x) jointly with f_t* to avoid bias from conditioning on the maximum. To make robust decisions from these Monte Carlo estimates, Algorithm 2 performs sequential hypothesis tests using Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals: it draws samples in geometrically growing batches (n_j = ⌈β^{j−1}N⌉) with shrinking risk tolerances (d_j = j^{−α}(α−1)/α · δ_est^t), stopping as soon as the interval for E[Z] excludes the threshold λ = 1 − δ_mod. Theoretical analysis via the Borell-TIS inequality and posterior variance bounds (extending Lederer et al.) proves that under mild assumptions (compact domain, Lipschitz kernel, dense query sequence), the posterior mean maximizer almost surely satisfies the PRB criterion, and the full BO loop terminates with an (ε,δ)-optimal solution with probability at least 1−δ.

## Results

On synthetic GP problems with true models (GP†), PRB consistently returns (ε,δ)-optimal solutions with 96–100% success rates while often requiring the fewest function evaluations among model-based methods. Under moderate noise (γ²=10⁻²), PRB dramatically outperforms ΔCB, which fails to terminate within the budget on 4D and 6D problems (using all 256 or 512 evaluations), while PRB stops at 86 and 235 evaluations respectively with 96–100% success. On real-world hyperparameter tuning, PRB stops CNN training at 17 evaluations (100% success) versus 64 for both ΔCB and ΔES, and XGBoost at 28 evaluations (99%) versus 90 for ΔCB and 51 for ΔES.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRB is compared against four model-based stopping rules: Acq (acquisition-value threshold, [23,35]), ΔCB (confidence-bound gap, [29]), and ΔES (expected-supremum change, [22]), plus two oracle baselines. On noisy GP problems (γ²=10⁻²), PRB decisively wins: ΔCB fails to terminate within the budget on 4D (256/256 evals) and 6D (512/512 evals) problems, while PRB stops at 86 and 235 evaluations with 96–100% success. On the CNN hyperparameter tuning task, PRB stops at 17 evaluations (100% success) versus 64 for both ΔCB and ΔES and 128 for Acq, approaching the oracle budget of 11. On XGBoost, PRB (28 evals, 99%) outperforms ΔCB (90) and ΔES (51) but trails the oracle budget (8). On low-noise synthetic problems and standard benchmarks (Branin, Hartmann-3, Rosenbrock), PRB is competitive with or slightly behind ΔCB and ΔES in stopping time, but uniquely provides a formal (ε,δ)-optimality guarantee that the other baselines lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) Bayesian optimization with Gaussian process priors, (2) adaptive Monte Carlo testing (building on Bardenet et al.'s subsampled MCMC acceptance and Mnih et al.'s empirical Bernstein stopping), and (3) GP posterior sampling via pathwise conditioning (Wilson et al.). For stopping rules specifically, it departs from prior approaches like ΔCB (Makarova et al.) and ΔES (Ishibashi et al.) that rely on lower bounds or proxy quantities, instead directly estimating the probability of (ε,δ)-optimality via joint sampling of f_t and f_t*. The convergence analysis extends Grünewälder et al.'s expected-supremum bounds and Lederer et al.'s posterior variance results with the Borell-TIS inequality to establish almost-sure termination.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first BO stopping rule with formal convergence and correctness guarantees (up to model error), giving practitioners an interpretable (ε,δ) interface instead of opaque fixed budgets. The key limitation acknowledged is model mismatch: overly confident GP posteriors (e.g., underestimated lengthscales on Hartmann-6) can trigger premature stopping, and the paper recommends conservative priors or hyperparameter marginalization as mitigations. Open questions include designing stopping-aware acquisition functions (the paper notes the one-step optimal policy is better at finding than verifying solutions) and extending the framework beyond GP priors to more expressive models.
