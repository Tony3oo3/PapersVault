## Problem

External regret, the standard learning objective in multi-agent reinforcement learning, is inadequate for adaptive opponents because it evaluates the learner's comparator policy against the same realized opponent response, ignoring the counterfactual. Policy regret—the correct counterfactual notion that competes with the best fixed policy sequence in hindsight—has never been studied in Markov games. This paper establishes fundamental barriers (linear regret with unbounded memory, exponential sample complexity even against oblivious adversaries, polynomial scaling with policy-set cardinality) and identifies 'consistent adversaries' as the minimal structural condition that restores learnability.

## Input / Output

The learner operates in a two-player Markov game with S states, A learner actions, B adversary actions, and horizon H, knowing the reward function but not the adversary's response function f: Π^m → Ψ. Each episode the learner observes states, selects actions, observes the adversary's actions and rewards, and updates her estimates; the adversary's response at step h in state s depends only on the learner's action at (h,s) under the consistency assumption. The output is a sequence of deterministic Markov policies over T episodes, with the regret guarantee depending on log|Π| = HS·log A (the log-cardinality of the policy class) and the ε-bracketing number of the adversary's response distribution class, rather than on |Π| itself.

## Method

The paper introduces two algorithms under the structural assumption of consistent adversaries, where the opponent responds identically at (h,s) to identical learner actions at (h,s) across episodes. OPO-OMLE (for m=1 memory) performs doubly optimistic value iteration: optimistic over transition kernels via Hoeffding-style confidence bonuses AND optimistic over the adversary's response distributions via MLE version spaces Θ_{hsa} at each (h,s,a) tuple, where the version space is the set of parameters within log-likelihood α of the MLE. The key insight is that consistency reduces the adversary's response to learning only HSA action distributions, making the exponentially large policy class tractable. APE-OVE (for general m≥1) extends this with adaptive policy elimination and layerwise exploration, using a low-switching design where the learner plays each exploratory policy for m−1+T_k consecutive episodes to amortize the per-switch cost inherent in policy regret. The layerwise exploration estimates transitions and adversary responses layer by layer from h=1 to h=H, identifies infrequent transitions into a set U^k, and constructs an absorbing Markov game to handle the resulting bias, with the version space refined each epoch to retain only near-optimal policies.

## Results

The paper establishes fundamental lower bounds and matching (up to logarithmic factors) upper bounds on policy regret in Markov games against adaptive adversaries. Against unbounded-memory adversaries, any learner incurs linear regret Ω(T) (Theorem 1). Against 1-memory bounded, stationary, and consistent adversaries, OPO-OMLE achieves Õ(H³S²AB + √(H⁵SA²BT)) policy regret (Theorem 4). For general m-memory bounded, stationary, and consistent adversaries, APE-OVE achieves Õ((m−1)H²SAB + √(H³SAB)(SAB(H+√S)+H²)√(T/d*)) policy regret (Theorem 5), where d* is the minimum positive visitation probability. The upper bound for m=1 is worse than the single-agent MDP lower bound Ω(min{√(H³SAT), HT}) only by a factor of H√AB, attributed to the need to learn the opponent's response.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are between upper bounds (algorithms) and lower bounds (fundamental barriers). The lower bound of Ω(min{T, |Π|}) for 1-memory bounded stationary adversaries (Theorem 3) shows that without the consistency assumption, no algorithm can avoid polynomial dependence on the policy class size, ruling out a simple reduction to a |Π|-armed bandit problem. The proposed OPO-OMLE algorithm (Theorem 4) achieves √T regret for m=1, which is the first sublinear policy regret guarantee in Markov games against adaptive adversaries. Compared to the single-agent MDP minimax lower bound Ω(min{√(H³SAT), HT}) [Domingues et al., 2021], the m=1 upper bound is worse only by a factor of H√AB, reflecting the additional cost of learning the opponent's response. For general m, APE-OVE (Theorem 5) introduces a dependence on the instance-dependent quantity d* (minimum positive visitation probability) and grows linearly with memory length m, at the cost of worse dependence on H, S, A, B compared to the m=1 case. The paper notes that the gap between the lower bound Ω(√(T(SA)^H)) for oblivious adversaries and the upper bound for consistent adversaries highlights the critical role of the consistency assumption in enabling learnability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the policy regret framework from online bandit learning (Arora et al., 2012) and repeated games (Arora et al., 2018) to the multi-agent setting of Markov games, arguing that the more commonly used external regret is inadequate when opponents adapt to the learner's strategies. Algorithmically, OPO-OMLE builds on optimistic value iteration (Azar et al., 2017) and optimistic MLE (Liu et al., 2023) to handle the unknown adversary's response distributions, while APE-OVE generalizes the adaptive policy elimination framework of Qiao et al. (2022) from single-agent MDPs to Markov games with memory-bounded opponents. The key conceptual departure from prior MARL work (Liu et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2021) is the shift from external regret or Nash equilibrium objectives to a counterfactual policy regret benchmark that accounts for the adversary's strategic response to the learner's hypothetical fixed policy sequence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that policy regret minimization in Markov games is fundamentally harder than its single-agent or bandit counterparts: even an oblivious adversary forces exponential sample complexity, and stationarity alone is insufficient when the policy space is exponentially large. The introduction of 'consistent adversaries' as a structural assumption that restores learnability is a natural and well-motivated condition, but it remains unclear whether it is the right complexity measure or whether a more general notion (analogous to VC dimension or eluder dimension) is needed. Open questions include whether the dependence on the minimum positive visitation probability d* is unavoidable, whether computationally efficient algorithms exist (current ones require iterating over an exponentially large policy set), and whether function approximation can avoid the polynomial dependence on state and action space sizes.
