## Problem

Training large-scale multi-agent systems from scratch is intractable due to the exponential growth of the joint action-state space, sparse rewards, and the 'start-up' problem. Existing knowledge-transfer approaches for MARL rely on step-by-step expert demonstrations that are impractical to produce for many agents in complex tasks, and prior work on abstract human knowledge is limited to single- or two-player settings. The paper asks whether suboptimal, abstract human knowledge (e.g., simple heuristics) can be integrated into MARL in a way that accelerates learning and improves scalability without requiring high-quality expert trajectories.

## Input / Output

The framework takes each agent's local observation vector from the SMAC game state (e.g., enemy distance, ally positions, HP values) and a set of human-authored fuzzy logic rules (8 abstract IF-THEN rules with linear membership functions) as inputs. It produces a joint action for all agents at each time step, where each agent's action is sampled from a knowledge-guided Q-value vector. The human rules are designed solely from local observations (no global state access), are intentionally suboptimal (0% win rate when used alone), and are shared identically across all agents. The group controller's cooperation graph is computed only during training to respect CTDE, while the local Q-network, knowledge controller, and knowledge integration remain active at test time.

## Method

The hhk-MARL framework is a three-level hierarchical architecture that mimics human hierarchical control (prefrontal cortex → spinal CPG → muscles). At the top level, a shared knowledge controller encodes human suboptimal knowledge as fuzzy logic rules with trainable weights β (initialized to 1), producing a human preference vector Q_F per agent. At the middle level, a hyper-network-based knowledge integration module generates state-dependent weights θ for an integration network that blends Q_F with the agent's own policy vector Q_LOC, allowing each agent to autonomously decide how much to trust the human guidance (analogous to the CPG); a decaying hyperparameter Ω encourages early exploration of the knowledge. At the bottom level, a shared group controller (RNN) outputs pairwise cooperation tendencies λ_{i,j} among agents, which are aggregated via softmax into per-agent importance weights λ^i that scale each agent's Q-value before aggregation by the base MARL algorithm's mixing network (e.g., QMIX). The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end via standard RL with the base algorithm's loss, and the framework is algorithm-agnostic, wrapping around IQL, QMIX, or Qatten without modification.

## Results

Across five SMAC scenarios scaling from 5v6 to 35v40 agents, the hhk-MARL framework consistently accelerates training convergence and improves final win rates over all three baselines (IQL, QMIX, Qatten). Most notably, hhkIQL—combining the weakest baseline with human knowledge—achieves performance comparable to standalone QMIX, the strongest baseline. At the largest scale (35m vs 40m), standalone IQL and QMIX fail to learn effectively, while their hhk counterparts still achieve meaningful win rates. The human prior knowledge used has a 0% win rate when applied in isolation, yet still provides substantial training acceleration and final performance gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against IQL (Tampuu et al., 2017), hhkIQL shows the largest relative improvement: IQL is the weakest baseline across all scenarios and fails entirely at 27m v 30m and 35m v 40m, while hhkIQL maintains strong win rates at those scales, effectively matching QMIX's best-case performance. Against QMIX (Rashid et al., 2018), hhkQMIX outperforms standalone QMIX in all five scenarios, and QMIX itself fails at the 35m v 40m scale where hhkQMIX still succeeds. Against Qatten (Yang et al., 2020), hhkQatten similarly outperforms the baseline across all scenarios with faster convergence. The paper emphasizes that these gains are achieved using only 8 abstract fuzzy-logic rules with 0% standalone win rate, demonstrating that even highly suboptimal human knowledge provides meaningful benefit when integrated through the hyper-network knowledge integration module.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of human knowledge transfer in MARL, which has predominantly relied on step-by-step action demonstrations via imitation learning or inverse RL, by instead using abstract, suboptimal fuzzy-logic rules that require far less human effort. The fuzzy-logic knowledge representation draws on Zhang et al. (Kogun, IJCAI 2020) for trainable rule weights and Li et al. (IEEE TFS 2022) for fuzzy agents in large-scale MAS, but departs from both by (a) operating in a multi-agent setting with non-stationarity and (b) introducing a hypernetwork-based knowledge integration module that lets agents autonomously decide how much to trust the human prior, analogous to the spinal central pattern generator in human hierarchical control. The graph-based group controller for agent coordination is inspired by graph attention and implicit coordination graph work, and the entire framework is designed as a plug-in compatible with existing value-factorization (QMIX), decentralized (IQL), and attention-based (Qatten) MARL algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical bottleneck in large-scale MARL: the exponential growth of the joint action-state space makes learning from scratch infeasible, yet requiring human experts to produce comprehensive step-by-step demonstrations is equally impractical. By showing that even deliberately low-quality, subjective fuzzy rules (0% win rate when used alone) can accelerate training and improve final performance across three algorithms and up to 40 agents, the paper lowers the bar for human involvement and opens the door to broader, less expert-driven knowledge injection. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to SMAC, an assumption of homogeneous agents, and the acknowledged primitiveness of fuzzy logic as a knowledge representation; open questions include whether the approach transfers to heterogeneous agent teams, whether richer knowledge representations (beyond fuzzy sets) yield further gains, and what structural properties of human knowledge make it most effective for multi-agent coordination.
