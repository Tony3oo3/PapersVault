## Problem

The circuit hypothesis posits that LLM capabilities are implemented by small subnetworks, and many circuits have been proposed in the literature, but there is no rigorous statistical framework to evaluate whether these circuits actually satisfy the idealized properties of mechanism preservation, localization, and minimality. Prior evaluation criteria (e.g., faithfulness scores, edge classification accuracy) lack reference distributions and formal significance testing, making it unclear whether a circuit's performance is meaningfully better than chance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a candidate circuit (a subgraph of the Transformer's computational graph over attention heads and MLP layers), the full Transformer model, and a task defined by a dataset and score function. Ablation is performed via zero-ablation or Symmetric Token Replacement (STR) patching, where removed edges are replaced with cached activations from a corrupted input. The output is a set of p-values and test statistics for five tests (three idealized, two flexible), yielding pass/fail verdicts per criterion. The flexible tests additionally require a user-specified reference distribution over random circuits (constructed via random walks through the graph) and a target quantile q*, allowing the stringency of the test to be tuned.

## Method

The paper formalizes three idealized circuit criteria into five hypothesis tests. The three idealized tests are: (1) an Equivalence test using a nonparametric sign test to check whether the circuit and model outperform each other equally often; (2) an Independence test using HSIC with a permutation test to verify that knocking out the circuit makes the complement's performance independent of the model's; and (3) a Minimality test that compares the impact of removing a candidate edge against removing random edges from 'inflated' circuits (the original circuit augmented with a random path), with Bonferroni correction for multiple edges. The two flexible tests—Sufficiency and Partial Necessity—use binomial tail tests comparing the candidate circuit's faithfulness or knockdown effect against random circuits drawn from a user-chosen reference distribution, where varying the reference distribution's size and composition modulates test stringency. The key methodological distinction from prior work is that no test requires searching the space of circuits; significance is established purely through reference distributions and permutation-based p-values.

## Results

Across the three idealized hypothesis tests (Equivalence, Independence, Minimality), the two synthetic Tracr circuits pass all three, validating the test framework. Among discovered circuits, the Induction circuit passes both Independence (p=0.669) and Minimality, while the Docstring circuit passes only Minimality; IOI and Greater-Than fail all three. On the flexible minimality analysis, approximately 50% of G-T edges and 20% of IOI edges can be removed with negligible impact on faithfulness, indicating these circuits are not minimal. For sufficiency, IOI and G-T circuits are significantly more faithful than random circuits at 90% of model size, while DS and Induction outperform random circuits at just 30% size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare competing methods but rather evaluates six existing circuits against a unified set of statistical criteria. The synthetic Tracr circuits (compiled RASP programs with ground-truth subnetworks) serve as positive controls, passing all three idealized tests and confirming the validity of the framework. Among discovered circuits, the Induction circuit (2-layer 8-head attention-only Transformer) is the most aligned with the circuit hypothesis, passing two of three idealized tests and showing the strongest sufficiency signal relative to its size. The Docstring circuit (4-layer attention-only Transformer) passes minimality but fails independence, suggesting it captures a necessary but not fully sufficient subnetwork. The IOI and Greater-Than circuits (both in GPT-2 small) fail all idealized tests, though they remain significantly more faithful than random subnetworks of comparable size. The partial necessity test reveals that all four discovered circuits show significant knockdown effects relative to their complements, but only Induction, IOI, and G-T maintain significance when compared against random circuits drawn from the full model, with DS failing under this stricter reference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the transformer circuit formalism of Elhage et al. (2021) and the circuit evaluation criteria of Wang et al. (2023), who proposed faithfulness, minimality, and completeness for the IOI circuit. The key departure is methodological: rather than searching the space of circuits (as Wang et al. do for minimality/completeness) or measuring edge classification accuracy (as Conmy et al.'s ACDC does), this paper recasts circuit evaluation as a suite of formal hypothesis tests with explicit null hypotheses, reference distributions, and p-values. The independence test leverages HSIC (Gretton et al., 2007), and the minimality test introduces a novel 'inflated circuit' reference distribution to avoid circularity. The empirical evaluation reuses the ACDC experimental setup and the same six benchmark circuits, positioning the contribution squarely as a new evaluation lens on existing artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous statistical framework for asking 'is this circuit actually good?' rather than reporting a faithfulness score without a reference point. The finding that well-known discovered circuits (IOI, G-T) are not minimal—losing 20–50% of edges with negligible faithfulness impact—challenges the implicit assumption that manually discovered circuits are near-optimal. The STR ablation artifact (where knocking out the full model is less damaging than knocking out the candidate circuit) reveals that knockdown metrics alone are insufficient for circuit quality assessment. Open questions include whether these results generalize beyond small attention-only transformers, the Type II error of the non-equivalence and non-independence tests, and whether the flexible tests can guide automated circuit improvement.
