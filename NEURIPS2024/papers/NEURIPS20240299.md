## Problem

Continual counting under differential privacy without expiration has a log T gap between the best upper bound O(log²(T)/ε) and the lower bound Ω(log(T)/ε). When data becomes less sensitive over time (privacy with expiration), prior work only handled the extreme case where data loses all privacy after a fixed deadline. This paper closes the gap for gradual expiration: it achieves O(log(T)/ε) additive error for a wide class of polylogarithmic expiration functions and proves a matching lower bound, showing that the log T gap disappears when privacy loss is allowed to grow with elapsed time.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a potentially unbounded stream of values in [0,1] (binary counting is the special case of {0,1}) one element at a time, along with a privacy parameter ε, a delay parameter B (the first B outputs are 0, giving perfect privacy for early data), and an expiration rate λ > 0 that controls the trade-off between how fast privacy degrades and the resulting error. At each time step t > B it outputs a single scalar: a private approximation of the prefix sum ∑_{i=1}^{t-B} x_i. The algorithm requires no knowledge of the total stream length T, uses O(B + log t) space, and runs in O(1) amortized time per step.

## Method

The algorithm is a modified pan-private binary tree mechanism built on dyadic interval decomposition over [1,∞). A Laplace noise variable Z_I is lazily drawn for every dyadic interval I, with scale (ℓ+1)^{1-λ}/ε at level ℓ, and at time t the output adds the sum of Z_I over all intervals I containing t−B. The key design choice is the uneven budget allocation across levels: level ℓ receives noise proportional to (ℓ+1)^{1−λ}, so that the total privacy loss from shifting O(log d) intervals grows as O(1 + log^λ d) rather than linearly in d. The delay of B steps provides perfect privacy for the first B inputs at the cost of O(B) additive error. The privacy proof shows that for two neighboring streams differing at position j, identical outputs up to time τ can be recovered by shifting the Laplace noise on the O(log(τ−j)) disjoint dyadic intervals covering [j, τ] each by at most 1, yielding the polylogarithmic expiration function. A matching lower bound is established via a packing argument over T/(2C) disjoint blocks, showing that any algorithm with additive error C must satisfy 2C · h(2C−1) ≥ Ω(log T / ε).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1.2) achieves an additive ℓ∞-error of O(log(T)/ε) for a wide class of privacy expiration functions g(d) = O(1 + log^λ(d−B+1)), closing the gap that exists in the no-expiration setting (where the best upper bound is O(log²(T)/ε) vs. a Ω(log(T)/ε) lower bound). The lower bound (Theorem 1.4) shows that for any algorithm with maximum error C, the product C·h(2C−1) must be Ω(log(T)/ε), and the proposed algorithm is tight when g(2C) = O(1). Empirically, Algorithm 3 exhibits polylogarithmic privacy loss growth in elapsed time d, while the windowed binary-mechanism baseline transitions to linear privacy loss after W time steps due to sequential composition across rounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Algorithm 3 against a windowed binary-mechanism baseline that partitions the stream into intervals of length W, runs the standard binary mechanism [8] on the current interval with privacy parameter ε_cur, and releases the sum of all prior intervals with a weaker guarantee ε_past (ratio ε_past/ε_cur = 0.1 in the main experiments). Both algorithms are normalized to achieve the same mean-squared error (MSE = 1000) over the stream. For small elapsed times d < W, the baseline generally achieves lower privacy loss because most of its budget is spent on the current-round binary tree. However, for d > W the baseline's privacy loss grows linearly in the number of rounds (i.e., linearly in d) due to sequential composition: each input is re-released in every subsequent round as part of the past-sum. In contrast, Algorithm 3 maintains polylogarithmic privacy loss growth (O(log^λ d)) indefinitely, never entering a linear regime. The empirical plots (Figures 4 and 5) confirm that for sufficiently large d, Algorithm 3 with λ = 1, 2, or 3 all outperform the baseline in privacy loss, with the crossover point depending on W and the chosen λ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the pan-private continual counting algorithm of Dwork et al. [16] and the binary tree mechanism of Chan et al. [8], modifying them in four key ways: uneven privacy budget allocation across dyadic interval levels, a delay parameter B, indexing intervals by t rather than t−1, and omitting a separate initialization noise. It generalizes the privacy-with-expiration model of Bolot et al. [4], who defined the formal framework but only provided algorithms for the special case where data loses all confidentiality after a fixed time. The central conceptual departure is showing that allowing privacy to decay gradually (rather than remaining constant or vanishing entirely) fundamentally changes the error landscape for continual counting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes the long-standing log T gap between upper and lower bounds for continual counting by showing that O(log(T)/ε) additive error is both achievable and necessary under a wide class of gradual privacy expiration functions, even when the expiration function is O(1) at the relevant time scale. This has direct practical implications for privacy-preserving streaming deployments (e.g., federated learning, location tracking) where data sensitivity naturally decays over time, and it provides a principled alternative to the ad-hoc 'privacy budget refresh' heuristic used in industry. Open questions include extending results to approximate DP and zero-concentrated DP, applying the framework to other continual observation problems (histograms, distinct counting), and studying slower-growing expiration functions.
