## Problem

Decision Transformer and other Reinforcement Learning via Supervised Learning (RvS) methods condition policies on observed or expected returns-to-go, but in adversarial environments these returns are contaminated by the behavior adversary's strategy during data collection. When the test-time adversary is stronger or distributionally shifted, the policy overfits to the weak behavior adversary and fails to achieve its target return. This paper is the first to formally characterize this vulnerability (Theorem 1) and to propose a robust training algorithm for DT in adversarial settings.

## Input / Output

The trained Transformer takes as input a sequence of states, protagonist actions, and a scalar target return-to-go (replaced during training with the learned in-sample minimax return-to-go), and autoregressively outputs the protagonist's next action. Adversarial action tokens are deliberately excluded from the input sequence to keep model capacity consistent across baselines, since the paper argues that rewards and states already encode sufficient information about the adversary's actions. At test time, the policy is prompted with a desired initial target return and updated return-to-go (subtracted by observed rewards) to generate actions in an autoregressive loop against a live adversary.

## Method

ARDT is a two-stage procedure. First, it estimates in-sample minimax returns-to-go using coupled Expectile Regression: two networks Q̃_ν and Q_ω are alternately updated with asymmetric MSE losses (expectile level α≈0.01 for the min-over-adversary operator, 1−α for the max-over-protagonist operator), approximating the Bellman-style minimax recursion without querying actions outside the dataset. Second, trajectories are relabeled with the learned minimax returns-to-go, and a standard Decision Transformer is trained via behavior cloning (cross-entropy for discrete, MSE for continuous actions) conditioned on these relabeled returns. The key insight is that conditioning on worst-case returns rather than observed or expected returns aligns the policy with the maximin (Nash Equilibrium) strategy, making it robust to distributional shifts in the adversary's policy at test time. A leaf regularization term ensures accurate value prediction at terminal states, and the expectile level α serves as a tunable knob between full minimax conservatism and expected-return behavior.

## Results

On MuJoCo Noisy Action Robust MDP tasks (worst-case return against 8 adversarial policies), ARDT achieves 477.9 on Hopper-lr vs. 112.1 (DT) and 66.8 (ESPER), and 508.4 on Walker2D-mr vs. 391.0 (DT) and 426.9 (ESPER). On Connect Four with mixed near-random and near-optimal data, ARDT achieves 0.55 average return against a 30%-optimal adversary vs. 0.18 (DT) and 0.44 (ESPER), maintaining positive returns up to 70% adversary optimality while DT drops to -0.57. In full-coverage sequential games, ARDT recovers the maximin (Nash Equilibrium) return against an optimal adversary, whereas DT and ESPER fail to select the robust action.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARDT is compared against vanilla Decision Transformer (DT) and ESPER (Expected Return-Conditioned DT) across all settings. Against DT, ARDT achieves dramatically higher worst-case returns in Hopper (477.9 vs. 112.1 on lr, a 4.3× improvement) and consistently outperforms DT on Connect Four at every adversary optimality level (e.g., 0.55 vs. 0.18 at 30% optimal, 0.02 vs. -0.57 at 70% optimal). Against ESPER, ARDT wins decisively in Hopper (477.9 vs. 66.8 on lr) and Walker2D-mr (508.4 vs. 426.9), but ESPER is competitive or slightly ahead in Halfcheetah (1691.8 vs. 1509.2 on lr) and Walker2D-hr (502.6 vs. 492.6). In the full-coverage sequential games, ARDT uniquely recovers the Nash Equilibrium (maximin) return against an optimal adversary, while DT fails to select the robust action and ESPER is vulnerable to adversarial perturbations. The paper also shows that simply conditioning DT on the largest worst-case return at test time is insufficient for robustness, as demonstrated in the Gambling environment where DT still fails even at the correct target return.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Reinforcement Learning via Supervised Learning (RvS) framework—specifically Decision Transformer (DT) and ESPER—into adversarial two-player zero-sum Markov games, a setting where prior RvS methods have not been analyzed. The core mechanism repurposes Expectile Regression, previously used in Implicit Q-Learning for approximating optimal Q-values, to estimate in-sample minimax returns-to-go that serve as relabeling targets for the Transformer. The paper explicitly positions itself as the first study of RvS robustness from an adversarial perspective, distinguishing from cooperative offline multi-agent DT methods and from online game-solving approaches (Nash Q-Learning, fictitious self-play) that require environment interaction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Theorem 1 provides a clean formalization of why standard DT and ESPER are fundamentally vulnerable to test-time adversaries stronger than the behavior policy, shifting the discussion from empirical observation to a structural limitation of the information function. This opens a path for robust offline RL in safety-critical domains (autonomous vehicles, robotics, cybersecurity) where adversaries or environmental perturbations are expected at deployment. Key limitations include the restriction to deterministic transitions and action-robustness (NR-MDP) only; extending to stochastic adversarial environments, state-observation attacks, and larger-scale competitive games remains open. The expectile level α also introduces a tunable conservatism knob whose optimal setting is environment-dependent, as shown in the Connect Four ablation.
