## Problem

While multi-agent reinforcement learning has advanced significantly, no flexible, general benchmark existed for comparing the cooperative capabilities of learning agents in mixed-motive settings across diverse environments and populations. Existing RL generalization benchmarks primarily measure adaptation to new physical environments rather than new social partners, leaving a critical gap in evaluating whether agents can zero-shot generalize their cooperative skills to previously unseen co-players.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240517/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a partial pixel-based observation window (88×88×3, composed of 8×8×3 sprites) and outputs discrete actions (navigate, zap, plant, clean, interact). The unit of evaluation is a multi-agent population learning algorithm (MAPLA) producing a focal population, tested in scenarios that mix focal agents with pre-trained background agents in either resident mode (more focal than background) or visitor mode (more background than focal). Scores are reported as per-capita focal return normalized against published baselines, supplemented by capability profiles inferred via hierarchical Bayesian triangulation over 16 environment tags (resident, visitor, defense, flexibility, etc.).

## Method

The contest defines a three-phase evaluation protocol (development, practice generalization, final evaluation) where participants train focal populations on four substrates (Prisoner's Dilemma Arena, Clean Up, Allelopathic Harvest, Territory Rooms) and are scored on 51 held-out scenarios with novel background populations. Background agents are constructed via a 'puppet' method that composes event-conditioned RL behaviors using hierarchical policies—one-hot goal embeddings feed into LSTM-mixed policies—enabling complex social behaviors like reciprocity, convention-following, and grim-trigger strategies. For capability analysis, the paper applies both an XGBoost assessor model and hierarchical Bayesian measurement layouts to infer interpretable capability profiles (e.g., visitorAbility, defenseAbility, flexibilityAbility) from performance across tagged scenarios, enabling prediction of agent performance on unseen environments and revealing that top-scoring teams often overfit to specific substrates while neglecting prosocial behavior toward background agents.

## Results

In the Melting Pot Contest 2023, 8 out of 23 finalist teams achieved a normalized per-capita focal return above 1.0, meaning they outperformed the best published baseline from Melting Pot 2.0 across 51 held-out scenarios spanning 4 substrates. The top-ranked team (Marlshmallows) used hard-coded, substrate-specific policies and achieved the highest aggregate score, while the next top teams employed cooperative MARL (VDN), reward shaping with IMPALA/PPO, and rule-based approaches. Confidence intervals at 95% showed minimal overlap between top teams, indicating statistically significant performance differences. The contest attracted over 600 participants across 100+ teams globally.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the set of published results from Melting Pot 2.0 [2], which includes independent PPO agents, Actor-Critic (ACB), V-MPO [25], and OPRE/PopArt [26] approaches. Eight of the 23 finalist teams surpassed the best of these baselines (normalized score > 1.0) on the full 51-scenario held-out set, demonstrating community-driven progress beyond the state of the art. The top-performing approach (hard-coded policies, Team Marlshmallows) significantly outperformed all RL-based baselines, though the paper notes this may reflect overfitting to the focal objective rather than genuine cooperative generalization. Among RL-based submissions, cooperative MARL using VDN [32] and reward shaping with IMPALA/PPO/PopArt [27,28,30] achieved competitive but lower scores than the hard-coded solution. The paper cautions that rule-based solutions showed more overfitting to focal returns at the expense of background population welfare, a limitation not captured by the single per-capita focal return metric used for ranking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the Melting Pot environment suite (Leibo et al., 2021; Agapiou et al., 2022) as a large-scale contest benchmark, deliberately shifting the evaluation axis from physical to social generalization: agents must generalize to novel co-players rather than novel environments. The formalization of 'cooperative intelligence' as average per-capita return across a distribution of cooperation-eliciting scenarios extends the Legg-Hutter intelligence definition to multi-agent settings (Hernández-Orallo et al., 2011) and aligns with the Cooperative AI program (Dafoe et al., 2020, 2021). For capability profiling, the paper introduces a novel evaluation methodology combining XGBoost-based assessment (Hernández-Orallo et al., 2022) with hierarchical Bayesian measurement layouts (Burden et al., 2023), a combination not previously applied to MARL benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The contest revealed that high-performing focal agents frequently achieved their scores through in-group coordination that harmed background population welfare, exposing a critical gap between optimizing a single focal-return metric and achieving genuinely prosocial behavior—a concern directly relevant to AI safety and value alignment. Melting Pot remains substantially unsolved, with most scenarios admitting large score improvements, and the authors identify concrete design lessons (per-substrate ranking, secondary welfare metrics, more varied held-out scenarios) for successor contests. The planned extension to LLM-based agents on the Concordia platform signals a trajectory toward evaluating cooperative intelligence in more realistic, language-grounded multi-agent settings, where the social-generalization challenge becomes even more pressing.
