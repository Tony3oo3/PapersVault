## Problem

Existing climate model validation methods either compare only long-term climatological means (RMSE, MAE) or reduce each spatial field to a single global mean before comparing distributions (Vissio et al.'s Wasserstein distance), thereby discarding local spatial variability. No prior method can compare full distributions of spatial fields defined on the sphere while preserving geographic structure, because the sliced Wasserstein distance and its convolutional extensions are defined only for finite-dimensional or square-grid data, not for continuous fields on S².
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241885/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two time series of spatial fields on the sphere (e.g., daily 2m surface temperature or total precipitation from a CMIP model and from ERA5/GPCP/NCEP reanalysis), each defined on its own latitude-longitude grid. All fields are regridded to a common 361×720 grid, and slicing is performed at 7,200 center points on a 60×120 strided grid. The output is a single scalar SCWD value (r=2) summarizing global distributional dissimilarity, plus a spatial map of local Wasserstein distances at each slice location that can be visualized to identify regions of high or low model fidelity.

## Method

The paper introduces the functional sliced Wasserstein distance (FSWD), which extends the sliced WD to distributions in P(L²(S)) by replacing linear projection slices with a convolution slicer c_s(f) = ∫_S f(u)k(s,u)du that maps each function to a scalar while preserving local spatial information. For the spherical case (SCWD), the kernel is a Wendland function of chordal distance with a range parameter l < 12,750 km, ensuring positive definiteness and thus an injective spectral correspondence via the convolution theorem on S². SCWD is computed as the L^r average over the sphere of the univariate Wasserstein distances between the pushforward measures ω_s#P and ω_s#Q at each location s, leveraging the closed-form quantile-based formula for 1D WD. The authors prove FSWD is a pseudometric with r-convexity, and demonstrate empirically that it detects simultaneous shifts in both local means and anomaly variance—changes that RMSE/MAE and the global-mean-based WD miss entirely.

## Results

For CMIP6 surface temperature, AWI-CM-1-1-MR and MPI-ESM1-2-HR achieve the lowest SCWD to ERA5 (≈1.35), while NCEP Reanalysis (SCWD ≈ 1.20) serves as the observational baseline. For CMIP6 total precipitation, NorESM2-MM achieves the lowest SCWD to GPCP (1.335), with ERA5 (1.133) as the best reference. Comparing CMIP5 to CMIP6, the median SCWD decreases for both variables, with the improvement more pronounced for precipitation, indicating greater gains in precipitation modeling. A synthetic experiment confirms that SCWD detects simultaneous changes in local means and anomaly variance, whereas RMSE/MAE detect only mean shifts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GMWD (Vissio et al., 2020), SCWD produces substantially different and more physically meaningful rankings: for surface temperature, GMWD places NCEP Reanalysis at only the median among CMIP6 models, while SCWD correctly ranks NCEP below all CMIP models (SCWD 1.20 vs. best model ≈1.35); for precipitation, ERA5 is best under SCWD (1.133) but drops to 3rd under Sliced WD and to the median under GMWD. Against RMSE/MAE on climatologies (Gleckler et al., 2016), SCWD detects additional variance-of-anomalies differences that RMSE/MAE are structurally blind to; the SAM0-UNICON model is a concrete example, ranking significantly worse under SCWD (1.984) than under RMSE (2.797) because it exhibits large anomaly-variance differences near Antarctica that RMSE/MAE cannot capture. Against the multivariate WD and Sliced WD computed on an icosahedral grid (Villani, 2009; Bonneel et al., 2015), SCWD rankings are moderately correlated but diverge for key reference datasets: ERA5 precipitation is 1st under SCWD but 3rd under Sliced WD, and the icosahedral regridding to 642 points loses spatial resolution that SCWD preserves natively. Overall, SCWD wins on interpretability (local WD maps), sensitivity to variance changes, and correct ordering of reanalysis vs. model outputs, while the baseline metrics remain competitive on mean-state accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sliced Wasserstein distance lineage (Bonneel et al. 2015 → Kolouri et al. 2019 → Nguyen and Ho 2022) by generalizing the convolution slicer from discrete square images in R^{n×n} to continuous functional data on the sphere S². The key departure is from Vissio et al. (2020), who applied WD to spatial means of climate fields, losing all local spatial structure; SCWD instead computes local WD values via Wendland kernel convolutions at every point on the sphere and aggregates them globally. The positive-definiteness of the Wendland kernel ensures the convolution is injective in spectral space, preserving full spatial information that would be lost with a flat (global-mean) kernel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SCWD provides climate scientists with a metric that simultaneously captures differences in local means, variances, and extremes across the globe, addressing a documented blind spot in RMSE/MAE (which miss variance-only changes) and GMWD (which collapses spatial structure). The synthetic experiment in Appendix E cleanly demonstrates this: SCWD detects both mean shifts and anomaly-variance scaling, while RMSE/MAE detect only the former. Open questions include whether SCWD can be promoted from a pseudometric to a full metric (requiring an invertible Radon-like transform on P(L²(S²))), its sample complexity, and whether the kernel range parameter can be learned adaptively. The modest CMIP5→CMIP6 improvement found here suggests the metric is sensitive enough to detect real but small gains in model realism.
