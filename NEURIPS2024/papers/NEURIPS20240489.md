## Problem

Despite LLMs demonstrating strong multilingual capabilities, the internal mechanism by which they process queries across languages remains largely unexplained. Prior interpretability work on attention and feed-forward structures has focused exclusively on English, leaving the multilingual processing pipeline uncharacterized. This paper asks: how do LLMs internally handle multilingualism, and can this understanding be leveraged for targeted language-specific enhancement?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240489/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

PLND consumes a free-text corpus in the target language (e.g., 20k-180k documents from OSCAR, sized to cover the majority of that language's vocabulary) and the frozen parameters of a pre-trained LLM, requiring no labels, parallel corpora, or parameter updates. It outputs a sparse set of language-specific neurons (~0.13% of all neurons) organized by layer and structure (self-attention vs. feed-forward). Downstream, deactivating or fine-tuning these neurons yields per-language performance deltas on four benchmark tasks (XQuAD, MGSM, X-CSQA, XLSum) and, in the enhancement setting, an improved model that preserves other-language performance.

## Method

The paper proposes the Multilingual Workflow (MWork) hypothesis: LLMs first convert non-English inputs into English-centric representations in early layers (understanding), then reason in English via self-attention while extracting multilingual factual knowledge via feed-forward networks in intermediate layers (task-solving), and finally revert to the query's original language in late layers (generation). To verify this, the authors introduce Parallel Language-specific Neuron Detection (PLND), which quantifies each neuron's importance for a given input as the L2 norm of the hidden-embedding change when that neuron is zeroed out, and identifies language-specific neurons as those exceeding a threshold across all documents in a language corpus. PLND is made efficient through parallel computation: diagonal mask matrices for feed-forward neurons and batched attention-weight difference tensors for self-attention neurons, avoiding sequential per-neuron traversal. Verification is performed by selectively deactivating language-specific neurons in specific layer groups and structures, confirming that each component's removal disproportionately degrades the corresponding task in non-English languages while leaving English largely intact. Finally, fine-tuning only the identified language-specific neurons with as few as 400 Wikipedia documents improves target-language performance by 3.6% (high-resource) or 2.3% (low-resource) on average without degrading other languages.

## Results

Deactivating only 0.13% of all neurons (the detected language-specific neurons) causes a ~99% drop in XLSum summarization performance across all four tested languages (e.g., Chinese drops from 61.1 to 0.00 in Vicuna), while deactivating the same number of random neurons has no measurable effect. Verification of MWork shows that deactivating language-specific neurons in the understanding layer reduces XQuAD accuracy by 7.9–15.3% in non-English languages with minimal English impact (Δ ≈ +7.4 for Vicuna). Fine-tuning language-specific neurons with just 400 Wikipedia documents yields an average improvement of 3.6% for high-resource languages (De, Fr, Zh, Es, Ru) and 2.3% for low-resource languages (Vi, Th, Ar, Sw) across four tasks, with a notable 3.5% English improvement even without English training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability study rather than a method comparison, so the key contrasts are internal ablations. The central comparison is between deactivating detected language-specific neurons and deactivating an equal number of randomly sampled neurons: the former collapses multilingual performance by ~99% on XLSum while the latter produces no change (e.g., Vicuna avg. 26.6 → 0.37 vs. 26.6 → 26.7). Within the MWork verification, deactivating language-specific neurons in the understanding layer yields the largest English-vs.-non-English gap (Δ = +7.4 for Vicuna on XQuAD), confirming that early layers handle language understanding. For reasoning (MGSM), deactivating language-specific neurons in the self-attention structure of the task-solving layer produces the sharpest non-English drop (Δ = +8.2 for Mistral), while deactivating the feed-forward structure has a smaller effect. For knowledge (X-CSQA), the feed-forward structure in the task-solving layer is the critical component (Δ = +2.3 for Mistral). The enhancement experiments compare fine-tuning language-specific neurons against fine-tuning an equivalent number of random neurons with 400 documents: the language-specific approach improves all four low-resource languages (e.g., Thai 25.6 → 28.5) while the random baseline shows no gain (25.6 → 25.2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the interpretability literature on transformer architectures—specifically the finding that feed-forward layers store factual knowledge (Geva et al., 2021) and self-attention layers support reasoning (Hou et al., 2023)—from English-only settings to multilingual processing. It departs from prior neuron-detection methods that rely on labeled data, parallel corpora, or fine-tuning by introducing PLND, which identifies language-specific neurons purely through activation significance on unlabeled free text. The MWork hypothesis synthesizes these structural insights into a unified three-stage workflow (understand → solve in English → generate in source language), a conceptual framing absent from prior multilingual interpretability work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical upshot is that multilingual capability is concentrated in a tiny fraction (~0.13%) of neurons, enabling targeted fine-tuning with as few as 400 documents to improve a specific language without degrading others—a data-efficient alternative to full-model fine-tuning. The English-centric intermediate-layer finding has broader implications for how multilingual LLMs are designed and evaluated, suggesting that cross-lingual transfer may be bottlenecked by the English reasoning stage. Open questions include whether MWork generalizes to architectures without a dominant English pretraining signal, to languages with very different scripts or morphologies, and whether the overfitting observed at 800 documents can be resolved with more diverse training data.
