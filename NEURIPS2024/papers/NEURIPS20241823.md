## Problem

Existing video-text datasets provide only short (1–3 sentence) captions for brief clips, limiting vision-language alignment to superficial content descriptions that ignore camera language and temporal structure. Additionally, current video understanding benchmarks test only short videos (<5 min) with shallow questions, leaving hallucination in dense captions, multi-hop reasoning over long videos, and temporal ordering of events in extended videos largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241823/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Vriptor consumes sampled video frames (16 or 64 frames at 224×224 resolution depending on training stage), optional voice-over transcriptions (up to 2048 tokens), and a text instruction specifying whether to produce a whole-video summary or scene-by-scene script. The output is a structured caption per scene (~145 words) including shot type, camera movement, detailed content description, and scene title, with timestamps marking scene boundaries; for multi-scene inputs the output is a coherent 'sub-script' of up to ~1.5K words. The underlying Vript dataset covers 12K untrimmed videos at 720p–2K resolution spanning 5 s to 2.9 h, yielding 420K annotated clips.

## Method

The paper introduces three training paradigms beyond standard clip-caption alignment: (1) video-script alignment, where successive scene captions are concatenated into a coherent sub-script (up to 10 scenes, ~1.5K words) using scene titles as semantic separators; (2) voice-over transcription as an auxiliary speech modality fed alongside frames to ground proper nouns and reduce hallucination; and (3) explicit video and voice-over timestamps in text format to anchor scene boundaries. Vriptor is fine-tuned from ST-LLM in two stages (16→64 input frames, 500+60 A100 GPU-hours) on Vript plus a synthetic Vript-Extend set of 200K concatenated long videos. For evaluation, the authors propose Vript-Hard with three tasks: Vript-HAL scores object/action precision and recall against ~250-word ground-truth captions using embedding-similarity matching (threshold 0.5), Vript-RR requires locating a hinted scene in a 2–40 min video then answering multi-hop questions, and Vript-ERO asks models to reorder three shuffled 10-s scenes from a 2 min–2 h video into correct chronological order.

## Results

On Vript-HAL, Vriptor-S (scene-by-scene) achieves an F1 of 56.6 (58.3 with voice-over), surpassing GPT-4V (F1 49.3) and GPT-4O (F1 49.3) by ~7 points, making it the top-performing open-source video captioning model. On MSR-VTT, Vriptor-S with 5 scenes achieves a recall of 139.5, compared to 83.0 for the whole-video strategy. On Vript-RR, GPT-4O (1 fps/100 fr) leads with 78.5% accuracy on Whole-Multiple-choice, while the best open-source model (VILA-1.5 8B) reaches only 55.3%. On Vript-ERO, even GPT-4O (1 fps/100 fr) achieves only 38.6% @3 accuracy, highlighting the extreme difficulty of temporal event re-ordering in long videos.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Vriptor-S (scene-by-scene) achieves an F1 of 56.6 on Vript-HAL, outperforming the strongest open-source baselines PLLaVA 7B (F1 32.8) and VILA-1.5 8B (F1 31.8) by over 24 points, and surpassing GPT-4V (F1 49.3) and GPT-4O (F1 49.3) by ~7 points. Vriptor-W (whole-video) achieves the highest precision (79.1, 80.3 with voice) among all models, but its recall (26.8) is lower than Vriptor-S (46.1), reflecting the trade-off between brevity and detail. Among closed-source models, GPT-4O (1 fps/100 fr) leads Vript-RR Whole-Multiple-choice at 78.5% and Vript-ERO @3 at 38.6%, while the best open-source model VILA-1.5 8B reaches only 55.3% on Vript-RR Scene-Multiple-choice. On Vript-ERO, all models struggle severely: even GPT-4O (1 fps/100 fr) correctly orders all three events in only 38.6% of cases, and open-source models like VideoChat achieve just 17.1% @3. The paper demonstrates that Vriptor's video-script alignment paradigm (concatenating multiple scene captions) and voice-over transcription are the key factors enabling its superior recall over prior open-source video LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on ST-LLM as the base architecture for Vriptor but fundamentally rethinks the video-text alignment paradigm by introducing three training strategies (video-script alignment, voice-over transcription, and timestamp grounding) that go beyond the standard clip-caption pairing used in prior work like Panda-70M and WebVid-10M. Conceptually, it departs from the image-description pipeline that prior video datasets (HD-VG-130M, Panda-70M) adopt for video, instead framing annotation as 'video scripting' that explicitly encodes camera language (shot types, movements) alongside content. For evaluation, it extends the short-video captioning and QA benchmarks (MSR-VTT, EgoSchema) into the long-video regime with tasks specifically designed to expose hallucinations, retrieval-then-reasoning, and temporal event ordering that prior benchmarks do not test.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× longer dense captions (~145 words per scene vs. ~10–17 words in prior datasets) address a core bottleneck in video-language alignment, and the Vript dataset (12K videos, 420K clips) is likely to become a standard resource for training and evaluating video LLMs. Vript-Hard fills genuine gaps: Vript-HAL is the first systematic hallucination benchmark for video LLMs, and Vript-ERO reveals that even GPT-4O achieves only ~33% on full event re-ordering, exposing a significant temporal understanding weakness. Key limitations include reliance on GPT-4V annotations without manual verification (introducing potential hallucinations into the training data), academic-use-only licensing, and the fact that the 'needle in a timestack' effect and ERO failures suggest current video LLMs still lack robust long-range temporal grounding.
