## Problem

Existing confounding detection methods either assume causal sufficiency (no unobserved confounders), impose strong parametric assumptions for identifiability, or treat confounding detection merely as a sub-step of causal graph recovery. This paper addresses the gap of providing a unified, non-parametric framework that detects and *measures* confounding strength, separates observed from unobserved confounding effects, and compares relative confounding across variable sets—without requiring causal sufficiency or parametric generative models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241970/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational data from multiple contexts (environments), where each context is generated by sparse hard or soft interventions on a subset of variables, along with the detected mechanism-shift pattern indicating which variables' causal mechanisms changed in each context. It outputs three bounded [0,1] confounding scores (CNF-1, CNF-2, CNF-3) for any pair or set of variables, with conditional variants that isolate unobserved confounding by conditioning on observed confounders. The measures are designed for relative comparison across variable pairs rather than absolute point estimation, and the number of required contexts scales with the number of variable pairs to evaluate.

## Method

The paper defines three information-theoretic confounding measures tailored to different levels of available context information. CNF-1 (Setting 1) uses directed information—the KL divergence between conditional and interventional distributions—under hard interventions, taking the minimum of bidirectional directed information and applying an exponential transform 1−e^{−min(·)} to bound the score in [0,1]. CNF-2 (Setting 2) exploits the Shift Faithfulness assumption: when a latent confounder Z's mechanism shifts, it induces correlated changes in the context-dependent expectations E_i^C and E_j^C of its children, so mutual information I(E_i^C; E_j^C) across contexts quantifies confounding. CNF-3 (Setting 3) refines CNF-2 by incorporating known causal direction, using mutual information between conditional expectations (e.g., E_{ji}^C and E_j^C) to capture the confounding signal more precisely. All three measures extend to multivariate confounding via total correlation and to conditional (unobserved) confounding by conditioning on observed confounders, with formal proofs of reflexivity, symmetry, positivity, and monotonicity under assumptions of independent causal mechanisms, sparse mechanism shifts, and confounder minimality.

## Results

On Erdős-Rényi causal graphs with 10–25 nodes and 100–500 samples per context, the three confounding measures (CNF-1, CNF-2, CNF-3) achieve F1 scores ranging from 0.68 to 0.91, with Setting 1 (directed information) generally achieving the highest recall (up to 1.0) and Setting 2 (mutual information) providing the most balanced precision-recall tradeoff. In downstream causal effect estimation, controlling for the detected confounding variable reduces the absolute bias from ~0.55 to ~0.03 (graph G₃) and from ~0.24 to ~0.04 (graph G₄) across sample sizes of 1000–5000. Small-scale simulations confirm that all three measures output exactly zero for unconfounded pairs and positive values in [0,1] for confounded pairs, validating the theoretical positivity property.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external confounding-detection baselines; instead, it validates the three proposed measures (CNF-1, CNF-2, CNF-3) through simulation studies and a downstream causal effect estimation task. The three settings are designed to be complementary: Setting 1 (directed information, hard interventions) achieves the highest recall (up to 1.0 at N=15, 20, 25) but lower precision (~0.64–0.83), while Setting 2 (mutual information, soft interventions) offers the most stable performance across scales (F1 ≈ 0.74–0.87). Setting 3 (leveraging known causal direction) performs comparably to Setting 2 when causal path direction is available. In the downstream task (Table 3), controlling for the detected confounder reduces estimation bias by roughly 90–95% relative to the uncontrolled setting, demonstrating practical utility. The authors note that for the same confounded pair, different measures may yield different absolute values, and the measures are intended for relative comparison rather than point-to-point cross-measure comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanism-shift-based confounding detection framework of Mameche et al. (AISTATS 2024) and the sparse mechanism shift hypothesis of Perry et al. (NeurIPS 2022) by shifting the focus from binary detection to quantitative measurement. While prior work (Mameche et al., Mooij et al.) uses multi-context data to identify confounded pairs as a subroutine of causal graph discovery, this paper treats confounding measurement as the primary objective, introducing three information-theoretic measures (CNF-1 via directed information, CNF-2 via mutual information of context-dependent expectations, CNF-3 via conditional mutual information with known causal direction) that also separate observed from unobserved confounding and enable relative strength comparison. The Markov property under mechanism shifts (Guo et al., NeurIPS 2023) and the directed information formalism (Raginsky, Allerton 2011) provide the theoretical scaffolding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified, non-parametric framework for not just detecting but *measuring* confounding strength, separating observed from unobserved confounding, and ranking relative confounding across variable pairs—capabilities that prior mechanism-shift methods lacked. Key limitations include the requirement for multiple contexts with known sparse mechanism shifts (which can be expensive to obtain), the fact that different measures may yield different values for the same confounded pair (limiting point-to-point comparability), and validation only on synthetic data. Open questions include applying these measures to real-world multi-context datasets, developing more efficient context utilization, and whether the Confounder Minimality assumption (Assumption 4.2) holds in practice for large-scale systems.
