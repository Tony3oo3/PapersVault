## Problem

Neural networks in domain generalization exhibit frequency shortcut learning, over-relying on specific class-discriminative frequency bands rather than semantic cues, which degrades generalization to unseen domains. Critically, prior data augmentation techniques (e.g., AugMix, style augmentation) that improve generalization metrics actually cause models to learn *more* frequency shortcuts, creating a hallucination of improved generalization. No existing method effectively prevents frequency shortcut learning by manipulating the dataset's frequency structure from a data-driven perspective.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images from a single source domain (e.g., 32×32 for Digits/CIFAR-10, 224×224 for PACS, 256×128 for person re-ID) along with their class labels. Internally, each image is decomposed via FFT into amplitude and phase spectra; the augmentation modules operate exclusively on the amplitude spectrum while preserving the original phase. The trained model outputs either a class probability vector (classification) or a feature embedding for metric-based retrieval (person re-ID), with augmented samples generated in-batch at each training iteration (3 augmented samples per original, following SADA protocol).

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary adversarial frequency augmentation modules that dynamically modify the dataset's Fourier-domain statistical structure to suppress frequency shortcut learning. AAUA treats the channel-wise mean and variance of low-frequency amplitude components as Gaussian samples, then adversarially perturbs these statistics via T=5 sign-gradient optimization steps (PGD-style) to reconstruct a disrupted low-frequency spectrum through instance normalization, thereby shifting the model's learning away from dominant low-frequency shortcuts. AAD computes backward gradients w.r.t. the full amplitude spectrum as a frequency sensitivity map, then adaptively dropouts frequency bands whose gradient magnitude exceeds a random threshold, removing high-frequency shortcuts the model has become over-reliant on. Training combines the task loss (cross-entropy or triplet loss) with a Jensen-Shannon divergence consistency loss between predictions on benign and augmented samples, ensuring the model remains invariant to the frequency perturbations.

## Results

On the Digits benchmark, the proposed method achieves 81.39% average accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline AdvST (80.10%) by 1.29% and SADA (76.56%) by 4.83%. On CIFAR-10-C, it reaches 78.33% average accuracy, surpassing FFM (77.77%) and SADA (77.33%). On PACS, the method achieves 67.91% average accuracy, beating SADA (65.71%) and ABA (66.02%), though FFM (70.4%) remains higher. For person re-ID, the method boosts mAP from 29.7 (StyleNeophile) to 33.8 on Market→Duke and from 32.2 to 34.2 on Duke→Market.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method achieves the best Digits accuracy (81.39%), surpassing the previous best AdvST (80.10%) by 1.29% and SADA (76.56%) by 4.83%. On CIFAR-10-C, it edges out FFM (77.77%) by 0.56% and SADA (77.33%) by 1.00%. On PACS, the method (67.91%) trails FFM (70.4%) by 2.49% but clearly outperforms SADA (65.71%), ABA (66.02%), and L2D (64.74%). For person re-ID, the method dominates all baselines in both directions: on Market→Duke it achieves mAP 33.8 vs. StyleNeophile's 29.7 (+4.1) and SADA's 33.1 (+0.7); on Duke→Market it reaches mAP 34.2 vs. StyleNeophile's 32.2 (+2.0) and AdvStyle's 32.0 (+2.2). Notably, the frequency shortcut evaluation (Table 3) shows the combined AAUA+AAD reduces average TPR from 0.286 (ResNet-18) to 0.212 and FPR from 0.041 to 0.030, whereas AugMix and style augmentation actually increase shortcut reliance, confirming the paper's central claim that prior augmentation methods cause hallucinated generalization gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Pinson et al.'s [24] theoretical formulation showing that neural network learning dynamics are biased toward dominant frequencies in the dataset's statistical structure, and on Wang et al.'s [35] empirical demonstration that classification models exploit frequency shortcuts. It departs from the broader data augmentation lineage (AugMix, L2D, FACT, SADA) by arguing that these methods, despite improving generalization scores, actually increase frequency shortcut reliance rather than suppress it. The key conceptual departure is using adversarial gradients in the Fourier domain to dynamically reshape the dataset's frequency structure per iteration, rather than applying fixed or random frequency perturbations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that widely-adopted augmentation strategies inadvertently amplify frequency shortcuts challenges a core assumption in the DG community and motivates re-examining how generalization gains are measured. The proposed AAUA+AAD framework offers a practical, plug-and-play augmentation pipeline that outperforms prior frequency-domain methods across classification and retrieval benchmarks. However, the approach remains indirect (modifying dataset statistics rather than directly identifying and removing shortcuts), and the DFM-filtered evaluation metric's robustness is acknowledged as an open question, limiting confidence in the shortcut-suppression claims.
