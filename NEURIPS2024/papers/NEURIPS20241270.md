## Problem

Existing LLM defenses against jailbreaking attacks—perplexity filters, input smoothing, few-shot examples—are effective against specific attacks like GCG but fail to generalize across multiple jailbreaks or adapt to new, unseen attacks. No formal optimization objective for defense has been proposed that accounts for an adaptive adversary who can freely select and combine attacks, making it unclear how defenses should evolve as new jailbreaks emerge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241270/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During optimization, RPO takes 25 harmful instructions from AdvBench and a set of three jailbreaks (GCG, PAIR, JBC) as input, iteratively generating worst-case adversarial prompts against the current suffix. It produces a single 20-token defensive suffix that is appended after the user prompt as part of the system prompt at inference time. The suffix is model-agnostic: optimized on Llama-2-7B, it transfers directly to Vicuna-13B, Qwen-14B, Llama-2-13B, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 without re-optimization, adding only 20 tokens of inference overhead.

## Method

RPO formalizes a minimax defensive objective (Eq. 8) that composes an inner adversarial minimization (the jailbreak that maximizes harmful output probability) with an outer defensive minimization (the suffix that minimizes safe-refusal loss under that worst-case attack). The algorithm alternates two steps: (1) a jailbreak generation and selection step that applies the attack from a candidate set minimizing the adversarial loss given the current suffix, simulating an adaptive adversary, and (2) a discrete token optimization step using greedy coordinate descent (similar to GCG/AutoPrompt) that replaces suffix tokens with top-k gradient-selected candidates to reduce the safe loss. Theoretical analysis (Propositions 3.1–3.4) proves that RPO guarantees non-negative ASR reduction on the training set, generalizes to new samples via McDiarmid's inequality, and remains effective against weaker unknown adversaries. The resulting suffix enhances the model's existing refusal mechanism rather than introducing a new one, explaining its cross-model and cross-attack transferability.

## Results

On JailbreakBench, RPO reduces the attack success rate (ASR) to 6% on GPT-4 and 0% on Llama-2-7B, setting the state-of-the-art across all three attacks (PAIR, GCG, JBC). Notably, RPO achieves 0% ASR on GCG for all six evaluated models (Vicuna-13B, Llama-2-7B, Qwen-7B, Llama-2-13B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4), fully neutralizing that attack. On HarmBench, where four of six attacks (AutoDAN, TAP, Few-Shot, PAP) are unseen during optimization, RPO still reduces average ASR by 18% (Vicuna), 6.6% (Llama-2), 8.7% (GPT-3.5), and 3.5% (GPT-4). Benign performance is largely preserved, with only a 0.12-point drop on MT-Bench for GPT-4 (9.32→9.20) and no measurable change on MMLU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RPO outperforms all three baseline defenses on the strongest attack (PAIR) across every model: it reduces ASR by 66% on Vicuna (16% vs. 47% for SmoothLLM), by 44% on GPT-4 (6% vs. 25% for SmoothLLM), and by 31% and 19% relative to SmoothLLM on Vicuna and GPT-4 respectively. Against GCG, RPO achieves 0% ASR on all six models, matching or slightly exceeding the best baselines (Perplexity Filter and Few-Shot also reach 0% on most models). On JBC, RPO is the weakest defense on Vicuna (70% ASR vs. 15% for Rephrasing), likely due to Vicuna's lack of safety training, but matches or beats all baselines on the remaining five models. The Few-Shot defense (Wei et al., 2024) is the closest competitor on PAIR for closed-source models (10% on GPT-4 vs. RPO's 6%), but RPO still wins by 4 points. Overall, RPO is the only defense that simultaneously achieves near-zero ASR on both PAIR and GCG across all models, and it does so with only 20 additional tokens of inference cost versus the multiple extra inference calls required by SmoothLLM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct analogy between adversarial training in vision (minimax optimization over worst-case perturbations) and LLM jailbreak defense, formalizing the first joint minimax objective that explicitly incorporates the adversary into the defense optimization. Methodologically, it repurposes the discrete token optimization machinery from GCG and AutoPrompt (greedy coordinate descent with top-k gradient selection) but inverts the objective: instead of finding tokens that maximize harmful output probability, it finds tokens that maintain refusal under worst-case jailbreaks. It departs from prior defenses (perplexity filters, SmoothLLM, rephrasing, few-shot examples) by optimizing a single 20-token suffix against multiple attacks simultaneously rather than applying heuristics or model-specific retraining, and it distinguishes itself from concurrent work (Mo et al., 2024) by optimizing across a set of attacks rather than GCG alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RPO provides a lightweight, deployable system-level defense (20 extra tokens) that transfers across open-source and closed-source models without retraining, which is practically significant for production LLM deployments where model weights are inaccessible. The theoretical guarantees (Propositions 3.1–3.4) formalize when and why the defense generalizes to unseen attacks and datasets, giving practitioners confidence beyond empirical benchmarks. Key limitations remain: the defense does not achieve full robustness on all attacks (especially on HarmBench where gains are smaller), does not cover multimodal inputs or LLM agents, and the paper acknowledges that publishing the defense may catalyze stronger adaptive attacks. Open questions include whether semantically meaningful suffixes can eliminate the minor benign-use degradation, and whether combining RPO with other guardrails (output filters, content moderation) yields complementary robustness.
