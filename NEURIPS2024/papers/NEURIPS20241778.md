## Problem

Existing multimodal sentiment analysis methods lack a comprehensive, fair evaluation protocol under random data missing, and no prior method explicitly preserves the integrity of the dominant (language) modality when it is corrupted by noise. This paper addresses both gaps by proposing a unified evaluation across MOSI, MOSEI, and SIMS at ten missing rates, and by introducing LNLN, which corrects the dominant modality using auxiliary modalities before fusion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241778/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes BERT-encoded language token sequences, Librosa-extracted audio features, and OpenFace-derived visual features, each represented as a sequence U_m^0 ∈ R^{T_m × d_m}. Random data missing is applied at rates r ∈ {0, 0.1, …, 0.9}, erasing information and filling with zeros (audio/visual) or [UNK] tokens (language). The model outputs a continuous sentiment score (regression, range −3 to 3 on MOSI/MOSEI, −1 to 1 on SIMS) and discrete class labels; evaluation reports averaged performance across all ten missing rates for each metric.

## Method

LNLN extends ALMT's Adaptive Hyper-modality Learning with a Dominant Modality Correction (DMC) module and a Reconstructor. DMC first predicts the completeness fraction w of the language modality via a two-layer Transformer encoder; a Proxy Dominant Feature Generator (two Transformer layers) then synthesizes a proxy feature from audio and visual inputs, trained adversarially with a Gradient Reverse Layer so the proxy is indistinguishable from language features. The corrected dominant feature H_d^1 = (1−w)·H_p^1 + w·H_l^1 blends the proxy and actual language representation proportionally to the predicted completeness. In the DMML module, this corrected feature serves as Query in multi-head attention with audio/visual features as Key/Value to build hyper-modality representations, which are fused by a four-layer Cross-Transformer for prediction. A separate two-layer Reconstructor reconstructs the original modality sequences from the corrupted inputs, providing an auxiliary L2 supervision signal. Training jointly optimizes four losses: completeness prediction (L2), adversarial discrimination (binary cross-entropy through GRL), reconstruction (L2), and sentiment regression (L2), with dataset-specific weighting hyperparameters.

## Results

On MOSI, LNLN achieves 34.26% Acc-7 (a 9.46% relative improvement over the next-best MMIM at 31.30%), 38.27% Acc-5, and the best MAE of 1.046. On MOSEI, LNLN reaches 45.42% Acc-7 and 77.77/79.95 F1, though it trails CENET on Acc-7 (47.18) and ALMT on MAE (0.674 vs 0.692). On SIMS, LNLN attains 34.64% Acc-5, 57.14% Acc-3, and a 79.43 F1 (a 9.17% relative improvement over ALMT's 72.76), with the best Corr of 0.397. Overall, LNLN is the only method that consistently leads or is near-best across all three datasets and most metrics under averaged random missing rates of 0–0.9.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LNLN clearly outperforms all seven baselines on MOSI, leading on Acc-7 (34.26 vs. 31.30 for MMIM), Acc-5 (38.27 vs. 37.25 for CENET), MAE (1.046 vs. 1.070 for Self-MM), and Corr (0.527 vs. 0.524 for MISA). On MOSEI, LNLN is competitive but not dominant: CENET edges it on Acc-7 (47.18 vs. 45.42) and Acc-5 (47.83 vs. 46.17), while ALMT achieves a slightly lower MAE (0.674 vs. 0.692); however, LNLN wins on Corr (0.530) and F1 (77.77/79.95). On SIMS, LNLN achieves the best Acc-5 (34.64 vs. 33.42 TETFN), Acc-3 (57.14 vs. 56.75 Self-MM), F1 (79.43 vs. 72.76 ALMT), and Corr (0.397 vs. 0.387 TETFN), though Self-MM has a marginally lower MAE (0.508 vs. 0.514). TFR-Net and CENET show the weakest overall robustness, with TFR-Net's MAE degrading to 1.200 on MOSI and CENET's Corr collapsing to 0.107 on SIMS. The paper attributes LNLN's MOSEI shortfall to its refusal to over-predict the over-represented neutral category, a trade-off that preserves calibration at the cost of raw accuracy on imbalanced splits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends ALMT (Zhang et al., EMNLP 2023), which introduced language-guided adaptive hyper-modality learning for MSA, by adding a Dominant Modality Correction (DMC) module that uses adversarial learning with a Gradient Reverse Layer to generate proxy dominant features from auxiliary modalities, plus a Reconstructor for missing data recovery. It positions itself within the 'Noise-aware MSA' subfield, distinct from the broader 'Context-based MSA' line, and draws on adversarial domain adaptation (Ganin & Lempitsky, 2015) for its correction mechanism. A second contribution is a unified empirical evaluation protocol across MOSI, MOSEI, and SIMS with standardized random missing rates (0–0.9), addressing the inconsistency in evaluation settings that prior noise-aware methods (TFR-Net, NIAT, UMDF) employed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most valuable contribution is the observation that nearly all existing MSA methods exhibit 'lazy behavior' at high missing rates—collapsing predictions toward majority classes—rather than genuinely learning robust representations, which reframes how the community should interpret high accuracy under extreme noise. The unified evaluation protocol provides a much-needed common benchmark for future robust MSA work. However, LNLN itself does not generalize well to full modality-missing scenarios, and the authors acknowledge that real-world noise (cultural context, platform effects, ASR errors) is far more complex than random erasure, leaving the practical deployment gap wide open.
