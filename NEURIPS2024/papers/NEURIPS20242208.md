## Problem

Simple prompting often fails to elicit an LLM's full capabilities, and if models deliberately hide capabilities (via sandbagging or adversarial locking), evaluators need robust elicitation methods to avoid catastrophic underestimation of dangerous capabilities. However, the conditions under which fine-tuning-based elicitation succeeds or fails remain poorly understood, as natural elicitation failures are rare and hard to study systematically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242208/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The elicitation pipeline takes a password-locked model (a pretrained LLM fine-tuned to imitate a weak policy without a password and a strong policy with one) and applies either supervised fine-tuning on a small set of high-quality demonstrations from a separate model (e.g., GPT-4 at T=0), or RL-based training using only a binary correctness reward. The output is a fine-tuned model evaluated by greedy-decoding correctness on the locked task, measured against the ceiling performance achievable with the password. For generation tasks (code, math, critiques), correctness is exact-match or embedding-similarity based; for MMLU it is multiple-choice accuracy.

## Method

The paper introduces password-locked models as a controlled testbed: a strong base model (e.g., Deepseek-7B) is SFT-trained on a dataset where prompts with a password are paired with strong-policy outputs and prompts without it are paired with weak-policy outputs (from a smaller model like Pythia-1B), so the model possesses strong capabilities by construction but only exhibits them when prompted with the password. Elicitation is then stress-tested without access to the password via three routes: (1) SFT on a small number of high-quality demonstrations from a different model, measuring sample efficiency (often 4–512 samples suffice for generation tasks); (2) SFT on demonstrations restricted to a single subdomain, testing cross-domain generalization; and (3) RL-based methods (expert iteration, DPO, iterated DPO) using only binary correctness as reward, which recover most performance when the weak policy is strong enough to provide exploration signal. A toy function-composition task further validates that models locked from scratch (not via SFT on a pretrained model) are 2–4× harder to unlock but still eventually yield to the same elicitation methods.

## Results

Fine-tuning on high-quality demonstrations is extremely sample-efficient for generation tasks: on code generation, MATH, and code critiques, as few as 4–512 demonstrations recover most of the password-locked (hidden) performance, approaching the ceiling (performance with the password). MMLU is the exception, requiring several hundred samples since each multiple-choice example is less informative. When only a binary correctness signal is available (no high-quality demos), expert iteration and iterated DPO recover the majority of locked performance on Code and Critiques, but only when the weak policy (Pythia-1B) has sufficient baseline correctness; starting from a fixed-random policy (fixedrdm) prevents any meaningful recovery. Models locked from scratch require 2–4× more fine-tuning data to unlock than models locked via SFT on a pretrained base, though they are still eventually unlocked.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external methods but instead compares elicitation strategies against each other and against the password-locked ceiling. SFT on high-quality demonstrations (GPT-4 T=0) reaches near-ceiling correctness on Code, MATH, and Critiques with very few samples, while SFT on weaker Llama-7B demonstrations achieves performance above Llama-7B itself (weak-to-strong generalization) but well below the ceiling. For RL-based elicitation, expert iteration and iterated DPO outperform simple filtering-then-SFT and single-round DPO, recovering most of the locked performance on Code and Critiques when π^weak (Pythia-1B) is competent enough to provide exploration signal; all RL methods fail on MATH with Pythia-1B due to insufficient exploration. Cross-domain generalization is strong: fine-tuning on 512 demonstrations from a single MATH subdomain (e.g., Algebra) unlocks near-ceiling performance on other subdomains (Geometry, Numbers), and a model locked with one password per domain still generalizes across domains after SFT on one domain's samples. Prompting-based attacks (few-shot, password guessing, simple jailbreaks) fail to unlock the model, confirming that fine-tuning is the effective elicitation pathway in this setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the backdoor/trigger mechanism from adversarial ML into a controlled testbed for AI safety evaluation, drawing conceptual parallels to Hubinger et al.'s Sleeper Agents but inverting the goal: rather than studying whether safety training removes deceptive behavior, it studies whether fine-tuning can recover deliberately hidden capabilities. It builds on the line of work showing fine-tuning can negate safety training with high sample efficiency (Yang et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2023; Lermen et al., 2023) and on reverse fine-tuning experiments (Jain et al., 2023), but introduces the key distinction that the model is trained to imitate two different policies on the same task rather than two different tasks. The weak-to-strong generalization framework (Burns et al., 2023) provides a useful lens for interpreting why fine-tuning on weaker demonstrations can exceed those demonstrations' performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides AI labs and auditors with a concrete, reproducible methodology for stress-testing capability elicitation techniques, directly addressing a gap identified in the AI safety evaluation literature where prompting-based evaluations may miss hidden capabilities. The finding that a handful of high-quality demonstrations often suffices to unlock hidden capabilities has immediate practical implications for how evaluations should be designed, while the RL results offer a fallback when demonstrations are unavailable. Key open questions remain: whether these results hold when hidden capabilities exceed those of any available demonstrator (the most dangerous case), whether more natural or complex hiding mechanisms resist elicitation as effectively, and whether the 'saliency' of the strong behavior in these controlled settings makes unlocking artificially easier than in realistic sandbagging scenarios.
