## Problem

Probabilistic inference over high-dimensional time series—answering questions like 'what will happen next?' or 'what path connects these two states?'—typically requires expensive generative models or iterative optimization. This paper asks whether discriminative contrastive learning can instead produce representations that admit compact, closed-form solutions for prediction, interpolation, and planning, without the computational overhead of reconstruction-based methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241877/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes time series observations from a Markov process (e.g., 39- to 46-dimensional robot states or 2D maze positions) and, given an initial observation x_0 and a final observation x_{t+}, produces a Gaussian posterior over k-dimensional intermediate waypoint representations. Inference is performed entirely in the low-dimensional representation space by inverting a tridiagonal precision matrix; in the special case where the learned dynamics matrix A is a rotation and regularization is weak, this reduces to simple linear interpolation between the initial and final representations, with the posterior mean at each waypoint being a convex combination of the forward and backward predictions.

## Method

The paper applies a regularized symmetrized infoNCE objective to temporal pairs, using two encoders: ψ(·) encodes state contents while Aψ(·) (with a learned matrix A) encodes multi-step predictions, thereby handling temporal asymmetry (e.g., climbing vs. sledding). Under two assumptions—that the marginal representation distribution is an isotropic Gaussian (justified via maximum entropy under an expected L2 norm constraint) and that the learned representations encode a probability ratio between the joint and marginal distributions—the paper proves that sequences of representations form a Gauss-Markov chain. This yields closed-form Gaussian posteriors: prediction is a single Gaussian with mean (c/(c+1))Aψ_0 and covariance (c/(c+1))I; single-waypoint planning is a Gaussian with mean Σ(A^Tψ_{t+} + Aψ_0); and multi-waypoint planning involves inverting a tridiagonal precision matrix whose structure reduces to a second-difference matrix (and hence linear interpolation) in the weak-regularization, rotation-dynamics limit.

## Results

On the 2D maze control task, using contrastive representations for waypoint planning boosts the success rate of a proportional controller by 4.5× on the most difficult goals (18% → 84%). On the 39-dimensional D4RL door-opening task and the 46-dimensional D4RL hammering task, the proposed method achieves the lowest mean squared error (MSE) between inferred and true waypoint observations across all baselines, with error bars over 500 validation trajectories. A qualitative stock-prediction experiment (S&P 500, 470 stocks, 4-year window) demonstrates that the same interpolation framework can jointly inpaint intermediate prices for all stocks given first and last observations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: (1) naive interpolation in observation space ("no planning"), (2) interpolation in PCA space, and (3) VIP [69], which learns representations via approximate dynamic programming to encode temporal distances. On the 2D maze, both PCA and VIP fail to improve the proportional controller's success rate, while the contrastive method achieves the 4.5× gain. On the 39-dim door task and 46-dim hammer task, the contrastive method achieves the lowest MSE for inferred waypoints, outperforming VIP, which despite learning nonlinear representations without reconstruction, does not expose its temporal structure in a way that supports planning by interpolation. The PCA and no-planning baselines perform poorly on both high-dimensional tasks, confirming that the input time series are highly nonlinear and that the contrastive representations are doing more than denoising.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of contrastive representation learning (Wang & Isola, 2020) by showing that regularized temporal contrastive learning yields Gaussian marginal representations, and connects the symmetrized infoNCE objective (Radford et al., 2021) to the discounted state occupancy measure used in goal-conditioned RL (Eysenbach et al., 2022). Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate literatures: the geometric analysis of contrastive/word embeddings (Arora et al., Hashimoto et al.) and probabilistic planning as inference (Attias, Theodorou et al.), showing that the representations learned by a discriminative objective form a Gauss-Markov chain whose inference reduces to inverting a tridiagonal matrix. The key departure from prior work is that planning and prediction—tasks typically handled by generative models like sequential VAEs—are shown to be solvable in closed form using only the contrastive representations, without any reconstruction loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that contrastive representations provably support closed-form inference (prediction, interpolation, waypoint planning) via a Gauss-Markov chain is a clean theoretical bridge between discriminative representation learning and probabilistic inference, potentially eliminating the need for expensive generative models in high-dimensional time-series settings. The special case where inference reduces to linear interpolation between representations is both elegant and practically appealing. However, the analysis hinges on two strong assumptions (Gaussian marginals and exact probability-ratio encoding), and the paper acknowledges it is open whether these can always be satisfied with finite-capacity networks. Empirical validation is limited to small-scale tasks (2D mazes, 39/46-dim D4RL), and the 4.5× improvement on the maze task, while encouraging, is a single-environment result; scaling to truly high-dimensional or long-horizon settings remains untested.
