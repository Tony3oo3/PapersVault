## Problem

While gradient descent conserves Noether charges along symmetry directions of the loss landscape, the behavior of SGD in these degenerate directions was poorly understood. The paper asks how gradient noise systematically biases parameter dynamics along symmetry-induced degenerate directions, and whether SGD converges to a unique, initialization-independent fixed point distinct from the strongly initialization-dependent solutions found by GD.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242976/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a per-sample loss function possessing a continuous exponential symmetry (characterized by a symmetric matrix A), the model parameters θ, SGD hyperparameters (learning rate η and batch size S, combined as σ² = η/(2S)), and the gradient noise covariance Σ(θ). It produces a unique noise equilibrium fixed point θ* along the degenerate direction, characterized by the balance condition Tr[Σ(θ*)A] = 4γC(θ*)/σ², along with explicit predictions for layer-wise norm balance and alignment in deep networks. Unlike GD, the fixed point is initialization-independent, and the convergence time scale in the degenerate direction is proportional to σ², decoupled from the risk-minimization time scale.

## Method

The paper derives the 'Noether flow' equation via Ito's lemma applied to the continuous-time SDE of SGD, showing that the Noether charge C(θ) evolves as dC/dt = σ²Tr[Σ(θ)∇²C(θ)], a purely noise-driven drift absent in GD. It unifies rescaling, scaling, and double-rotation symmetries under the 'exponential symmetry' framework where the infinitesimal generator is J(θ) = Aθ for a symmetric matrix A, making the Noether charge quadratic: C(θ) = θᵀAθ. The central result (Theorem 4.3) proves that for every exponential symmetry and every θ, there exists a unique and attractive fixed point λ* along the degenerate direction, established by showing the flow function G(θ_λ) is strictly monotonic in λ via the exponential map e^{λA}. Applied to matrix factorization and deep linear networks, this yields explicit noise-equilibrium solutions (Theorems 5.2, 5.4) where intermediate layers become rescaled orthogonal matrices with balanced norms, and the sharpness of the converged solution depends on the data covariance structure rather than being universally flat.

## Results

The paper's headline result is Theorem 4.3 (Fixed Point Theorem): for every exponential symmetry and every parameter θ, there exists a unique and attractive fixed point θ* along the degenerate direction, where gradient noise from positive- and negative-eigenvalue subspaces of the symmetry matrix A balance exactly. In the two-layer linear network (Theorem 5.2), SGD converges to the noise-aligned solution WΓ_W W^T = U^T Γ_U U, which is an order of magnitude away from the GD solution even from the same initialization (Figure 2). For deep linear networks (Theorem 5.4), all intermediate layers converge to identical norms Tr[Σ_i²] = (Tr S′)^{2/D} d^{1−2/D}, while only the first and last layers carry information (Figure 5). The progressive flattening/sharpening prediction is confirmed: Kaiming initialization yields S_init ≥ S_end (stability improves), while Xavier initialization yields S_init ≈ S_end (mild sharpening), and warmup is shown to be necessary precisely because SGD improves the stability condition S during early training (Figure 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between SGD and GD (and GD+weight decay) on identical architectures and initializations. On matrix factorization, SGD converges to the theoretically predicted noise equilibrium (WΓ_W W^T = U^T Γ_U U) with all elements of the alignment residual Δ_ij fluctuating near zero, while GD solutions remain an order of magnitude away from this equilibrium and are strongly initialization-dependent (Figure 2). On deep linear networks trained on MNIST, SGD produces balanced intermediate-layer norms regardless of initialization (same-norm or random-norm init), matching Theorem 5.4, whereas GD does not exhibit this balancing (Figure 5). For sharpness, the paper shows that SGD can converge to either sharper or flatter solutions depending on the data covariance structure (φ_x parameter), in contrast to the common narrative that SGD always prefers flat minima; weight decay, by contrast, always drives toward norm-balanced solutions regardless of data distribution (Figure 3). The noise equilibrium is shown to be robust across learning rates (η = 0.01–0.1), batch sizes (S = 10–1000), and network widths (d = 100–2000) in Figure 8, confirming that the alignment is a structural property of SGD rather than a hyperparameter artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes two closely related prior analyses of SGD under specific symmetries — Liu et al. [48] on rescaling symmetry and Li et al. [20] on scale invariance with weight decay — into a unified framework of 'exponential symmetries' governed by a symmetric matrix A. It builds on the continuous-time SDE formulation of SGD from Li et al. [19] and the conservation-law literature for gradient flow [18, 24, 34], but departs fundamentally by showing that stochasticity breaks the Noether conservation law and replaces it with a systematic 'Noether flow' driven by the Hessian of the conserved quantity. The key conceptual departure from prior conservation-law work is that SGD noise creates a unique, attractive fixed point along the degenerate direction, whereas GD trajectories remain constrained to initialization-dependent level sets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled explanation for several practical phenomena — progressive sharpening/flattening, layer-norm-induced divergence, warmup necessity, and cross-layer representation alignment — that were previously understood only empirically or through ad hoc arguments. A key limitation is that the theory applies only to exponential symmetries (quadratic Noether charges), leaving more general continuous symmetries unaddressed. Open questions include extending the analysis to non-exponential symmetries, understanding the role of these noise equilibria in very large-scale models where interpolation minima may not exist, and connecting the noise-balance mechanism to representation learning in deeper, more complex architectures.
