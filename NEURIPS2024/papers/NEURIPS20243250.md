## Problem

The SKILL-MIX evaluation revealed that smaller LLMs (e.g., LLaMA-2-70B-Chat) struggle to compose even 3 language skills in a short text, while GPT-4 handles 5–6 skills. This raises the question of whether compositional skill-combination ability, previously thought to be determined by model scale and pre-training, can instead be induced in smaller models through fine-tuning on a small set of synthetic examples demonstrating skill composition.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243250/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt specifying k language skills (drawn from a pool of 101 skills spanning rhetorical, literary, reasoning, logic, theory of mind, pragmatics, common sense, and physical knowledge categories) and a single topic, and must produce a short text of at most k−1 sentences that illustrates all k skills within that topic. Evaluation is performed by a grader model (GPT-4 or Claude 3 Opus) scoring each generation on k+3 binary criteria (one per skill, topic adherence, coherence, length), with majority voting over three grading rounds; a harder variant disallows explicitly naming the skill in the output.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages: (1) GPT-4 generates ~14,000 synthetic text pieces, each illustrating a random subset of k=1, 2, or 3 skills from a 53-skill training set (literary and rhetorical categories only) on 50 training topics, keeping only full-mark generations; (2) LLaMA-2-13B-Chat and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 are fine-tuned on these pieces for 4,000 steps (batch 64, lr 2e-5, 4×H100/A100), computing loss only on the answer tokens while mixing in pre-training data to prevent catastrophic forgetting; (3) the fine-tuned models are evaluated on held-out skills (48 skills from reasoning, logic, theory of mind, etc.) and held-out topics at k up to 5. The key finding is that training on k=2,3 compositions yields significant gains at k=4,5 (never seen in training) and that the compositional ability transfers to entirely unseen skill categories, suggesting the model acquires a meta-skill for composition rather than memorizing specific skill pairings.

## Results

Fine-tuning LLaMA-2-13B-Chat on D_SKILL-MIX(1,2,3) (13,957 texts with k=1,2,3 training skills) improves the Ratio of Full Marks for composing k=3 held-out skills from 4% to 37%, k=4 from 1% to 9%, and k=5 from 0% to 2%. Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 shows even larger gains: k=3 from 8% to 49%, k=4 from 1% to 26%, and k=5 from 1% to 13%. Critically, the models were never trained on k=4 or 5 compositions, nor on any held-out skill categories (reasoning, logic, theory of mind, etc.), yet still generalize to both. For reference, GPT-4 achieves ~50%+ Ratio of Full Marks at k=5 in the original SKILL-MIX evaluation, while no other model in that study exceeded 15% at k=3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between base models and their fine-tuned counterparts under the held-out skills setting (Setting II), where training skills (literary, rhetorical) are disjoint from test skills (reasoning, logic, theory of mind, pragmatics, common sense, physical knowledge). Fine-tuning on D_SKILL-MIX(1,2,3) dramatically outperforms the base models: LLaMA-2-13B-Chat improves from 4% to 37% at k=3, and Mistral-7B from 8% to 49%. Fine-tuning on D_SKILL-MIX(1) alone (single-skill texts) improves k=1 performance substantially (e.g., 52%→87% for LLaMA-2) but provides almost no benefit for k≥3 composition, confirming that the gains come from learning to compose skills rather than from better individual skill knowledge. The ablation in Table 4 shows that a sub-sampled 8,000-sample version of D(1,2,3) (containing k=3 examples) still significantly outperforms the full D(1,2) dataset (~10,000 samples with only k=1,2), demonstrating that skill-richer data is more data-efficient. GPT-4 remains the upper-bound reference from the original SKILL-MIX paper, achieving substantially higher scores (e.g., ~50%+ at k=5), but the fine-tuned 7B/13B models close the gap considerably, with Mistral-7B reaching 13% at k=5 held-out—far exceeding the <2% achieved by all non-GPT-4 models in the original SKILL-MIX evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the SKILL-MIX evaluation framework (Yu et al., ICLR 2023), which established a protocol for testing whether LLMs can compose k language skills in a short text, and on the theoretical analysis of Arora and Goyal (2023) on skill emergence in language models. While SKILL-MIX evaluated pre-trained models and found that only GPT-4 could reliably compose 5–6 skills, this paper departs by asking whether the compositional ability can be *induced* in smaller models (LLaMA-2-13B, Mistral-7B) through fine-tuning on a small synthetic dataset (~14K examples) generated by GPT-4. The work also situates itself within the broader compositional generalization literature spanning NLP, vision, and logical inference, but focuses specifically on natural-language skill composition as instantiated by SKILL-MIX.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that fine-tuning on texts composing only 2–3 skills substantially improves composition of 4–5 skills—and generalizes to entirely held-out skill categories (e.g., rhetorical/literary → reasoning/logic/theory of mind)—challenges the assumption that compositional ability is fixed by pre-training scale and suggests a practical recipe for boosting composition in smaller models. The paper also raises a concrete AI safety concern: improved composition ability could enable models to produce harmful content by combining individually benign skills without explicitly naming the harmful behavior. Key limitations include the narrow scope (SKILL-MIX setting only, two model families, single random seed due to compute costs), reliance on LLM-as-judge evaluation (mitigated by cross-checking with Claude 3 Opus), and the open question of how far out-of-distribution generalization extends beyond 'near-relative' skill categories.
