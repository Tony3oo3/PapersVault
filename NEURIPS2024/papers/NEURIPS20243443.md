## Problem

Existing reversible normalization methods for time series forecasting (e.g., RevIN, SAN) perform normalization over a fixed period or window in the time domain, which fails to capture distribution variations that occur at time-varying periods and intensities. The paper identifies that wavelet decomposition reveals frequency-specific distribution shifts that single-domain, fixed-window normalization cannot adequately model, leaving non-stationary factors insufficiently removed and reconstructed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243443/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

DDN is a model-agnostic plug-in that wraps around any forecasting backbone. It consumes a multivariate historical sequence X ∈ R^{M×L} (M channels, L look-back steps) and outputs a predicted future sequence Y ∈ R^{M×T} at prediction horizons T ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720}. The sliding normalization window defaults to size 7 (stride 1), and the wavelet decomposition uses a single-level DWT with learnable Coiflet-3 bases, so the module adds minimal latency relative to the underlying transformer or linear model.

## Method

DDN eliminates non-stationarity through two parallel branches: (1) Frequency Domain Normalization (FDN) applies a single-level DWT with learnable Coiflet wavelet bases to split each channel into low- and high-frequency components, performs point-level sliding normalization (window 2k+1, stride 1) on each component independently, then uses IDWT to restore the original sequence length; (2) Time Domain Normalization (TDN) applies the same sliding normalization directly on the raw sequence. The two resulting stationary sequences are combined via a learnable scalar α (β=1−α) before being fed to the forecasting model. A Distribution Prediction Model (DPM) with two FFN branches (512→1024) predicts future sliding mean and standard deviation by forecasting the deviation from the historical mean of the statistics, then de-normalization reconstructs the non-stationary output. Training follows a three-phase collaborative schedule: 5-epoch DPM pre-training on assumption-based distribution ground truth, T₁ epochs of frozen-DPM FM training, then joint fine-tuning of both modules to correct residual distribution-assumption errors.

## Results

DDN, as a plug-in module, consistently improves all four backbone models (Autoformer, FEDformer, DLinear, iTransformer) across seven real-world datasets. On the three largest datasets (Weather, Electricity, Traffic), Autoformer+DDN achieves relative MSE reductions of 19.2%, 24.7%, and 25.6% over the unnormalized baseline, while FEDformer+DDN achieves 13.1%, 16.2%, and 22.3%. In direct comparison with the strongest existing normalization method SAN, DDN achieves lower average MSE on 6 out of 7 datasets with Autoformer (e.g., 0.270 vs 0.305 on Weather, 0.177 vs 0.204 on Electricity, 0.483 vs 0.594 on Traffic) and on all 7 datasets with FEDformer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDN outperforms all four compared normalization methods (RevIN, NST, Dish-TS, SAN) on the majority of dataset-model combinations. Against RevIN (series-level statistics), DDN achieves the largest gains on highly non-stationary datasets: 28.2% lower MSE on ETTm1 and 25.6% on Traffic with Autoformer. Against Dish-TS (input/output-level prediction), DDN is dramatically superior on ETTh2 (0.403 vs 1.175 MSE with Autoformer), where Dish-TS's distribution prediction fails catastrophically. Against the most recent and strongest baseline SAN (slice-level prediction), DDN wins on 6/7 datasets with Autoformer and 6/7 with FEDformer, with the sole exception being ETTm1+FEDformer where SAN edges out DDN (0.377 vs 0.390). The paper also shows that replacing RevIN inside iTransformer with DDN still yields significant improvements (e.g., 0.301 vs 0.311 MSE on Weather at T=720), confirming DDN's advantage even when the backbone already has some non-stationarity handling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reversible normalization lineage initiated by RevIN (Kim et al., ICLR 2021) and refined by slice-level methods like SAN (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2024) and distribution-prediction approaches like Dish-TS (Fan et al., AAAI 2023). The key conceptual departure is twofold: (1) replacing fixed-window or slice-level statistics with point-level sliding statistics in the time domain, and (2) introducing a frequency-domain normalization branch via Discrete Wavelet Transform that decomposes the series into low- and high-frequency components before applying the same sliding normalization independently to each. The distribution prediction architecture borrows from Dish-TS's mean/std prediction branches, and the collaborative training strategy addresses a known weakness of assumption-based distribution ground truth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Non-stationarity remains a primary bottleneck for deploying DNN-based forecasters on real-world data, and DDN's plug-and-play design means it can be dropped into any existing forecasting backbone without architectural changes, lowering the adoption barrier. The frequency-domain perspective is a genuinely useful lens: by normalizing low- and high-frequency components separately, the method can handle slow trend drift and rapid local fluctuations with different effective window sensitivities. Open questions include how the method scales to very long sequences (the sliding window cost grows linearly), whether multi-level wavelet decomposition would further improve results, and whether the learnable wavelet bases (initialized as Coiflet) are truly necessary or whether fixed bases suffice. The paper does not report error bars beyond three-run averaging, and the evaluation is limited to seven standard benchmark datasets.
