## Problem

Prior ICL theory established that a single linear self-attention (LSA) layer can implement one-step gradient descent from zero initialization (GD-0) for linear regression with a zero-mean Gaussian task prior. However, when tasks share a common signal (non-zero mean β*), LSA incurs an irreducible additive approximation error proportional to ‖β*‖²_H that no parameter choice can eliminate. This paper asks whether the MLP component in a standard Transformer block resolves this limitation and characterizes how the full Linear Transformer Block (LTB) achieves near-optimal ICL in the presence of a shared signal.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a token matrix E ∈ R^{(d+1)×(M+1)} where the top d rows hold the transposed context features X^T alongside the query vector x, and the bottom row holds the context labels y^T alongside a zero entry (since the query label is unknown). The LTB applies a linear attention layer (with a masking matrix that prevents attending to the query column) followed by a linear MLP layer, and the scalar prediction of y is read off as the bottom-right entry of the transformed token matrix. Data are generated from a linear regression model with Gaussian features N(0, H) and a Gaussian task prior N(β*, Ψ) with additive Gaussian noise.

## Method

The paper defines a Linear Transformer Block (LTB) combining a linear self-attention component and a linear MLP component, and proves it can implement one-step gradient descent with learnable initialization (GD-β), where the initialization vector β encodes the shared signal across tasks. The central theoretical result shows that every optimal LTB model minimizing the in-class ICL risk is effectively a GD-β model, and the optimal GD-β achieves nearly Bayes optimal ICL risk (matching an optimally tuned ridge regression centered at β*) when the signal-to-noise ratio is bounded. The paper further proves global convergence of gradient flow on the population ICL risk despite non-convexity, by exploiting that the risk is strongly convex in β for fixed Γ and approximately convex in Γ once β is near β*. Empirically, GPT-2-small models without the MLP component fail to learn the shared signal (loss 8.87 vs. 0.013 for β*=10·1), confirming the theoretical approximation gap between LSA and LTB.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that the optimal LTB model achieves an ICL risk matching the Bayes optimal estimator (up to constant factors) for linear regression with a Gaussian prior and non-zero mean, while the optimal LSA model incurs an irreducible additive approximation error of at least max{2/(3(M+1)), (tr(HΨ)+σ²)²/((M+1)²tr((HΨ)²))} · ||β*||²_H. Empirically, on a GPT2-small model (6 layers, 4 heads, d=20, M=40), the model with MLP achieves a test loss of 0.013 when β*=10×1, while the model without MLP suffers a loss of 8.871 — a ~680× degradation. When β*=0, both models perform comparably (0.003 vs 0.024).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the Linear Transformer Block (LTB, with MLP) and Linear Self-Attention (LSA, without MLP). Theoretically, Theorem 4.1 establishes a strictly positive approximation gap: LSA's best ICL risk grows with ||β*||²_H while LTB's best risk is independent of β*, meaning LSA cannot learn the shared signal. Theorem 5.3 further shows that every optimal LTB model is effectively a GD-β model, and the optimal GD-β risk matches the Bayes optimal risk (Corollary 6.2) under bounded signal-to-noise ratio. Empirically, GPT2 with MLP outperforms GPT2 without MLP by a factor of ~680 in test loss when the task mean is non-zero (0.013 vs 8.871), while both perform well when the mean is zero. The paper also shows that a 'scratchpad' token (adding a row of ones to the input) does not recover the GD-β estimator, confirming that the MLP component is specifically what enables learning the shared signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting Transformer ICL to gradient descent estimators (Von Oswald et al. [35], Ahn et al. [1], Zhang et al. [38], Wu et al. [37]), which established that linear self-attention (LSA) implements one-step GD with zero initialization (GD-0) and achieves nearly Bayes optimal ICL for linear regression with a zero-mean Gaussian prior. The key departure is the introduction of a non-zero mean (β* ≠ 0) in the task prior, which captures shared signals across tasks. Under this generalization, the paper shows LSA incurs an irreducible approximation error, while a linear Transformer block (LTB) that adds a linear MLP component can implement GD-β (one-step GD with learnable initialization) and achieve nearly Bayes optimal risk. This positions the MLP layer as essential rather than incidental, revealing that prior LSA-centric theories do not fully explain Transformer ICL power.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that LSA is fundamentally insufficient for ICL when tasks share a signal challenges the prevailing theoretical narrative that attention alone explains Transformer in-context learning, and elevates the MLP component from an architectural afterthought to a theoretically necessary piece. The clean equivalence between optimal LTB and GD-β, combined with the gradient flow convergence guarantee despite non-convexity, provides a tractable theoretical framework for understanding how Transformers encode shared task structure. Open questions include the statistical complexity of learning LTB/GD-β (which is non-convex, unlike the GD-0 case), whether these results extend to non-linear attention and ReLU activations, and what information the MLP stores in practice—questions the authors explicitly flag as future work.
