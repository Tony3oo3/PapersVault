## Problem

Existing unsupervised 3D keypoint detectors are designed primarily for rigid-body objects and fail to preserve semantic consistency when objects undergo dramatic deformations such as folding, dragging, or dropping. The spatial and geometric structure of keypoints shifts substantially under deformation, making it difficult for skeleton-based or sparse-representation approaches to maintain stable, semantically meaningful keypoint assignments across shape variations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241556/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a normalized 3D point cloud of N=2048 points as input and produces K keypoints (10 for ShapeNetCoreV2, 8 for ClothesNet, 20 for SUN3D) along with learnable weights for all C(K,2) skeleton segments connecting keypoint pairs. Internally, the encoder generates a 16×16×16 grid heatmap (4096 scalar features) in normalized cubic space, and the decoder hierarchically reconstructs the full point cloud from this dense representation combined with multi-scale encoder features.

## Method

Key-Grid is an autoencoder where a 4-layer PointNet++ encoder predicts keypoints as a softmax-weighted sum of input points, and a decoder reconstructs the point cloud. The core novelty is the grid heatmap: a dense 3D feature map where each of 4096 uniformly sampled grid points is assigned the maximum weighted point-to-skeleton-segment distance (using learnable segment weights and a Gaussian kernel), providing a continuous geometric landscape that smoothly extrapolates beyond the sparse skeleton. The decoder fuses grid heatmap features, U-Net-style skip connections from each encoder layer, and a neighbor-weighted feature projection from the previous decoder level at every hierarchical stage. Training uses a Chamfer distance similarity loss between reconstructed and input point clouds plus a farthest-point-sampling keypoint loss that encourages even spatial distribution. The framework extends to SE(3)-equivariant keypoints by serving as the teacher in the USEEK framework with a SPRIN student backbone.

## Results

On ShapeNetCoreV2 (13 rigid-body categories, 10 keypoints), Key-Grid achieves an average DAS of 72.8 and mIoU of 49.0, outperforming KeypointDeformer (67.7 DAS, 40.7 mIoU), Skeleton Merger (65.8 DAS, 45.6 mIoU), and SC3K (69.7 DAS, 38.7 mIoU). On the ClothesNet deformable-object benchmark (8 keypoints), Key-Grid reaches 89.8 DAS under dropping deformation and 67.7 DAS under dragging deformation, surpassing the previous SOTA KeypointDeformer by 8.0% and 9.1% respectively. Key-Grid also outperforms supervised baselines PointNet (28.2 mIoU) and SpiderCNN (47.6 mIoU) on the KeypointNet dataset, though it trails the supervised PointConv (87.0 mIoU).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against KeypointDeformer (KD), the prior SOTA for deformable objects, Key-Grid wins on average DAS across all three deformation types (drop: 89.8 vs 81.8; drag: 67.7 vs 57.9; fold: 92.0/100.0 vs 81.6/72.4) and on ShapeNetCoreV2 (72.8 vs 67.7 DAS; 49.0 vs 40.7 mIoU), with the largest margins on categories like Long Pants (83.7 vs 57.5 DAS under drop) and Chair (93.4 vs 84.3 DAS). Against Skeleton Merger (SM), Key-Grid achieves a 7.0-point DAS advantage on ShapeNetCoreV2 (72.8 vs 65.8) and a 22.3-point advantage on ClothesNet drop (89.8 vs 67.5), though SM slightly edges out Key-Grid on mIoU for straight-line-geometry categories like Airplane (73.5 vs 74.4) and Knife (45.0 vs 46.8). Against SC3K, Key-Grid leads by 3.1 points on ShapeNetCoreV2 DAS (72.8 vs 69.7) and by a substantial 21.4 points on ClothesNet drop DAS (89.8 vs 68.4). Among supervised baselines on KeypointNet, Key-Grid (75.1 avg mIoU) clearly outperforms PointNet (28.2) and SpiderCNN (47.6) but is surpassed by PointConv (87.0), which benefits from direct keypoint supervision. The paper notes that KD requires mesh inputs for shape transformation, making the comparison somewhat unfair to Key-Grid which operates purely on point clouds.

## Relation to Prior Work

Key-Grid builds directly on the autoencoder-based unsupervised keypoint detection paradigm established by Skeleton Merger (SM) and KeypointDeformer (KD), both of which use a sparse 'skeleton' of keypoint-connected segments to guide reconstruction. The key architectural departure is replacing the use of this sparse skeleton as a one-shot decoder input with a dense 3D grid heatmap—a continuous feature field computed from point-to-skeleton distances—that is hierarchically fused with encoder features in a U-Net-style decoder. The method also inherits the PointNet++ backbone and the farthest-point distribution loss from prior work, and extends to SE(3)-equivariant keypoints via the USEEK teacher-student framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By extending unsupervised keypoint detection to deformable objects (clothing under dropping, dragging, and folding), Key-Grid addresses a practically important gap for robotic manipulation and vision tasks where rigid-body assumptions break down. The grid heatmap provides a principled dense geometric representation that forces the encoder to produce precise keypoints, and the method demonstrates robustness to noise, downsampling, and occlusion. A key limitation is the fixed, manually-set number of keypoints; the authors note that adaptive keypoint counting remains open. The striking ablation result—that removing the farthest-point distribution loss collapses DAS from ~90–100 to ~13–17—suggests that keypoint distribution constraints, rather than reconstruction fidelity, are the primary driver of semantic consistency, an insight that may reshape how future detectors are designed.
