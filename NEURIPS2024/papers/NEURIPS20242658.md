## Problem

Autoformalization research is bottlenecked by the absence of large-scale parallel datasets of informal-formal mathematical pairs that are both natural in expression and sufficiently large for neural training. Existing approaches either rely on expensive manual curation of small corpora or few-shot prompting of LLMs, both of which are limited in scale and domain coverage. Additionally, it remains unclear whether training on multiple formal languages simultaneously can improve autoformalization performance compared to single-language training.

## Input / Output

The model takes a natural-language mathematical statement (e.g., a lemma or theorem description) as input and produces a syntactically valid formal statement in either Isabelle or Lean4 as output. The MMA dataset consists of 332K informal-formal pairs: 244K from Isabelle's Archive of Formal Proofs and 88K from Lean4's mathlib4, with informal statements averaging ~340 characters and formal statements ~166 (Isabelle) or ~108 (Lean4) characters. Evaluation is performed on miniF2F (488 high-school competition problems) and ProofNet (371 undergraduate-level problems), with 50 randomly sampled problems per benchmark per model, assessed by expert manual inspection on a 0–4 correction-effort Likert scale.

## Method

The paper constructs the MMA dataset via distilled back-translation: GPT-4 is prompted with an instruction template to informalise every theorem statement extracted from Isabelle's AFP (via Portal) and Lean4's mathlib4 (via LeanDojo), producing diverse natural-language counterparts for 332K formal statements. Two open-source LLMs (LLaMA-3 33B and Mistral 7B) are then fine-tuned with cross-entropy loss (input masked) on MMA using AdamW with cosine learning-rate decay over 40K steps on a TPUv4-64. The key empirical finding is that jointly fine-tuning on both Isabelle and Lean4 data yields significantly lower validation loss and higher autoformalization quality (29–31% of outputs acceptable with minimal corrections for Mistral) than fine-tuning on either language alone with the same number of training steps, demonstrating that multi-language diversity acts as a regulariser that improves data efficiency and generalisation across formal languages.

## Results

Fine-tuning Mistral 7B on the multi-language MMA dataset yields 29% (Isabelle) and 31% (Lean4) of formalisations acceptable with minimal corrections on miniF2F and ProofNet, up from 0% with the base model. LLaMA-3 33B achieves 18% and 16% respectively under the same protocol. The jointly fine-tuned model attains a lower average correction effort (2.415 for Isabelle, 2.495 for Lean4) than single-language fine-tuning (2.785 and 2.670), with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals confirming statistical significance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three fine-tuning regimes (base, single-language, multi-language) across two model families. The base LLaMA and Mistral models produce 0% acceptable formalisations; after fine-tuning on Isabelle-only data, LLaMA reaches 6% and Mistral 20% acceptable (Isabelle), while Lean4-only fine-tuning yields 10% (LLaMA) and 26% (Mistral). Multi-language fine-tuning outperforms both single-language variants: Mistral jumps to 29% (Isabelle) and 31% (Lean4), and LLaMA to 18% and 16%, despite seeing fewer tokens of each individual language. Against the prior few-shot prompting baseline (Codex, 13–16% on ProofNet, 25.3% on MATH), the best fine-tuned Mistral 7B model achieves 22% on miniF2F and 12% on ProofNet with zero-shot inference, matching or exceeding the larger Codex model on purpose-built autoformalization benchmarks. The multi-language advantage is statistically confirmed: the 95% CI for the jointly fine-tuned Isabelle effort (2.251–2.579) lies entirely below the single-language Isabelle CI (2.622–2.948).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the back-translation paradigm from neural machine translation (Sennrich et al., 2016) to the autoformalization setting: rather than manually curating informal-formal pairs or relying on few-shot prompting with LLMs (Wu et al., 2022; Azerbayev et al., 2023), it uses GPT-4 to informalise existing formal libraries (Isabelle AFP, Lean4 mathlib4), producing a 332K-pair parallel dataset. The key conceptual departure from prior autoformalization work is the multi-language dimension: no previous dataset or model handled more than one formal language, and the paper explicitly argues that informalisation is easier than formalisation, making LLM-based back-translation a viable data-generation strategy at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MMA dataset is the first multi-formal-language parallel corpus for autoformalization and is 4× larger than the prior best (Azerbayev et al., 2023), making it a practical resource for the theorem-proving and AI-for-math communities. The finding that multi-language fine-tuning is more data-efficient than single-language fine-tuning—despite seeing fewer tokens of each individual language—suggests that cross-language transfer is a meaningful axis for future autoformalization research. Key limitations include a 74% informalisation correctness rate, only one informalisation sampled per formal statement, and evaluation on just 100 problems per benchmark with manual inspection, leaving open whether the multi-language advantage persists at larger scale or with additional formal languages (Coq, HOL Light).
