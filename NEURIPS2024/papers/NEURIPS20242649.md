## Problem

Scaling laws in deep learning predict that larger datasets, larger models, and finer data granularity monotonically improve performance, yet time series forecasting exhibits seemingly contradictory behaviors: more capable models do not always outperform simpler ones, and longer look-back horizons can degrade performance. Existing scaling law theories from NLP and CV do not account for the look-back horizon, a critical and adjustable parameter unique to time series forecasting, leaving these anomalies unexplained.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a multivariate time series with N variables observed over H look-back frames and predicts the next S frames, but its primary analytical output is the optimal look-back horizon H* as a closed-form function of dataset size D, model complexity N, and the Zip-f exponent α_Z characterizing feature degradation in the intrinsic space. The loss is decomposed into a Bayesian component (irreducible uncertainty from unseen intrinsic dimensions) and an approximation component (model/data limitations), with the optimal horizon emerging from the trade-off between these two terms. The theory further distinguishes two regimes—data-dense (piecewise-linear approximation) and data-scarce (nearest-neighbor)—separated by the order parameter ξ = D/(NH).

## Method

The paper formulates time series forecasting in an intrinsic space M(L) of dimension d_I(L), where time series slices of length L are represented as vectors, and makes eight structural assumptions including Zip-f eigenvalue decay, linear truncation, and inverse Lipschitz continuity of the reconstruction map. The total loss is decomposed into a Bayesian loss (scaling as d_I(H)^{-(α_Z-1)} from unseen high-index dimensions) and an approximation loss that takes different forms depending on whether the model operates in a data-dense regime (piecewise-linear quantization error plus noise averaging, scaling as N·d_I(H)/D) or a data-scarce regime (nearest-neighbor distance, scaling as d_I(H)·D^{-2/d_I(H)}). Solving for the optimal d_I(H) in each regime yields explicit formulas: in the data-dense large-model case, d_I* ∝ (D/N)^{1/α_Z}, showing the optimal horizon grows with more data and shrinks with larger models; in the data-scarce case, d_I* ∝ ln D / ln ln D, making it nearly invariant to model size. Empirical validation across NLinear, MLP, iTransformer, and ModernTCN on eight datasets confirms the predicted scaling exponents and the existence of a data-dependent optimal horizon.

## Results

The paper empirically validates that the scaling formula loss(D) = A + B/D^α fits data-scaling behavior across 8 datasets (ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2, Exchange, Weather, ECL, Traffic) and 4 model architectures (NLinear, MLP, ModernTCN, iTransformer). AIC/BIC analysis confirms the proposed formula outperforms alternatives: for ModernTCN on Traffic, AIC = −103.9 vs. −95.3 (pure power law), −94.6 (linear+quadratic), and −93.3 (polynomial). The key empirical finding is that an optimal look-back horizon exists at each data amount and grows with more training data, and that different models (e.g., ModernTCN vs. NLinear) exhibit different optimal horizons for the same dataset, with channel-dependent models tending toward the few-data regime and channel-independent models toward the data-dense regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new forecasting model but instead uses four existing architectures as testbeds to validate the scaling theory. NLinear (Zeng et al., 2022) is a Channel-Independent linear model that the theory predicts should remain in the data-dense limit even at long horizons on small datasets like ETTh1, and experiments confirm its loss scales as D^{-1}. ModernTCN (Donghao et al., ICLR 2024) is a Channel-Dependent convolutional model that exhibits overfitting when model width is too large relative to data (e.g., on ETTm1), and its optimal horizon decreases as model width N increases — matching the theory's prediction for the large-model regime. iTransformer (Liu et al., 2024) is a Channel-Dependent Transformer that operates in the data-dense limit on the large Traffic dataset, with its optimal horizon growing with available training data. Gated MLP (Liu et al., 2021) as a Channel-Independent FFN model sits in the few-data limit at small horizons on Traffic, transitioning toward data-dense behavior as horizon increases. The central comparative finding is that no single model universally benefits from longer look-back: the optimal horizon is model-specific, dataset-specific, and data-amount-dependent, undermining the common practice of comparing models at a fixed horizon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the intrinsic-space/data-manifold scaling law framework from NLP (Kaplan et al., Sharma & Kaplan) and CV (Zhai et al.) to time series forecasting, introducing the look-back horizon as a third scaling axis alongside dataset size and model complexity. It departs from the prevailing assumption in recent time series work (e.g., iTransformer, ModernTCN, MICN) that longer look-back horizons monotonically improve performance by showing an optimal horizon exists and is governed by the interplay of data volume, model capacity, and the Zipf-law feature degradation of the intrinsic space. The piecewise-linear approximation model and quantization-error analysis draw on classical results (Zador 1982) but are applied here in a novel way to connect horizon to effective intrinsic dimensionality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication that different models have different optimal look-back horizons for the same dataset challenges the standard benchmarking practice of comparing models at a fixed horizon, and the finding that larger models can have *smaller* optimal horizons is counter-intuitive and actionable for practitioners. Open questions include whether the theory holds for foundation models trained on large mixed datasets, how prediction length S interacts with optimal H, and whether the assumptions (Zipf-law eigenvalue spectrum, linear truncation) hold for high-dimensional or non-stationary series. The framework also provides a principled explanation for why downsampling and patching (PatchTST, FITS) help: they effectively reduce the intrinsic dimension to a more favorable regime.
