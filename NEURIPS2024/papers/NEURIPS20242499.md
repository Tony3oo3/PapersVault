## Problem

Direct sim2real transfer in RL—training a task-solving policy in simulation and deploying it in the real world—provably fails to achieve suboptimality better than O(ε_sim) when the sim-real dynamics mismatch is non-trivial, and naive exploration without a simulator requires exponentially many samples. The paper asks whether a simulator can still yield a provable, exponential gain in real-world RL even in regimes where direct policy transfer is insufficient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242499/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes black-box access to a simulator (trajectory sampling and policy optimization oracles), a real environment for data collection, known simulator feature maps φ^s, and a real interaction budget T with confidence δ. The sim and real MDPs share state/action spaces, rewards, and initial state but differ in transition dynamics bounded by ε_sim in total variation. The output is an ε-optimal policy for the real MDP (with probability ≥ 1−δ) plus a set of exploratory policies learned in simulation. Notably, the real feature maps and all transition measures are unknown, and the algorithm relies only on a simple least-squares regression oracle rather than complex computation oracles.

## Method

The core insight is that it is easier to learn to explore than to solve the task: rather than transferring the optimal task-solving policy from sim, the algorithm transfers a set of exploratory policies that span the simulator's feature space. Algorithm 1 first learns these policies via a LEARNEXPPOLICIES subroutine (ensuring full-rank covariates in sim), then plays them in the real environment coupled with random exploration to collect high-coverage data, and finally applies least-squares regression to estimate a near-optimal real policy. The random exploration component is critical—it ensures the transferred policies reach relevant states in the real MDP within a logarithmic number of steps, converting what would be exponential exploration into polynomial complexity. The main theorem shows that under low-rank MDP structure, Bellman completeness, and ε_sim ≤ λ_min^*/(64dHA³), the sample complexity is polynomial in d, H, and ε^{-1}, an exponential improvement over both naive exploration and direct sim2real transfer. In practice, the exploratory policies are generated via discriminator-based diversity training (inspired by DIAYN) and the real-world learner is SAC, validated on a Franka Emika Panda puck-pushing task where direct transfer fails 0/6 times while exploration policy transfer succeeds 6/6.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that naive exploration (ζ-greedy) and direct sim2real transfer both require exponentially many samples (Ω(2^(H/2)) and Ω(2^H) respectively) in the horizon H, while their exploration policy transfer method achieves polynomial sample complexity of O(d²H¹⁶/ε⁸ · log(H|F|/δ)). In the real-world Franka Emika Panda puck-pushing experiment, exploration policy transfer solves the task 6/6 times over 6 trials, while direct sim2real transfer with finetuning solves it 0/6 times. In the TychoEnv sim2sim reaching task, direct policy transfer fails to collect any reward and never learns, while exploration policy transfer successfully solves the sparse-reward task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two main baselines: (1) direct sim2real transfer, which trains the optimal policy in simulation and deploys it in the real world (optionally with finetuning via SAC), and (2) naive exploration using ζ-greedy with a least-squares regression oracle. On the real-world Franka puck-pushing task, direct sim2real transfer with SAC finetuning fails completely (0/6 success), converging to a suboptimal solution, while exploration policy transfer achieves 100% success (6/6) with substantially higher reward. On the TychoEnv sim2sim reaching task with sparse rewards, direct policy transfer collects zero reward in the real environment and SAC cannot learn, whereas exploration policy transfer makes occasional contact with the target ball, providing sufficient data for SAC to solve the task. Theoretically, both baselines are shown to require exponentially many samples in the horizon H (Propositions 1 and 3), while the proposed method achieves polynomial complexity (Theorem 1), representing an exponential improvement. The paper also notes that the simulation lemma (Proposition 2) guarantees direct transfer only achieves O(ε_sim)-optimal policies, which is insufficient when the target accuracy ε ≪ ε_sim.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the classical simulation lemma (Kearns & Koller, Brafman & Tennenholtz), which bounds the value gap of a directly transferred policy by O(H²ε_sim), by showing that this bound is tight and cannot be improved with naive exploration. It also contrasts with lower-bound results (Malik et al.) showing transfer is infeasible in general, by identifying a specific regime (low-rank MDPs with a reachability condition) where exploration policy transfer provably succeeds. The practical instantiation draws on diversity-based exploration (Eysenbach et al., Kumar et al.) for generating exploratory policies in simulation, and on off-policy RL (SAC) for learning from the collected real data, but the core contribution is the theoretical framework showing that transferring exploration policies—rather than task-solving policies—yields an exponential sample complexity improvement over both direct transfer and learning without a simulator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical result showing that sim2real transfer can yield a provable (exponential) gain in RL beyond the trivial regime where direct transfer already succeeds, which is a significant conceptual advance for the sim2real literature. The practical validation on a real Franka robot (6/6 success vs. 0/6 for direct transfer) is compelling but limited to a single task with few trials, and the theory requires low-rank structure, a reachability assumption, and a specific sim2real mismatch condition (ε_sim ≤ λ_min*/(64dHA³)). Open questions include extending to perceptual shift (not just dynamics shift), leveraging arbitrary resets in the simulator, and determining whether the reachability condition is truly necessary—each of which could broaden the practical applicability of the exploration-transfer principle.
