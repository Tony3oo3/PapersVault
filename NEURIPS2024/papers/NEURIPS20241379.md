## Problem

It remains unclear whether computer vision models genuinely represent 3D object structure or merely exploit 2D visual shortcuts, and existing evaluations lack a direct, multi-granularity comparison between human and model performance on shape inference tasks. This paper addresses this gap by introducing MOCHI, a benchmark drawn from cognitive science experiments (multiview object consistency) that probes zero-shot 3D shape inference under controlled viewpoint variation, enabling formal human-model alignment analysis beyond average accuracy.

## Input / Output

Each trial presents a triplet of RGB images: two views of the same object (A and A') from different viewpoints and one view of a different object (B), drawn from four stimulus sets (barense, majaj, shapenet, shapegen) spanning real objects, abstract 'greebles,' and procedurally generated shapes. Models produce a continuous normalized accuracy score (0 = chance, 1 = ceiling) via a lightweight same-different SVM classifier applied to pairwise feature difference vectors, repeated 100 times per trial to yield a distribution. Human participants additionally provide reaction time and, for a subset of barense stimuli, 1000 Hz gaze data converted into per-image saliency maps.

## Method

The benchmark constructs over 2,000 unique image triplets with parametrically controlled difficulty (object similarity and viewpoint variation) and collects 35K behavioral trials from 500+ participants via Prolific and in-lab eye-tracking. For model evaluation, the authors extract features from DINOv2 (pooled final-layer), MAE ([CLS] token), and CLIP (vision encoder final layer), then train a task-specific SVM on pairwise difference vectors (A−A′ labeled 'same,' A−B labeled 'different') using 75% of triplets within the same condition and evaluate on the held-out triplet, repeating 100 times to obtain a continuous performance estimate with SEM/STD. This same-different classifier outperforms both standard linear probes and zero-shot distance metrics (cosine, L2, etc.) by a significant margin. Human-model alignment is then assessed at three granularities—dataset, condition, and individual trial—by correlating model and human performance and scaling by the split-half reliability ceiling of human behavior at each level, revealing that DINOv2-Giant captures ~60% of explainable variance at the condition level but only ~28% at the trial level.

## Results

On the MOCHI benchmark, humans achieve 78% normalized accuracy, while the best-performing model (DINOv2-Giant) reaches only 44%. DINOv2 models average 39% accuracy (ranging 33–44% across scales), CLIP models average 28% (23–33%), and MAE models perform at or below chance (−4% to −6%). Human–model performance is correlated at the trial level (r = 0.35, p < 10⁻⁶⁰) and condition level (r = 0.58, p = 0.002), with DINOv2-Giant explaining ~60% of the explainable variance at the condition level but only ~28% at the trial level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three model families—DINOv2 (contrastive self-supervised), CLIP (contrastive image-text), and MAE (autoencoding self-supervised)—each at three scales (Base, Large, Giant/Huge). DINOv2 dominates all other model families: it outperforms all CLIP variants (paired t-test t(16150)=14.31, p=3.17×10⁻⁴⁶) and all MAE variants (t(12112)=47.23, p=0). Within DINOv2, a 13× parameter increase from Base to Giant yields only a 12-point accuracy gain (33%→44%), indicating diminishing returns from scale. CLIP also improves with scale (23%→33%) but remains 11 points below DINOv2-Giant. MAE performs at or below chance across all scales (t(6056)=0.11, p=0.91), suggesting its autoencoding objective does not capture the shape-discrimination signals needed for this task. No model approaches human performance (78%), and the gap widens on high-similarity trials where compositional 3D reasoning is required.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classic cognitive science paradigms for 3D shape inference (Shepard & Metzler's mental rotation task, Barense et al.'s odd-one-out discrimination, Majaj et al.'s object recognition under viewpoint variation) into a systematic benchmark for evaluating modern vision models. It extends prior human-vs-model comparisons (Bonnen et al., 2021; O'Connell et al., 2023) by introducing a multi-scale evaluation protocol that moves beyond average accuracy to correlated choice behaviors, reaction time, and gaze dynamics. The evaluation methodology draws on the lightweight same-different linear probe as a task-tailored readout, departing from both zero-shot distance metrics and standard linear probing, and positions itself within the broader benchmarking tradition of Rajalingham et al. (2018) for aligning model and human performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides the field with a principled, multi-granularity evaluation of 3D shape understanding that goes beyond single-metric accuracy, revealing that the human-model gap is driven by adaptive processing time and focal attention rather than a uniform deficit. The finding that model self-attention maps do not predict human gaze patterns above chance is a clear negative result with implications for how we interpret transformer attention as a proxy for visual processing. Open questions include whether architectures with iterative or recurrent processing (rather than single-pass feedforward) can close the gap, whether the evaluation metrics favor contrastive-trained models over autoencoding ones, and how to scale the stimulus set to cover more diverse 3D shape properties (semantics, texture, spatial frequency).
