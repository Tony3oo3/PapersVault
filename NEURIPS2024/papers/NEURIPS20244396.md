## Problem

Extending self-reflection mechanisms from single-agent to multi-agent LLM systems faces two critical challenges: the credit assignment problem (environmental rewards reflect collective performance, making it unclear how much each agent's reflection contributed) and the computational cost of training a separate reflector per agent. Existing approaches like Retroformer approximate reflection rewards with episode return differences, which in multi-agent settings leads to lazy reflectors and does not scale with the number of agents.

## Input / Output

The system takes a task question (multi-hop QA, math word problem, or chess position), agent role profiles (natural-language descriptions of each agent's function and action space), and prior interaction trajectories with scalar environmental rewards (F1 for HotPotQA, exact match for GSM8K/Checkmate) as input to the reflector. It produces two outputs: (1) a task answer from the actor models, and (2) short verbal reflections (<50 words) that are stored in long-term memory and prepended to subsequent actor prompts as additional context. The reflector operates iteratively over up to 5 trials per problem, with reflections accumulating across trials.

## Method

COPPER introduces a shared reflector (LongChat-7B fine-tuned with LoRA) that generates role-specific reflections for all agents in a multi-agent system, using agent profiles in the reflector prompt to condition on role while sharing a single set of parameters. To solve credit assignment, the paper proposes counterfactual rewards: the overall reward is the episode return difference (r_{λ+1} − r_λ), and the marginal reward is computed by re-running the interaction with one agent's reflection removed; the counterfactual reward is their difference, isolating each reflection's individual contribution. The shared reflector is trained via a three-stage counterfactual PPO pipeline: (1) SFT on reflections with positive counterfactual scores, (2) training a regression reward model (GPT-2) with MSE loss on the counterfactual dataset, and (3) PPO optimization of the reflector against the learned reward model with a KL penalty to the SFT policy. Actor models (GPT-3.5) remain frozen, preserving general capabilities while the reflector adapts to the specific collaborative task.

## Results

After 5 reflection trials, COPPER achieves improvements of 31.8%, 18.5%, and 86.4% over the initial success rate on HotPotQA, GSM8K, and Checkmate in One Move, respectively. On HotPotQA, un-fine-tuned Reflexion with LongChat and GPT-3.5 yields only 15.9% and 22.7% improvements, while COPPER's fine-tuned shared reflector substantially outperforms both. When transferred to GPT-4 actor models, COPPER still delivers 27.7%, 9.0%, and 53.3% improvements on the three datasets, matching GPT-4-as-reflector performance after 5 trials. On ALFWorld (appendix), COPPER brings a 37.2% improvement with 4 reflections.

## Baselines & Comparisons

COPPER outperforms all four baselines across all three primary datasets. Against CoT/ReAct (the no-reflection initial success rate), COPPER gains 31.8% on HotPotQA, 18.5% on GSM8K, and 86.4% on Checkmate in One Move. Against Reflexion with un-fine-tuned reflectors (LongChat or GPT-3.5), COPPER roughly doubles the improvement on HotPotQA (31.8% vs. 15.9%/22.7%) and extends the advantage to GSM8K and Checkmate where Reflexion baselines are also weaker. Against Retroformer (which fine-tunes a separate reflector per agent using episode-difference rewards), COPPER converges to higher success rates faster, attributed to the counterfactual reward mechanism and the shared-reflector design that pools training data across agents. The ablation study confirms that removing either the counterfactual reward or the PPO stage degrades performance, validating that both components contribute to the gains over baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-reflection paradigm for LLM agents—established by Reflexion (verbal feedback from environment rewards) and Retroformer (policy-gradient fine-tuning of a reflector)—from single-agent to multi-agent collaboration. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of counterfactual rewards to solve the credit assignment problem that arises when a single episode-level reward must be attributed to multiple agents' reflections, and the design of a shared reflector that generates role-specific reflections via prompt conditioning rather than maintaining separate per-agent models. The multi-agent collaboration settings (debate, teacher-student) follow prior frameworks like Du et al. and AutoGen, while the training pipeline (SFT → reward model → PPO) mirrors standard RLHF but substitutes human preferences with counterfactual task scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The counterfactual reward mechanism provides a principled, model-agnostic way to evaluate individual agent contributions in cooperative LLM systems, addressing a gap that prior single-agent reflection methods cannot fill. The demonstrated generalization of a reflector trained on GPT-3.5 actors to GPT-4 actors suggests that reflection capabilities can be decoupled from the specific actor model, reducing the cost of adapting to new or upgraded LLMs. Open questions include the data overhead of constructing counterfactual rewards (requiring additional interaction trajectories per agent), the scalability of the shared reflector to larger agent populations, and whether more advanced memory structures (e.g., vector embeddings) could replace the current sliding-window long-term memory.
