## Problem

In class-incremental learning, the model must discriminate among all previously seen classes at test time without a task-ID, yet classes from different tasks are never seen together during training. This gives rise to task confusion (TC), which has been empirically identified as the dominant source of performance degradation—distinct from and more severe than catastrophic forgetting (CF)—but lacks any rigorous mathematical characterization. The paper asks whether TC can be formally proven to make optimal class-IL infeasible under discriminative modeling, and whether an alternative modeling paradigm can provably eliminate it.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on standard RGB images (MNIST 28×28, CIFAR-10/100 32×32, CORe50 128×128) with class labels available only during training; at test time no task-ID is provided, forcing the model to infer which task a sample belongs to. The key structural detail is that the N-way classification loss is decomposed into C(N,2) pairwise binary-classifier losses, which are then organized into a T×T block matrix where diagonal blocks encode intra-task discrimination and off-diagonal blocks encode inter-task discrimination. The output is a single class label over all N = T×C classes seen so far.

## Method

The paper introduces a mathematical framework that decomposes an N-way discriminative classifier's loss into C(N,2) virtual binary classifiers (Lemma 1) and arranges them into a task-level loss matrix P(θ) with intra-task (diagonal) and inter-task (off-diagonal) blocks. The Infeasibility Theorem (Theorem 1) then proves that even a CF-optimal discriminative model—minimizing only diagonal blocks—cannot achieve optimal class-IL because the total loss and diagonal loss are incompatible, leaving inter-task blocks unminimized (i.e., TC persists). Conversely, the Feasibility Theorem (Theorem 2) shows that under generative modeling (joint probability P(X,Y)), the loss matrix Q(θ) has no off-diagonal blocks, so preventing CF alone guarantees optimality. The framework yields corollaries bounding the performance of regularization (upper-bounded by 100/T%), bias-correction, generative replay (optimal only if the surrogate density matches the true density), and generative classifiers (optimal with CF prevention via expansion-based architectures). Empirical validation on four benchmarks confirms that generative classifiers (SLDA, GenC) substantially outperform all discriminative strategies in the class-IL setting.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it proves the Infeasibility Theorem (optimal class-IL is impossible under discriminative modeling even with CF prevention) and the Feasibility Theorem (optimal class-IL is achievable under generative modeling with CF prevention). Empirically, generative classifiers dominate all discriminative strategies: GenC achieves 56.02% on CIFAR-10, 49.53% on CIFAR-100, and 70.80% on CORe50, while the best discriminative method (AR1) reaches only 25.13%, 21.01%, and 44.13% respectively. Regularization methods (EWC, SI) barely exceed the None baseline (e.g., EWC: 18.77% vs. None: 18.74% on CIFAR-10), confirming the theoretical prediction that they cannot overcome task confusion. The Joint upper bound (all data available simultaneously) reaches 82.07% on CIFAR-10 and 54.08% on CIFAR-100, showing GenC closes most of the gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 10 baselines across four class-IL strategies and shows that generative classifiers (GenC, SLDA) decisively outperform all discriminative approaches. Regularization methods (EWC [1], SI [2]) and distillation (Dis [19]) are essentially impotent, scoring within 1% of the None baseline on CIFAR-10 (18.77%, 18.00%, 18.31% vs. 18.74%), confirming the Infeasibility Theorem's prediction that preventing CF alone cannot overcome TC. Bias-correction methods (CWR [20], CWR+ [21], AR1 [21], Label [11]) provide modest gains (up to 25.13% for AR1 on CIFAR-10) by slightly reducing inter-task bias but remain far below generative classifiers. Generative replay (DGR [6], BI-R/BI-R+SI [18]) shows strong results on MNIST (91.12% for DGR) but collapses on CIFAR-10 (18.13% for DGR) due to poor surrogate density quality. GenC [4] achieves the best overall performance (56.02% CIFAR-10, 49.53% CIFAR-100, 70.80% CORe50), approaching the Joint upper bound (82.07%, 54.08%, 71.85%), while SLDA [10] is competitive on CORe50 (70.80%) but weaker on CIFAR-10 (38.33%). The paper's key empirical claim is validated: generative modeling is essential for optimal class-IL, as no discriminative strategy comes close to matching GenC's performance on natural image benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by introducing a mathematical framework that formally distinguishes task confusion (TC) from catastrophic forgetting (CF) in class-incremental learning, a distinction that prior work has largely conflated. It departs from the dominant CF-centric framing inherited from task-IL (Kirkpatrick et al., Zenke et al.) and from Kim et al.'s [17] theoretical study, which neither defines TC nor addresses the discriminative-vs-generative modeling distinction. It also contrasts with Soutif-Cormerais et al. [16], who provided only empirical (black-box) evidence for cross-task feature importance, whereas this paper offers white-box proofs (Infeasibility and Feasibility Theorems) grounded in a loss-matrix decomposition. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper repurposes existing class-IL strategies (EWC, SI, distillation, bias-correction, generative replay, SLDA, GenC) as test cases for its theoretical framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Infeasibility Theorem — that discriminative modeling cannot achieve optimal class-IL even with perfect CF prevention — is a strong conceptual reframing that challenges the field's default assumption that regularization-based CF mitigation is sufficient. The 100/T% upper-bound hypothesis for CF-optimal models provides a clean, testable diagnostic for practitioners. However, the practical solutions (generative classifiers like GenC and SLDA) already exist in the literature, and they rely on expansion-based architectures that grow unboundedly, limiting real-world deployment. Open questions include: how to reconcile the small empirical discrepancies where some regularization schemes slightly exceed the 100/T% bound; how to achieve generative-modeling benefits without expansion-based architectures; and whether the incompatibility assumption (Definition 4) holds broadly enough to make the theorems universally applicable.
