## Problem

In domain generalization, the target-domain risk of a classifier is often non-transportable: the combination of source data and structural assumptions (encoded in causal diagrams) is insufficient to uniquely determine the worst-case performance in an unseen domain. No general estimation technique existed for computing tight upper-bounds on such non-transportable queries, leaving practitioners without a principled way to assess or optimize worst-case generalization risk.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244375/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes (i) finite samples from K source domains, (ii) a selection diagram G^Δ — a causal diagram augmented with selection nodes S_i pointing to variables whose mechanisms may differ between domain i and the target — and (iii) a classifier h mapping covariates to outcomes. Neural-TR (Alg. 1) outputs a scalar tight upper-bound on E_{P*}[ψ(V)] for any functional ψ (e.g., 0-1 loss or MSE). CRO (Alg. 2) additionally outputs a classifier h_CRO that provably minimizes worst-case risk over all SCMs compatible with the data and diagram, in the large-sample limit.

## Method

The paper extends two SCM parameterizations to the multi-domain transportability setting. First, canonical models (Balke–Pearl latent-variable parameterization) yield a linear program with equality constraints enforcing source-distribution matching and mechanism invariance (P^{N^i}(r_V) = P^{N^j}(r_V) for V ∉ Δ_{ij}), giving a tight bound (Thm. 1). Second, Neural Causal Models are adapted via parameter sharing: for every variable V not in Δ_{ij}, the network parameters θ_V^i are tied to θ_V^j, and source-data likelihood terms anchor the remaining free parameters (Thm. 2). The key practical insight is the s-admissibility decomposition: the target distribution factors into c-components, some of which are directly transportable from source data (pre-trained as constants) and only the non-transportable ancestral c-factors require NCM optimization, drastically reducing the parameter space (Alg. 1). CRO (Alg. 2) then runs an iterative min-max game: Neural-TR acts as an adversary generating the worst-case target NCM for the current classifier, and group DRO updates the classifier to minimize maximum empirical risk over all accumulated adversarial distributions until the gap between the Neural-TR upper-bound and the DRO risk falls below a threshold δ.

## Results

On the Colored MNIST (CMNIST) benchmark, CRO achieves a worst-case risk of approximately 0.25, matching the theoretically optimal classifier that relies solely on the grayscale digit. In contrast, ERM, group DRO, and IRM achieve worst-case risks of ~0.95, ~0.90, and ~0.65 respectively, all performing worse than random classification (0.5). On the synthetic Example 2, CRO recovers the optimal classifier h(z):=z with worst-case risk 0.05, while on Example 3 it recovers h(x)=¬x with worst-case risk 0.18, both confirmed as minimax-optimal by Theorem 3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CRO against three standard domain generalization baselines on the Colored MNIST task: ERM (Vapnik, 1991), Invariant Risk Minimization (Arjovsky et al., 2019), and group Distributionally Robust Optimization (Sagawa et al., 2019). CRO decisively outperforms all three, achieving a worst-case risk of ~0.25 versus ~0.95 for ERM, ~0.90 for group DRO, and ~0.65 for IRM. Notably, all three baselines perform worse than random classification (0.5) in the worst case, while CRO matches the theoretically optimal grayscale-only classifier. The paper notes this comparison is not entirely apples-to-apples, since CRO has access to the selection diagram (structural assumptions about which mechanisms are invariant) that the baselines do not exploit, positioning CRO as a meta-algorithm that leverages richer domain-relatedness information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the partial identification framework (Balke & Pearl 1997; Zhang et al. 2021) and the causal transportability framework (Pearl & Bareinboim 2011; Lee et al. 2020) to a new multi-domain setting where the target query is not point-identifiable. It adapts Neural Causal Models (Xia et al. 2021) with a parameter-sharing scheme to encode structural invariances across domains, and leverages the s-admissibility criterion to decompose the optimization into transportable and non-transportable components. The CRO algorithm repurposes group DRO (Sagawa et al. 2019) as a subroutine within a min-max game against an adversarial domain generator, positioning itself as a meta-algorithm that operates on richer structural assumptions than invariant or robust learning methods like IRM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first general technique for bounding (rather than identifying) cross-domain statistics under structural assumptions, filling a gap where prior transportability algorithms could only provide point estimates. The CMNIST results showing that ERM, IRM, and group DRO all perform worse than random in the worst case are a cautionary finding for the domain generalization community, though the comparison is somewhat asymmetric since CRO has access to the selection diagram. Key limitations include the requirement for expert-specified selection diagrams, the scalability of NCM parameterization to high-dimensional continuous variables, and the fact that the empirical validation is limited to one semi-synthetic image dataset and small synthetic examples. Open questions include whether selection diagrams can be learned from data, whether the bounds remain informative at scale, and how to handle continuous variables more naturally.
