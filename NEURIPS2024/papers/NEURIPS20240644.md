## Problem

It remains unclear whether public data can fundamentally improve the rates of differentially private stochastic optimization and supervised learning, or whether the privacy cost is unavoidable regardless of public data availability. Prior work established limited lower bounds in restricted regimes (e.g., small n_pub or pure DP), leaving open the full characterization of when and how public data helps. This paper provides tight lower bounds showing labeled public data offers no rate improvement, while simultaneously demonstrating that unlabeled public data enables dimension-independent learning rates that are otherwise unattainable.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a private labeled dataset S_priv of size n_priv, a public unlabeled feature set X_pub of size n_pub (feature vectors only, no labels), and privacy parameters (ε, δ). For the GLM setting, the public data is used to compute an orthogonal projection U onto span(X_pub) ∩ W, after which the private subroutine operates on projected vectors U^T x_i in a space of dimension at most n_pub. The output is a model parameter ŵ = Uw̃ in the original d-dimensional space, accompanied by an excess risk bound that is dimension-independent when n_pub = Õ(n_priv ε).

## Method

The paper's core technique for unlabeled public data is a dimensionality reduction procedure: private feature vectors are projected onto the subspace spanned by public data (Algorithm 1), and a standard private optimization subroutine (projected noisy SGD or regularized exponential mechanism) is run in this lower-dimensional space, with the solution embedded back into R^d. For general hypothesis classes with finite fat-shattering dimension, Algorithm 2 constructs an α-cover of the hypothesis class with respect to the empirical ℓ₂ distance on public data points, then applies the exponential mechanism to privately select a hypothesis from this finite cover using private empirical loss as the score. The key insight is that with Õ(n_priv ε) public samples, the empirical cover on public data becomes a valid population cover, enabling the use of selection-based private learning with rates depending on fat-shattering dimension rather than ambient dimension d. The lower bounds for labeled public data are established via a novel analysis of fingerprinting codes that handles the correlation structure introduced by the public/private split, showing the simple strategy of discarding one dataset is optimal up to constants.

## Results

For labeled public data, the paper establishes a tight lower bound of Ω(min{1/√n_pub, 1/√n + √d/(nε)}) on excess risk for (ε,δ)-PA-DP SCO, matching the simple upper bound of either discarding private data or treating all data as private. For unlabeled public data with GLMs, the paper achieves a dimension-independent rate of Õ(1/√n_priv + 1/(√n_priv·ε)) using only Õ(n_priv·ε) public samples, which is shown to be optimal in both the rate and the public sample complexity. For general fat-shattering hypothesis classes, the rate is O(ℜ_{n_priv}(H) + inf_{α>0}(fat_α(H)/(n_priv·ε) + α)), and for depth-M feed-forward neural networks with 1-Lipschitz activations, the excess risk is Õ(√M·∏R_j/√n_priv + (M(∏R_j)²/(n_priv·ε))^{1/3}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against two trivial strategies for labeled public data: (1) discarding private data and solving non-private SCO on public data, yielding O(1/√n_pub), and (2) treating all data as private, yielding O(1/√n + √d/(nε)). The tight lower bound shows neither strategy can be improved upon by any more sophisticated PA-DP algorithm, up to constant factors. For unlabeled public data, the key comparison is against the no-public-data constrained GLM rate of O(1/√n_priv + √d/(n_priv·ε)) [BST14, BFTGT19]. The paper's dimensionality-reduction algorithm (Algorithm 1) eliminates the √d dependence entirely, achieving Õ(1/√n_priv + 1/(√n_priv·ε)), which is strictly better when d ≥ n_priv·ε. The lower bounds (Theorems 4 and 5) confirm that this rate cannot be improved with more public samples, and that fewer than Õ(n_priv·ε) public samples necessarily yield a worse rate. For non-Euclidean GLMs (e.g., ℓ_1-constrained), the paper achieves a rate of Õ(√(log d/n_priv) + (log d/(n_priv·ε))^{1/3}), improving upon the prior best of Õ(√(log d/n) + √(log d/(nε))) [BGM21] which required no public data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PA-DP framework established by [BNS13, ABM19] and the most directly related work of [LLHR23], which studied PA-DP mean estimation and SCO but either focused on the pure DP regime with small n_pub or failed to capture the dimension dependence in the approximate DP case. The lower bound technique builds on fingerprinting codes [BUV14] and the robust distribution framework [DSS+15], while the SCO reduction follows [BST14, CWZ21]. For the unlabeled public data setting, the algorithmic approach of constructing a finite cover via public data and applying the exponential mechanism is inspired by [ABM19], but the paper's key departure is providing tight upper and lower bounds simultaneously and extending the analysis from VC classes to fat-shattering classes, neural networks, and non-Euclidean geometries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean delineation of when public data helps in private optimization: labeled public data offers no fundamental rate improvement (the trivial strategy is optimal), while unlabeled public data enables dimension-independent rates for GLMs and more general hypothesis classes. This is practically important because it redirects effort toward collecting unlabeled public data rather than labeled data, and the fat-shattering extension shows that proper private learning is possible for classes (like norm-bounded linear predictors in high dimensions) that are impossible to learn privately without public data. Open questions include the intermediate regime d ∈ (nε/n_pub, nε) for pure PA-DP, and whether the optimistic rates for smooth losses can be tightened to match the unconstrained setting without the additional assumption that the global minimizer lies in the constraint set.
