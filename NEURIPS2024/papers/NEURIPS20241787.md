## Problem

Existing pre-trained time-series foundational models rely on fixed-length segmentation to tokenize inputs, which is inflexible when pre-training across heterogeneous domains with vastly different sampling rates, periodicity, and temporal dynamics (e.g., millisecond heart-sensor data vs. quarterly GDP series). This paper addresses the challenge of extracting semantically meaningful, domain-adaptive token representations from diverse time-series so that a single pre-trained model can generalize across domains in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings.

## Input / Output

The model consumes univariate time series that are first normalized via a reversible instance normalization (REVIN) layer, then segmented into variable-length segments whose boundaries are learned per-domain during pre-training. Each segment becomes a token embedding (produced by self-attention over GRU hidden states within the segment, augmented with sinusoidal positional encodings of the segment's start index and length), and the resulting variable-length token sequence is fed to a 10-layer transformer encoder. For forecasting, a 4-layer GRU decoder reconstructs masked or future segment values; for classification, a single linear layer over pooled output embeddings produces class logits.

## Method

The core contribution is an adaptive segmentation module that learns domain-specific segmentation strategies during self-supervised pre-training. A GRU encodes the input time series into hidden states, and a bilinear scoring function s(i,j) = v^T tanh(W₁z_i + W₂z_j + b) assigns a scalar score to every possible subsequence; the highest-scoring segments that collectively cover the full series are selected and pruned to reduce redundancy. Because segment selection is discrete, the score function is trained indirectly by matching the sum of chosen segment scores to the negative log of the total SSL loss (Eq. 5), with backpropagation through this loss every 10 batches for stability. Pre-training uses two masking tasks—RANDMASK (randomly mask γ=0.4 of segments) and LASTMASK (mask the last γ=0.2 fraction)—both decoded with a single-layer GRU and trained with MSE loss. The overall model (~100M parameters, 10 transformer layers, 8 heads) is 2–10× smaller than competing pre-trained time-series models, and downstream fine-tuning follows a two-stage protocol of linear probing followed by full-parameter fine-tuning to avoid out-of-distribution distortion.

## Results

In fine-tuned forecasting (Table 2), LPTM achieves the best or second-best RMSE across all 9 benchmarks, with a mean rank of 2.55 out of 22+ models. Headline numbers include RMSE of 0.79 on Flu-US (vs. 0.52 for domain-specific EpiFNP), 704 on Flu-Japan (vs. 694 for ColaGNN), 0.46 on ETT2, 2.37 on NY-Bike, and 0.17 on Nasdaq. In zero-shot forecasting (Table 1), LPTM outperforms all five pre-trained baselines (LLM-Time, TimesFM, Lag-LLAMA, Chronos, MOIRAI) on every benchmark, e.g., RMSE 1.14 vs. 1.29 (Chronos) on Flu-US and 996 vs. 1233 (TS2Vec) on Flu-Japan. For time-series classification across 35 UCI/UEA datasets, LPTM achieves the highest mean rank and the most instances of outperforming all baselines, with accuracy up to 1.00 on BasicMotions and SpokenArabicDigits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LPTM (100M params) outperforms all five recent pre-trained time-series foundation models — LLM-Time, TimesFM, Lag-LLAMA, Chronos, and MOIRAI — in fine-tuned forecasting across all 9 benchmarks, despite being 2–10× smaller in parameter count. Against domain-specific SOTA, LPTM beats EpiFNP on Flu-US (0.79 vs. 0.52 is EpiFNP's win, but LPTM wins on 5 other benchmarks) and STEP on ETT2 (0.46 vs. 0.93) and NY-Bike (2.37 vs. 2.52), while ColaGNN retains a narrow edge on Flu-Japan (694 vs. 704). Among general transformer-based forecasters, LPTM surpasses PatchTST (rank 7.5), MICN (rank 7.77), and Informer (rank 15.89) in overall ranking, and matches or beats Autoformer and TimesNet on most benchmarks. In zero-shot evaluation, LPTM dominates all pre-trained baselines, e.g., RMSE 1.14 vs. 1.29 (Chronos) on Flu-US and 0.49 vs. 0.52 (MOIRAI) on ETT2. For classification on 35 UCI/UEA datasets, LPTM achieves the best mean rank, outperforming TARNet, TS2Vec, TS-TCC, SimMTM, and CRT on the majority of datasets, with particularly large gains on ERing (0.97 vs. 0.92 TARNet) and LSST (0.98 vs. 0.97 TARNet). The SSL-based baselines (TS2Vec, TS-TCC, SimMTM) consistently underperform LPTM by a wide margin in both forecasting and classification, highlighting the importance of cross-domain adaptive segmentation over single-domain contrastive pre-training.

## Relation to Prior Work

LPTM extends the fixed-length segmentation tokenization introduced by PatchTST (Nie et al., 2022) into an adaptive, domain-specific segmentation module that learns optimal segment boundaries during self-supervised pre-training. It sits within the growing family of time-series foundation models (TimesFM, Chronos, MOIRAI, Lag-LLAMA, LLM-Time) but departs from them by addressing the cross-domain tokenization problem: rather than applying a single uniform segmentation or digit-based encoding across all domains, LPTM uses a GRU-scored segmentation function trained via SSL loss to identify dataset-specific segment strategies. The overall architecture (transformer encoder + masking-based SSL + REVIN + two-stage linear-probing/fine-tuning) follows well-established patterns from NLP pre-training and prior time-series SSL work, making the adaptive segmentation module the primary conceptual novelty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The adaptive segmentation mechanism addresses a practical bottleneck in cross-domain time-series pre-training that prior foundation models have handled with inflexible fixed-length patches or LLM-style digit tokenization. The consistent outperformance across 9 diverse benchmarks (epidemiology, energy, traffic, finance, demand) in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings, with 40% less data and 50% less training time, makes this a practically relevant contribution for deploying forecasting models in data-scarce real-world settings. Key limitations include univariate-only input, no probabilistic or calibrated uncertainty output, and no multivariate support, which restricts applicability to many real forecasting scenarios. Open questions include whether the adaptive segmentation generalizes to multivariate and multimodal (text + time series) settings, and whether the approach scales to the much larger pre-training corpora used by competing foundation models.
