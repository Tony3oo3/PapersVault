## Problem

Post-hoc transforms like temperature scaling, SWA, and ensembling are routinely applied only after models are finalized via standard base-metric selection (naive selection), operating under an implicit monotonicity assumption that better base models yield better post-transform models. This paper demonstrates that this assumption is frequently violated, especially on noisy data, where post-hoc transforms can reverse performance trends between models trained for different epochs, at different sizes, or with different hyperparameters.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of model checkpoints saved at epoch boundaries during a training run (or multiple parallel runs for ensembling) along with a held-out validation set. It outputs a selected final epoch (or set of epochs) that minimizes the post-transform validation metric—e.g., the cross-entropy loss of the SWA-averaged, temperature-scaled model—rather than the base model's validation loss or error. For SWA+Ens+TS, the constraint that all ensemble members share the same final epoch keeps the search tractable while still capturing the joint optimization benefit.

## Method

The paper introduces post-hoc selection, a technique where model development decisions (early stopping, checkpointing, hyperparameter choice) are informed by post-transform performance rather than base performance. The core empirical finding is post-hoc reversal: SWA and ensembling suppress the influence of mislabeled examples by exploiting their unstable learning dynamics (predictions on noisy examples flip more frequently between epochs), while continuing to reinforce generalizable patterns from clean examples; TS separately mitigates loss-error mismatch by downscaling overconfident logits. Practically, one monitors the SWA+TS (or SWA+Ens+TS) validation loss during training and selects the epoch that minimizes it, which often corresponds to a much later epoch than naive selection would choose. On noisy datasets this yields >2× improvement over naive selection, and on LLM instruction tuning it produces >1.5× MMLU gains from the same composed transform.

## Results

Post-hoc selection (selecting checkpoints based on post-transform validation performance) consistently outperforms naive selection (selecting based on base model performance) across all noisy datasets. On FMoW (OOD), post-hoc selection with SWA+Ens+TS reduces test error from 46.74% (naive) to 41.56%, a 5.2-point gain over naive selection. On CIFAR-10-N Worst (~40% noise), test error drops from 44.30% (naive) to 42.88% (post-hoc), and the improvement over no transform more than doubles (from 2.54 to 6.79 points). On LLM instruction tuning (Guanaco/LLaMA-2-7B), post-hoc selection yields >1.5× MMLU improvement over naive selection (47.54% vs 47.23% with SWA+Ens+TS, vs 46.64% baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between naive selection (picking the best base-model checkpoint, then applying post-hoc transforms) and the proposed post-hoc selection (picking the checkpoint that performs best after the transforms are applied). Post-hoc selection wins on virtually all noisy datasets: on FMoW (ID) it improves test error by 3.02 points over naive (34.93% vs 37.95%), and on FMoW (OOD) by 5.19 points (41.56% vs 46.74%). On CIFAR-10-N Worst, the gain over naive is 1.42 points in error, but the total improvement over no transform more than doubles (6.79 vs 2.54 points). On the LLM instruction tuning task, post-hoc selection with SWA+Ens+TS achieves 47.54% MMLU vs 47.23% for naive, a >1.5× larger improvement over the 46.64% untransformed baseline. On clean or low-noise datasets (e.g., C-10-N Clean, C-100-N Clean), the gap between naive and post-hoc selection is negligible (<0.1 points), confirming that the phenomenon is driven by noise. The paper also shows that post-hoc selection remains effective under out-of-distribution evaluation splits, unlike some methods that rely on clean validation sets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes three well-established post-hoc transforms—temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017), stochastic weight averaging (Izmailov et al., 2018), and deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2016)—by moving them from a fixed post-training step into the model selection loop itself. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of the noisy-data learning literature (early-learning regularization, double descent) and the calibration/ensemble literature, showing that the learning dynamics that make post-hoc transforms effective (suppressing mislabeled examples) also invalidate the implicit monotonicity assumption underlying naive model selection. The paper departs from prior work on double descent mitigation (regularization, data augmentation) by demonstrating that a purely post-hoc, training-free intervention can flatten the double descent peak and resolve loss-error mismatch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is immediate and broad: practitioners can improve model selection simply by monitoring post-transform metrics during training rather than base metrics, with no changes to the training procedure. The >2× improvements over naive selection on noisy datasets and the >1.5× MMLU gain in LLM instruction tuning are substantial for real-world pipelines where label noise and multi-epoch fine-tuning are common. Open questions include a theoretical characterization of when reversal occurs, whether the effect persists at larger scales (pre-training LLMs, ImageNet-scale vision), and how to extend the selection criterion to more complex post-hoc transforms (e.g., long-horizon temperature scaling, routing-based ensembles). The paper also leaves open whether the optimal checkpoint interval and SWA window should be jointly optimized with the selection criterion.
