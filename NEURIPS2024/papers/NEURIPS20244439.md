## Problem

Existing methods for detecting social bias in text either rely on simplistic keyword frequency counts that ignore contextual meaning, or require expensive human-labeled training data and model fine-tuning. In the labor market specifically, while gendered language in job advertisements (the demand side) has been well studied, the language in AI-generated job applications (the supply side) has been largely unexplored, despite the growing use of tools like ChatGPT by individual job seekers. This paper addresses both gaps by proposing a context-sensitive, training-free bias evaluation method and applying it to probe how ChatGPT reproduces and amplifies gender biases from job postings into generated applications.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input a text T (a job posting or ChatGPT-generated application), a feminine word list F, and a masculine word list M, all drawn from validated social science inventories (e.g., 73 feminine vs. 120 masculine words for psychological cues). Function words (articles, prepositions, conjunctions) are skipped during processing. The output is a scalar bias score B(T) where positive values indicate masculine orientation and negative values indicate feminine orientation, along with per-word scores S(w_i) that localize bias within the text. The score is computed via a single forward pass through a pre-trained MLM (BERT-base-uncased), requiring no training or fine-tuning.

## Method

PRISM (Probability Ranking bIas Score via Masked Language Model) iteratively masks each word w_i in the text, feeds the masked sequence to an MLM, and reads the predicted probability distribution over the vocabulary at the masked position. For each word in the feminine and masculine lists, it extracts the corresponding probability, merges both sets, filters to the top α% most probable predictions, and computes a rank-based score: the mean rank of feminine-list words minus the mean rank of masculine-list words. This rank-based (ordinal) design, rather than raw probability comparison, provides robustness across different MLMs and mitigates the dominance of low-probability noise in large vocabularies. The theoretical foundation is the Wilcoxon rank sum test: under the null hypothesis of no bias, the rank sums for the two word lists follow a known normal distribution (Theorem 1), enabling formal hypothesis testing at the word level. The overall text score B(T) is the mean of all per-word scores, and the method is validated against human expert labels (Spearman ρ = 0.85) and the BIOS benchmark (AUC = 0.97), outperforming unigram and word-embedding baselines.

## Results

PRISM achieves an AUC of 0.97 on the BIOS dataset for gender classification, surpassing three baseline methods from Dhamala et al. (2021). Validation against six human experts yields a Spearman rank correlation of 0.85 between algorithm scores and expert labels. On the main empirical analysis of 33K+ LinkedIn job postings and ChatGPT-generated applications, strong positive correlations are found between posting and application bias scores across all four dimensions: Social Characteristics (r=0.777), Role Description (r=0.708), Psychological Cues (r=0.644), and Work-Family Characteristics (r=0.451). Statistical tests confirm that ChatGPT shifts bias toward masculine language in 3 of 4 dimensions, increases bias magnitude in WFC and Social Characteristics, and consistently reduces variance across all dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline comparison is against three gender-bias metrics from Dhamala et al. (2021) on the BIOS dataset: unigram matching (counting gender-specific tokens), Gender-Wavg (weighted average of word-vector projections onto the gender direction), and Gender-Max (score from the most gender-polar word). PRISM achieves an AUC of 0.97, outperforming all three baselines, which rely on unigram counts or static word embeddings without contextual sensitivity. The paper also validates against six human expert coders, achieving a Spearman correlation of 0.85, confirming that the rank-based MLM approach captures bias in a manner consistent with expert judgment. An ablation study using DistilBERT and BERT-large as alternative MLMs shows consistent scatter-density patterns and statistical test results, confirming robustness to the choice of masked language model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test as its theoretical backbone, repurposing it to compare MLM-predicted probability distributions over two social-science word lists (feminine vs. masculine) rather than comparing two independent samples. It departs from prior text-bias evaluation methods (cosine-similarity in word embeddings, BOLD unigram/projection metrics, lexicon-based approaches) by combining contextual MLM understanding with domain-validated word inventories and a rank-based scoring that avoids the fragility of raw probability comparisons. The experimental design of probing a black-box generative LLM through simulated job-posting-to-application pipelines is a novel application of bias-detection methodology to the labor-market supply side, complementing prior demand-side analyses of job advertisements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ChatGPT-generated job applications not only replicate but amplify gender biases (increased magnitude in WFC and Social Characteristics dimensions) and standardize language toward masculine expressions (consistent variance reduction across all four dimensions) has direct implications for AI-assisted hiring equity and regulatory oversight. Key limitations include English-only evaluation, basic prompting, reliance on a single MLM (bert-base-uncased) for scoring, and tokenization mismatches with word lists. Open questions include whether inherently biased MLMs are necessary for effective bias detection, how results generalize to multilingual settings, and whether varied or adversarial prompts would reveal different bias patterns.
