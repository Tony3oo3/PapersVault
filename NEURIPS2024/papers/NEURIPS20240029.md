## Problem

Deep neural networks can fit training data perfectly by memorizing spurious, noise-derived correlations rather than capturing the underlying generalizable structure. Existing regularization techniques (L1/L2, dropout, weight decay) impose generic simplicity constraints but do not directly measure whether the learned correlations are the *right kind* of simple — i.e., the kind that is genuinely transmissible and imitable by other learners. The paper asks: can the ease with which a separate learner can imitate a model's predictions serve as a principled regularizer that steers training toward generalizable correlations?

## Input / Output

LOT is domain-agnostic and wraps around any standard training loop. For vision, inputs are RGB images (224×224 for CIFAR-100, 384×384 for ImageNet) producing class labels; for language, text sequences produce next-token distributions; for RL, game observations produce actions. The key additional requirement is an unlabelled dataset D_s (which may equal the training set or be generated/augmented) on which the student models learn to imitate the teacher. The teacher and student may use different architectures and parameter counts (e.g., a 12M ResNet-18 teacher with a 4M MobileNetV2 student), and the student models are initialized independently to encourage diverse representations.

## Method

LOT trains a teacher network T alongside K student networks S_i in an iterative loop: the teacher is updated once on its task loss plus a LOT regularizer R(θ), then each student is updated N times to minimize KL-divergence between its own predictions and the teacher's on unlabelled data D_s. The LOT regularizer R(θ) = (α/|D_s|) Σ λ_i μ_{t,s_i}(x) is precisely the weighted KL-divergence between teacher and student output distributions, so it directly measures how imitable the teacher's learned correlations are. The core hypothesis is that generalizable (low-Kolmogorov-complexity) correlations are inherently easier to imitate than spurious ones, so minimizing R(θ) pushes the teacher toward simpler, more transmissible representations. The regularization coefficient α controls the trade-off (optimal around 1.0 for most tasks), and the student steps ratio N balances feedback quality against overfitting to teacher errors (optimal around 4–5). Unlike knowledge distillation, both teacher and student are trained jointly from scratch (or from pretrained weights), and the feedback flows from student to teacher rather than the reverse.

## Results

LOT consistently improves generalization across all tested domains. On image classification, LOT achieves +1.99% accuracy on CIFAR-100 (e.g., 86.04% vs 84.09% for ResNet-50) and +0.83% on ImageNet-1K (e.g., 85.92% vs 85.15% for ViT-L/16). On language modeling, LOT reduces perplexity by up to 11.03 points (LSTM on PTB: 71.72 vs 82.75) and at least 2 points across all architectures. On Atari games, LOT yields normalized return enhancements of 63.14% (BeamRider), 66.48% (UpNDown), 35.70% (Breakout), and 9.79% (Gravitar) over Teacher-only.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LOT is compared against four external baselines plus the Teacher-only ablation (α=0). Against Teacher-only, LOT wins on every single experiment across all domains and architectures, with gains ranging from ~0.3% (Swin-L on ImageNet-1K) to ~11 PPL points (LSTM on PTB). Against Born-Again Networks (BAN) [29], LOT achieves higher teacher accuracy on CIFAR-100 (e.g., 86.04% vs 84.73% for ResNet-50; 93.17% vs 92.44% for ViT-B/16), confirming that the interactive joint-training process in LOT is more effective than one-shot distillation. Against In-Context Learning (ICL) [9] on LLM fine-tuning, LOT substantially outperforms (e.g., 36.42% vs 10.69% for LLaMA-17B on GSM8K), and also beats standard SFT (36.42% vs 34.39% on GSM8K; 5.39% vs 4.78% on MATH). Against ReviewKD [15] and DKD [108] on CIFAR-100, LOT again leads (86.04% vs 85.31% and 85.17% for ResNet-50; 93.17% vs 92.73% and 92.82% for ViT-B/16), further validating that the iterative teacher-student feedback loop in LOT provides a stronger regularizer than existing distillation variants.

## Relation to Prior Work

LOT repurposes the teacher-student paradigm from knowledge distillation (where the goal is to improve the student) into a regularization mechanism for the teacher, drawing on the 'ease-of-teaching' principle from emergent language research (Li & Bowling 2019; Chaabouni et al. 2020) that structured, compositional signals are more efficiently transmitted. Unlike KD or born-again networks, where a frozen teacher transfers knowledge one-directionally, LOT trains teacher and student jointly so that the student's imitability loss feeds back as a regularizer on the teacher. The paper also connects to the broader regularization literature (L1/L2, dropout, weight decay) by framing imitability as a new type of penalty that encourages lower-Kolmogorov-complexity correlations, and to the 'generalization equals optimal compression' intuition articulated by Sutskever.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LOT offers a conceptually grounded, architecture-agnostic regularizer that can be dropped into existing training pipelines across supervised, unsupervised, and RL settings with modest additional compute, and its consistent gains (e.g., 11 PPL on PTB, ~2% on CIFAR-100, 44% normalized reward on Atari) suggest it could become a standard component of generalization-focused training. Key open questions include whether the approach scales to frontier-scale LLMs beyond 27B parameters, whether a theoretical account of why imitability proxies generalizability can be formalized, and how to automatically select the optimal student architecture and regularization coefficient α rather than relying on grid search. The additional memory and compute for student models remain a practical constraint, though the paper shows LOT often converges faster than Teacher-only, partially offsetting this cost.
