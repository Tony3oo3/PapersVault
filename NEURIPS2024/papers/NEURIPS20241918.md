## Problem

Most existing vision-language benchmarks evaluate models on single image-text pairs, leaving a significant gap in assessing the ability to reason across multiple, potentially disparate images with step-by-step solutions. As frontier LLMs increasingly support interleaved multi-image inputs, there is no dedicated benchmark that systematically covers the diverse domains and structural properties (sequential vs. set consumption, interleaving, concept heterogeneity) inherent to multi-image reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241918/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each problem provides 2–6 heterogeneous images (charts, tables, equations, emojis, geometry shapes, maps, clocks, code renderings, graph diagrams) alongside a text question, with images either prepended to the prompt or interleaved at the point of reference. Models are prompted to output a JSON object with an 'explanation' field (step-by-step reasoning) and an 'answer' field (final response), capped at 512 output tokens. Scoring uses exact match for text, relaxed accuracy with 1% tolerance for numeric answers (3% for geometry tasks, 10-minute tolerance for clock reading), and a naive baseline that guesses from text alone without viewing images.

## Method

ReMI comprises 13 programmatically generated tasks spanning algebra, calculus, geometry, physics, logic, coding, graph theory, spatial/maps reasoning, chart understanding, and time arithmetic, each constructed to cover specific multi-image properties: sequential vs. set image consumption, same vs. different conceptual content across images, interleaved vs. non-interleaved presentation, and variable image count. Task generation leverages libraries such as Matplotlib (function graphs, charts), NetworkX (random graph generation for isomorphism), TikZ (clocks, geometry shapes, code rendering), and the Google Maps API (real-world spatial queries), with multi-round human quality checks on labels and instructions. The empirical evaluation benchmarks six frontier models (Idefics2, Claude 3 Sonnet, Gemini Ultra, Gemini Flash, Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4 Turbo) against a human baseline (95.8% average), revealing a substantial performance gap, and conducts ablations on single- vs. multi-image presentation, interleaving, sequence vs. set structure, and zero-shot vs. two-shot prompting, supplemented by a manual failure analysis of 125 incorrect predictions categorized into reasoning errors, image-reading errors, and calculation errors.

## Results

On the ReMI benchmark, the best-performing model (Gemini 1.5 Pro) achieves 50.5% overall accuracy, followed by GPT-4 Turbo (45.8%) and Gemini Flash (44.5%), while human performance reaches 95.8%—a gap of approximately 45 percentage points. The naive text-only baseline scores just 13.1%, confirming that visual information is essential but far from sufficient for current models. Performance is highly task-dependent: Gemini 1.5 Pro leads on IQ (76.0%), Isomorphism (72.0%), and GeomCost (81.5%), while GPT-4 Turbo leads on EmojiAlgebra (57.5%) and Collisions (62.0%), and all models struggle severely on Clocks (best: 5.0% for Claude 3 Sonnet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five model families (Idefics2, Claude 3 Sonnet, Gemini Ultra/Flash/1.5 Pro, GPT-4 Turbo) against a naive text-only baseline and human annotators. Gemini 1.5 Pro is the overall best model at 50.5%, outperforming GPT-4 Turbo (45.8%) by 4.7 points and Gemini Flash (44.5%) by 6.0 points; however, no model comes close to the 95.8% human score. GPT-4 Turbo wins on EmojiAlgebra (57.5% vs. 44.5% for Gemini 1.5) and Collisions (62.0% vs. 50.5%), while Gemini 1.5 dominates on IQ (76.0% vs. 62.5%), Isomorphism (72.0% vs. 71.5%), and GeomCost (81.5% vs. 70.5%). Claude 3 Sonnet and Gemini Ultra trail significantly (35.2% and 28.6% respectively), with Gemini Ultra underperforming even the naive baseline on EmojiAlgebra (2.5% vs. 0.0% baseline but far below all other models). Idefics2 is the weakest model at 15.7%, barely above the 13.1% naive baseline, indicating limited multi-image reasoning capability in open-source models. The authors also show that providing images separately (multi-image input) yields a 19.8% average error reduction over single-image input for interleaved tasks, versus only 4.9% for non-interleaved tasks, suggesting that current models benefit substantially from structured multi-image prompting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of vision-language models and multi-image reasoning. While prior vision-language benchmarks (MathVista, MMMU, BLINK) and cross-image reasoning datasets (NLVR, MLLM-CompBench) exist, the authors argue none test complex, step-by-step reasoning that requires integrating information across multiple heterogeneous images spanning diverse domains. ReMI extends the single-image evaluation paradigm by introducing 13 tasks that explicitly require sequential or set-based consumption of 2–6 images, interleaved with text, covering domains from algebra and physics to graph theory and spatial navigation. The benchmark inherits the vision-language model evaluation framework (prompting with images and text, measuring accuracy) but repositions the core challenge from perceiving a single visual scene to synthesizing and reasoning across multiple visual sources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark ~45-point gap between the best model (Gemini 1.5 Pro at 50.5%) and human performance (95.8%), with some tasks like Clocks showing near-total model failure (~4%) despite being conceptually simple. The failure analysis decomposes errors into reasoning errors (37.6%) versus image-reading errors (24.8%), providing actionable targets for future model development. The finding that interleaved, separately-fed images substantially outperform a single composite image (19.8% vs 4.9% ERP gain) has practical implications for how multi-image prompts should be structured. Open questions include whether the gap can be closed with better visual encoders, longer context windows, or specialized multi-image training, and whether the domain-specific weaknesses (e.g., analog clock reading, graph isomorphism) reflect fundamental architectural limitations.
