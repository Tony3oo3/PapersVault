## Problem

Prior knowledge-conflict research has exclusively examined the interaction between a model's parametric memory and externally retrieved evidence, leaving two critical scenarios unexplored: conflicts arising within the model's own encoded knowledge (e.g., from contradictory pre-training data) and the interplay between these two conflict forms. Additionally, no existing benchmark covers the full spectrum of real-world conflict causes—misinformation, temporal drift, and semantic polysemy—at a scale sufficient for systematic analysis across model families and sizes.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes Wikidata quintuplets (subject, relation, object, subject description, object description) and produces 7.45M claim-evidence pairs and 553K four-option QA pairs, where each question pairs a default answer with a substituted conflict answer plus two distractors. Evaluation is performed via greedy token-probability scoring over the four option letters (A–D), yielding three derived metrics: Original Answer Ratio (OAR), Counter Answer Ratio (CAR), and their ratio, the Memorization Ratio (M_R). For embedded-conflict experiments, the input is a mixture of default and conflict evidence at controlled ratios (1:0, 2:1, 1:1, 2:3) fed as continued-pretraining tokens (1.06B per configuration).

## Method

CONFLICTBANK is constructed through a four-stage pipeline: (1) extracting 2.86M facts from Wikidata as quintuplets over the top-100 relations; (2) generating conflict claims via type-specific entity substitution—misinformation replaces the object with a same-relation entity, temporal adds a future timestamp span, and semantic generates an alternative subject description to create polysemous ambiguity; (3) prompting LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct to produce coherent supporting evidence in three controlled text styles (Wikipedia, book, news); and (4) applying three quality gates—refusal-feature filtering, DeBERTa-V2 NLI entailment checking, and an SBERT-based conflict-confirmation classifier (validated at >95% human accuracy). The empirical study then evaluates 12 LLMs (Gemma, LLaMA2, LLaMA3, Qwen1.5; 0.5B–72B) under retrieved-conflict, embedded-conflict (via continued pre-training on 8×A100 GPUs with DeepSpeed ZeRO-3), and interplay settings, revealing that larger models are more susceptible to conflicting evidence, implicit temporal/semantic conflicts cause greater confusion than explicit misinformation, and internal conflicts amplify reliance on the most recent external evidence.

## Results

Across 12 LLMs (0.5B–72B) from four model families, all models exhibit Memorization Ratios (M_R) below 50% when presented with solely contradictory external evidence, confirming high susceptibility to retrieved misinformation. When given two evidence pieces (one matching, one conflicting), all models shift to M_R above 50%, revealing strong confirmation bias. LLMs are most sensitive to semantic conflicts (lowest M_R) and least to misinformation conflicts; for example, LLAMA2-70B shows M_R of 3.73% (semantic) vs. 9.45% (misinformation) in the two-evidence setting. Injecting conflicting data during continued pre-training causes dramatic OAR drops (e.g., LLAMA2-7B loses 41.5% OAR at a 2:1 conflict ratio), yet models retain the ability to follow external evidence when it is explicitly provided.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four open-source model families (GEMMA 2B/7B, LLAMA2 7B/13B/70B, LLAMA3 7B/70B, QWEN1.5 0.5B–72B) plus GPT-4o as a closed-source reference. Within each family, larger models consistently show lower M_R in the two-evidence setting, indicating greater susceptibility to conflicting knowledge: in LLAMA2, M_R drops by 5.12% (7B→13B) and 6.37% (13B→70B) on average across conflict types. GPT-4o mirrors the open-source pattern, with the lowest single-evidence M_R on semantic conflicts (2.86%) and the highest multi-evidence M_R also on semantic conflicts (78.26%), confirming that the benchmark generalizes to frontier closed-source models. For embedded conflicts, QWEN1.5-4B, MISTRAL-7B, and LLAMA3-8B all show significant OAR degradation under continued pre-training with conflicting data, with MISTRAL-7B and LLAMA2-7B being the most vulnerable (37.6% and 41.5% OAR drops at 2:1 ratio). Adding temporal or semantic descriptions to questions dramatically reduces M_R (e.g., LLAMA3-70B drops from 67.52% to 8.31%), suggesting that explicit context is a practical mitigation across all model families.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge-conflict evaluation line initiated by Longpre et al. (2021) and Xie et al. (2024), which studied conflicts between a model's parametric memory and externally retrieved evidence. Prior datasets (WikiContradiction, ClaimDiff, Pan et al., Tan et al.) focused almost exclusively on misinformation-type conflicts in the retrieval setting, using entity substitution or LLM generation at small scale. CONFLICTBANK departs by (i) adding two additional conflict causes—temporal and semantic—(ii) introducing embedded-knowledge conflicts via continued pre-training, and (iii) studying the interplay between retrieved and embedded conflicts, none of which were addressed together in prior work. The construction pipeline combines Wikidata triple extraction with LLM-based evidence generation in three text styles, scaling the substitution-plus-generation recipe of Xie et al. to 7.45M pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As RAG and tool-augmented LLM systems become standard in production, understanding how models handle conflicting evidence is critical for trustworthiness; CONFLICTBANK provides the first large-scale, multi-cause benchmark that enables controlled experiments across all three conflict scenarios simultaneously. The finding that larger models are paradoxically more susceptible to conflicting evidence, and that adding simple descriptions to questions dramatically shifts model behavior (e.g., LLAMA3-70B M_R drops from 67.5% to 8.3%), offers actionable guidance for prompt design and retrieval-augmented pipelines. Limitations include reliance on generative methods for conflict construction (which may not capture all real-world conflict patterns), a single future time span for temporal conflicts, and continued pre-training limited to 1.06B tokens on only three base models. Open questions include whether the observed susceptibility-to-scale trend persists beyond 70B parameters and whether the benchmark's Wikidata-centric entity structure generalizes to more open-ended or multi-hop knowledge conflicts.
