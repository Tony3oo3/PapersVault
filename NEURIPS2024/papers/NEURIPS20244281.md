## Problem

Standard accuracy in visual emotion recognition treats all misclassifications as equally severe, ignoring the psychological similarity between emotions (e.g., confusing 'excitement' with 'awe' is far less harmful than confusing it with 'anger'). No existing evaluation metric for emotion classifiers accounts for the graded severity of different misclassification types, leaving a gap between how humans perceive emotional errors and how algorithms are measured.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244281/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard N×N confusion matrix from any emotion classifier (N=8 for Mikel's model) and a precomputed 8×8 symmetric cost matrix W derived from Mikel's emotion wheel, where each entry W_{i,j} encodes both the angular wheel distance and a polarity penalty (constant C=4 for opposite-polarity pairs). It outputs two scalar scores: ECC, which weights every confusion-matrix entry by 1/W_{i,j} to give partial credit to near-miss errors, and EMC, which isolates off-diagonal entries, reweights them by 1/(W_{i,j}−1), and normalizes by the count of misclassified samples so its range is [0,1].

## Method

The core mechanism defines an emotional distance W_{i,j} on Mikel's wheel that adds a polarity constant C=4 when the two emotions have opposite valence, ensuring same-polarity distances are always smaller than cross-polarity ones. ECC replaces the binary 0/1 modulation of accuracy with the reciprocal 1/W_{i,j} applied to every confusion-matrix cell, so a misclassification between adjacent emotions receives near-full credit while a cross-polarity error receives minimal credit. EMC further extracts only the off-diagonal terms, shifts the denominator to W_{i,j}−1 to rescale the maximum to 1, and normalizes by the number of errors rather than total samples. The paper additionally introduces a ListMLE ranking loss (L_c = L_CE + α·L_ListMLE) that trains classifiers to order their predicted probabilities according to the emotional-distance ranking, and demonstrates two practical semi-supervised applications: using EMC to dynamically adjust the pseudo-labeling confidence threshold (τ'_t = τ·e/EMC_t) and using ECC/EMC to select the pseudo-labeling model that produces higher-quality labels, both validated on FI and EmoSet with ResNet backbones.

## Results

On the FI dataset with 1600 labeled samples, the proposed EMC-based threshold adjustment (Sec. 4.2.1) achieves 42.7% ACC, outperforming FixMatch (42.2%) and FlexMatch (40.6%). In the model selection setting (Sec. 4.2.2), the method built on S2-VER with ECC/EMC-guided pseudo-labeling reaches 52.1% ACC on FI (1600 labels) and 61.5% ACC on EmoSet (8000 labels), surpassing the S2-VER baseline by 0.3% and 1.3% respectively. Across all label budgets on both datasets, the proposed approach consistently outperforms all compared semi-supervised baselines by 1–3% in ACC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the threshold adjustment comparison (Sec. 4.2.1), the EMC-based dynamic threshold outperforms FixMatch by 1.5–3.0% and FlexMatch by 2.6–2.7% ACC across all label budgets on both FI and EmoSet, while being computationally cheaper since it adjusts a single global threshold rather than per-class thresholds. In the model selection comparison (Sec. 4.2.2), the ECC/EMC-guided pseudo-labeling built on S2-VER surpasses the S2-VER baseline by 1.1% on FI (80 labels: 40.2 vs 39.1) and 1.3% on EmoSet (8000 labels: 61.5 vs 60.2), and outperforms all other SOTA methods (CoMatch, SimMatch, FreeMatch, SoftMatch) by 2–12% depending on dataset and label budget. The largest gains appear at low label budgets (e.g., 4.2% over CoMatch on FI with 80 labels), suggesting the misclassification-aware metrics are most beneficial when labeled data is scarce. Notably, the method also achieves the lowest standard deviations (e.g., ±0.12 on EmoSet-8000 vs ±0.34 for S2-VER), indicating more stable training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cost-sensitive classification and hierarchy-aware severity literature (Bertinetto et al., Garg et al.) to the visual emotion recognition domain, but departs from the WordNet/LCA-based hierarchy that underpins those methods, since emotional categories lack the multi-level taxonomic structure of object classes. Instead, it grounds the misclassification cost in Mikel's emotion wheel (Zhao et al. 2016), augmenting the original wheel-distance with a polarity-aware penalty to produce a symmetric cost matrix. The semi-supervised experiments build directly on S2-VER (Jia & Yang, ECCV 2022) as the base framework, with the proposed ECC/EMC metrics inserted into the pseudo-labeling loop for threshold scheduling and model selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central observation—that recent methods have pushed ACC to high levels on FI while EMC has actually degraded—challenges the field's reliance on accuracy alone and suggests that current emotion classifiers may be making progressively worse errors even as they get more of them right. The metrics are validated through a user study (80% of images preferred by human raters) and shown to improve semi-supervised performance by ~1% over S2-VER, but the practical gains are modest and the evaluation is limited to two datasets and the 8-emotion Mikel's wheel. Open questions include whether the metrics transfer to Ekman's 6-emotion model, to video or multi-modal settings, and whether they can guide pre-training or large-model fine-tuning as the authors themselves note.
