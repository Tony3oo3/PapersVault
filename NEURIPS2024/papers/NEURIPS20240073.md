## Problem

State-of-the-art PPG-based heart rate estimation methods are trained and validated on datasets collected under controlled laboratory conditions, leaving them poorly equipped for the noisy, variable signals encountered in everyday outdoor use. Existing public datasets are small (1–36 hours), single-site, and lack the environmental diversity (temperature, altitude, ambient light, free-living activity) that real wearable deployments face. This paper addresses the gap by introducing WildPPG, a 216-hour in-the-wild multimodal dataset, and a temperature-aware estimation method that improves robustness under these real-world conditions.

## Input / Output

The proposed Temp-ResNet consumes an 8-second window of raw green PPG (128 Hz, z-score normalized, resampled to 25 Hz) from a wrist-worn reflective sensor together with the corresponding device case temperature reading (0.5 Hz, 8-second sliding window average), and outputs a single scalar heart rate estimate in bpm. The broader WildPPG dataset additionally provides synchronized red and infrared PPG, 3-axis accelerometer (128 Hz), barometric altitude, and Lead I ECG ground truth from four body sites (head, sternum, wrist, ankle), enabling multi-site and multi-wavelength analyses beyond the primary wrist-green-PPG task.

## Method

Temp-ResNet extends the 1D ResNet architecture (eight residual blocks with 1D filters of size 5, batch normalization, dropout 0.5, and global average pooling) by appending a two-layer MLP branch that encodes the segment's device case temperature: a scalar temperature value is first projected to a 5-dimensional vector (W¹ ∈ ℝ^{5×1}), then to a 10-dimensional vector (W² ∈ ℝ^{10×5}) with ReLU activations, and the resulting embedding is concatenated with the PPG feature representation before the final linear regression head. The key insight, supported by SNR analysis, is that lower device temperatures correlate with degraded PPG signal quality, so explicitly conditioning on temperature allows the network to down-weight unreliable spectral components. The model is trained with a batch size of 128, learning rate 5e-4, and early stopping on validation loss, achieving the lowest MAE (8.37 bpm) and highest Pearson correlation (45.50) among all baselines on WildPPG. The paper further benchmarks 16 additional methods spanning heuristic peak detection, supervised deep learning (DCL, FCN, LSTM, Transformer), self-supervised contrastive learning (SimCLR, TS2Vec, BYOL, etc.), and unsupervised periodicity detection across three datasets to characterize where each paradigm breaks down in the wild.

## Results

On WildPPG (the proposed real-world dataset), Temp-ResNet achieves MAE 8.37±0.02 bpm, RMSE 14.72±0.01 bpm, and ρ 45.50±0.14, outperforming the best supervised baseline 1D ResNet (MAE 8.62, RMSE 15.00, ρ 42.41) and all heuristic methods (best: PWD at MAE 11.72). On the DaLiA dataset, Temp-ResNet achieves MAE 4.40±0.02, RMSE 9.87±0.11, and ρ 85.90±0.17, marginally beating 1D ResNet (MAE 4.47, RMSE 10.03, ρ 85.87). On IEEE SPC12 (a controlled lab dataset), 1D ResNet leads with MAE 5.50±0.29, while Temp-ResNet is not evaluated due to the absence of temperature data in that dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Temp-ResNet outperforms all 14 baselines on WildPPG, the real-world dataset where it was designed. Against the closest supervised competitor 1D ResNet [52], Temp-ResNet reduces MAE from 8.62 to 8.37 bpm and improves ρ from 42.41 to 45.50 on WildPPG, and from 4.47 to 4.40 bpm MAE on DaLiA. All heuristic baselines (MSPTD [45], qppgfast [47], HeartPy [48], PWD [49]) are substantially worse on WildPPG, with the best (PWD) achieving only MAE 11.72 vs. Temp-ResNet's 8.37. Among self-supervised methods, TS2Vec [70] is the strongest (MAE 10.55 on WildPPG) but still trails Temp-ResNet by ~2 bpm; SimCLR [67], NNCLR [68], BYOL, TS-TCC [69], VICReg, and Barlow Twins [71] all perform worse. The unsupervised UPD [64] baseline performs poorly on noisy real-world data (MAE 23.12 on WildPPG), confirming that label-free periodicity detection struggles with motion artifacts. On the controlled SPC12 dataset, 1D ResNet dominates (MAE 5.50), while DCL [54], FCN, LSTM [56], and Transformer [57] all degrade significantly, highlighting that supervised deep models trained on clean lab data do not transfer well to real-world conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the HR estimation-from-PPG line of work by shifting the evaluation setting from controlled laboratory recordings (IEEE SPC, BAMI, WESAD) to genuinely free-living outdoor conditions, building on the partially outdoor DaLiA dataset but removing all protocol constraints. Methodologically, Temp-ResNet is a modest architectural extension of the 1D ResNet from Holmes (KDD 2020), adding a small MLP branch that ingests device-case temperature alongside the raw PPG signal. The broader methodological landscape it benchmarks against spans heuristic peak detectors (MSPTD, qppg, HeartPy), supervised deep architectures (DCL, FCN, LSTM, Transformer), and self-supervised contrastive pre-training (SimCLR, TS2Vec, BYOL, TS-TCC, Barlow Twins), positioning the contribution primarily as a dataset and benchmark rather than a novel architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

At 216 hours of synchronized multi-modal, multi-body-location, multi-wavelength recordings collected without a strict protocol, WildPPG is roughly 8× larger than prior PPG datasets and the first to cover continuous altitude-temperature variation (up to 3,571 m), making it a critical stress-test for wearable HR algorithms. The finding that device-case temperature inversely correlates with PPG signal quality—and that a simple temperature-aware branch improves robustness—highlights an underexplored environmental factor beyond motion artifacts. Key limitations include only 16 participants all with Fitzpatrick skin tones 1–3, 2.7% of windows rejected due to noisy ECG ground truth, and the modest absolute gain of Temp-ResNet over 1D ResNet (~0.25 bpm MAE), leaving open whether temperature awareness generalizes across skin tones, sensor types, and longer deployment periods.
