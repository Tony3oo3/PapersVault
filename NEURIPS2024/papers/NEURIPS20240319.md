## Problem

Joint optimization of camera poses and neural fields (bundle-adjusting NeRF/NeuS) is unstable under noisy poses because prior methods process each ray independently or rely on pixel-level correspondences from pre-trained matchers that are decoupled from the scene optimization. The key unexploited insight is that camera rays can be parameterized by the 3D feature volume itself, carrying both geometric and photometric information, which enables physically meaningful multi-view consistency constraints that pixel matching cannot provide.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240319/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an uncalibrated set of multi-view RGB images with noisy initial camera poses (e.g., from GPS or SfM with additive SE(3) noise). Keypoints are detected with SuperPoint and matched across views with SuperGlue, producing sparse ray-to-ray correspondences. The output is a jointly optimized 3D feature volume (16-level multi-resolution hash grid, base resolution 32) plus refined camera poses; the volume is queried by a geometry network (3-layer MLP → SDF) for mesh extraction and a texture network (4-layer MLP → color) for novel view synthesis.

## Method

CRAYM introduces camera ray matching into the joint pose-field optimization by distinguishing key rays (through matched keypoints) from auxiliary rays (sampled around keypoints). The Key Rays Enrichment (KRE) module fuses features from auxiliary rays into each key ray via a softmax-weighted sum, stabilizing pose optimization against per-ray gradient oscillation. The Matched Rays Coherency (MRC) module computes density-weighted accumulated features along each key ray (analogous to volume rendering transmittance) and uses their cosine similarity as a matchability weight to blend rendered colors between matched rays, allowing the formulation to gracefully degenerate for erroneous matches. Two explicit geometric losses are added: an epipolar loss that constrains camera poses by minimizing the distance between a keypoint's projection and the epipolar line (decoupled from unreliable depth), and a point-alignment loss that minimizes the 3D distance between back-projected matched points to promote depth convergence. The entire pipeline—feature volume, geometry network, texture network, and camera poses—is trained end-to-end with AdamW (lr=0.01) using a progressive feature mask for coarse-to-fine training.

## Results

On the NeRF-Synthetic dataset (mean over 4 objects), CRAYM achieves PSNR 30.34, SSIM 0.95, LPIPS 0.05, and Chamfer distance 0.06, outperforming the strongest baseline L2G-NeRF (PSNR 28.62, CD 0.17) by ~1.7 dB in PSNR and 3× in CD. On the LLFF real-scene dataset, CRAYM attains mean PSNR 24.76 and LPIPS 0.18, surpassing L2G-NeRF (24.54 / 0.20) and BAA-NGP (22.52 / 0.22). On the large-scale UrbanScene3D scenes (PolyTech, ArtSci), CRAYM dramatically outperforms NeuS and PET-NeuS, e.g., PSNR 22.51 vs. 14.27 on PolyTech, and achieves CD of 0.01 vs. 0.10 for NeuS. Pose registration errors are also lowest: 1.21° rotation and 0.19 translation on LEGO, compared to 2.90°/0.10 for L2G-NeRF and 9.02°/0.81 for BARF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRAYM consistently outperforms all eight baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the closest competitor L2G-NeRF (CVPR 2023), CRAYM gains ~1.7 dB PSNR and reduces Chamfer distance by 3× on NeRF-Synthetic (30.34 vs. 28.62 PSNR; 0.06 vs. 0.17 CD), and achieves lower pose registration error (1.21° vs. 2.90° rotation). SPARF (CVPR 2023), which relies on dense pixel correspondences from a pre-trained network, lags significantly behind CRAYM on PSNR (23.90 vs. 30.34 mean) and produces over-smoothed results on dense inputs. BARF (ICCV 2021) and NeRF (ECCV 2020) are substantially weaker, with BARF at 23.09 PSNR and NeRF at 13.29 PSNR on NeRF-Synthetic. On the large-scale UrbanScene3D scenes, NeuS and PET-NeuS (which require precise poses) degrade severely (PSNR 13–15), while CRAYM maintains PSNR 19–23 with CD of 0.01–0.02. On the DTU dataset, CRAYM achieves PSNR 26.86–31.30, far exceeding SPARF (19.80–27.45) and NeuS (16.83–28.45), confirming the advantage of ray-level matching over pixel-level re-projection losses.

## Relation to Prior Work

CRAYM extends the bundle-adjusting neural field paradigm initiated by BARF and refined by L2G-NeRF, but departs from both by replacing independent per-ray optimization with explicit ray-to-ray coherence constraints. Where SPARF enforces multi-view consistency through dense pixel re-projection losses computed by a separate pre-trained network, CRAYM parameterizes camera rays through a shared 3D feature volume (multi-resolution hash encoding, as in Instant NGP) so that geometric and photometric information are jointly carried and constrained. The geometry network follows NeuS's SDF formulation and the texture network follows NeRF's volume rendering, but the key novelty is the Matched Ray Coherency module and the epipolar/point-alignment losses that integrate sparse keypoint-based ray matching directly into the end-to-end optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in multi-view 3D reconstruction: noisy camera poses from SfM or GPS that degrade both rendering and geometry. The ray-matching formulation with a learned matchability weight provides a principled way to discount erroneous correspondences without requiring a separate confidence network, and the explicit geometric losses (epipolar, point-alignment) give the optimizer physically meaningful constraints beyond photometric error. Limitations include potential non-convergence under extreme pose noise or random initialization, residual inner-surface artifacts in extracted meshes, and a design primarily targeting dense-view settings. The authors identify extending ray matching to 3D Gaussian splatting as a promising direction for improving rendering efficiency.
