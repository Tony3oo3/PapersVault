## Problem

Extreme multi-label classification (XMC) with label spaces of hundreds of thousands to millions of labels requires a classification layer that alone can consume over 12 GB of GPU memory, making end-to-end training infeasible on commodity hardware. While Dynamic Sparse Training (DST) offers a path to memory-efficient training, direct application to XMC fails because poor gradient flow from the sparse classifier to the dense transformer encoder severely hampers convergence, especially at large label spaces and high sparsity levels. The paper tackles this gap by adapting DST with architectural and training modifications that make it practical for the challenging, long-tailed, data-scarce regime of XMC.

## Input / Output

The method takes short text inputs (Amazon product titles or Wikipedia page titles, up to 128 tokens) and produces top-k label predictions from an extremely large label space ranging from 31K to 2.8M labels. The critical constraint is the classification layer: for a 768-dimensional embedding and 1M labels, the dense weight matrix plus gradients and optimizer states consume approximately 12 GB. The sparse classification layer uses a fixed fan-in structure (e.g., 128 incoming connections per label neuron at 83% sparsity), stored in ELLPACK format with 16-bit indices, reducing the effective memory overhead to 12.5% compared to a dense layer.

## Method

SPARTEX replaces the dense One-vs-All classification layer with a fixed fan-in sparse layer trained via a prune-regrow-train loop using magnitude-based pruning and random regrowth (SET), requiring no auxiliary dense buffers. The key architectural innovation is an auxiliary meta-classifier branch that assigns instances to clusters of labels via a dense classification head, providing stable gradient signals to the encoder during early training when the sparse layer's gradients are noisy and misaligned; this auxiliary loss is gradually decayed and switched off after a dataset-specific cut-off epoch to avoid interfering with the final task. For smaller label spaces (≤31K), an intermediate dense layer between the encoder and sparse classifier suffices, but for larger spaces the auxiliary objective is necessary. Custom CUDA kernels implement the sparse forward and backward passes efficiently, and the backpropagated gradient benefits from the natural activation sparsity of hinge-like losses in the enormous label space, where only a small subset of labels act as hard negatives per instance.

## Results

On Amazon-3M (2.8M labels), SPARTEX at 83% sparsity achieves P@1 of 50.2 with only 13.5 GiB peak GPU memory, compared to 46.3 GiB for the dense model (P@1 53.4) — a 3.4× memory reduction at ~3% P@1 cost. On Amazon-670K, SPARTEX reaches P@1 47.1 (vs. 49.8 dense) using 3.7 GiB (vs. 11.5 GiB dense). Critically, SPARTEX consistently outperforms the same-capacity Dense Bottleneck baseline by 2.7–3.2 P@1 points across all datasets (e.g., 50.2 vs. 47.0 on Amazon-3M), demonstrating that structured sparsity preserves more capacity than naive parameter reduction at equal memory budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the dense baseline, SPARTEX trades ~3 P@1 points on Amazon-3M (50.2 vs. 53.4) and ~2.7 on Amazon-670K (47.1 vs. 49.8) for a 3.4× memory reduction, making it the only method that fits on commodity hardware at that scale. Against the same-capacity Dense Bottleneck, SPARTEX wins decisively on every dataset (e.g., +3.2 P@1 on Amazon-3M, +2.6 on Amazon-670K), confirming that fixed-fan-in sparsity is a more capacity-efficient use of a fixed memory budget than a dense bottleneck. Against RigL, SPARTEX outperforms on Amazon-670K (47.1 vs. 45.2 P@1) and Wiki-500K (76.7 vs. 74.5), while RigL runs out of memory on Amazon-3M due to its unstructured sparsity and dense-gradient requirement. Against state-of-the-art XMC methods, SPARTEX is competitive with CascadeXML (47.1 vs. 48.5 P@1 on Amazon-670K) and LightXML (47.1 vs. 47.1) while using 5× less memory (3.7 vs. 18.3 GiB for CascadeXML), and it surpasses AttentionXML on Amazon-670K (47.1 vs. 45.7). On the label-feature datasets (Table 3), SPARTEX at 83% sparsity achieves P@1 44.5 on LF-AmazonTitles-131K, outperforming RigL (43.0) and Dense BN (39.2) while matching the memory footprint of the dense model (2.2 GiB).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Dynamic Sparse Training (DST) lineage—SET, RigL, ITOP—into the extreme multi-label classification (XMC) domain, where the classification layer dominates memory. It builds directly on Schultheis and Babbar [35], who first applied fixed-fan-in sparse DST to the XMC classifier but assumed fixed, pre-trained encoder embeddings; here the encoder is trained end-to-end, which introduces severe gradient-flow problems that the prior work did not face. The auxiliary meta-classifier objective draws on the multi-resolution training paradigm of CascadeXML and LightXML, repurposing the shortlisting branch not for retrieval but as a gradient-stabilization mechanism during early training. For the sparsity mechanism itself, the paper adopts semi-structured fixed-fan-in sparsity with magnitude-based pruning and random regrowth (SET), deliberately avoiding gradient-magnitude methods like RigL that require dense gradient buffers incompatible with the enormous classifier layer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 3.4× reduction in peak GPU memory (46.3→13.5 GiB on Amazon-3M) with only ~3% P@1 loss makes end-to-end XMC training feasible on commodity hardware, whereas prior SOTA methods either OOM or require multi-GPU setups. The finding that the meta-classifier branch provides useful encoder gradients—and that random cluster assignment performs comparably to k-means—challenges the assumed role of structured label hierarchies in XMC pipelines. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger encoders (e.g., RoBERTa-large), how it interacts with explicit negative mining strategies, and whether the auxiliary-objective trick generalizes beyond XMC to other settings with a single dominant output layer. The paper acknowledges it cannot always surpass the dense baseline, particularly at very high sparsity (≥92%) without the auxiliary loss.
