## Problem

Continuous action spaces in reinforcement learning are typically defined as large multidimensional intervals, causing agents to frequently explore irrelevant actions that are physically impossible, forbidden, or counterproductive, which slows convergence and can prevent learning. Prior continuous action masking work was restricted to intervals as relevant action sets, which is insufficient when action dimensions are coupled (e.g., quadrotor thrust) or when the relevant set has a non-rectangular shape. This paper generalizes continuous action masking to expressive convex sets, particularly zonotopes, and derives the correct policy gradients for three masking mechanisms.

## Input / Output

At each time step, the method receives the current state s and a state-dependent relevant action set A^r(s) represented as a zonotope ⟨c(s), G(s)⟩_Z with a fixed number of generators P (matching the policy network's output dimension). The relevant action set is computed online via a convex exponential cone program that maximizes the zonotope volume subject to reachability constraints ensuring the next state remains within a robust control invariant set. The output is a masked action a^r guaranteed to lie within A^r, sampled from a transformed policy distribution π_θ^r(a^r|s) that is zero outside the relevant set.

## Method

The paper introduces three continuous action masking methods that transform a standard policy π_θ(a|s) into a relevant policy π_θ^r(a^r|s) supported on A^r. The ray mask maps each sampled action radially from the center of A^r to the boundary via a bijective scaling function, and its policy gradient is provably identical to the unmasked gradient (Proposition 1). The generator mask exploits the zonotope's generator representation by letting the agent sample from a hypercube [−1,1]^P in latent generator space and applying the linear map a^r = c + Ga, yielding a closed-form relevant policy as a transformed Gaussian (Proposition 2) with explicit gradient expressions for both mean and covariance (Proposition 3). The distributional mask truncates the policy density to A^r and samples via the hit-and-run MCMC algorithm, with the normalization integral treated as constant in practice. All three methods are integrated into PPO, and experiments on four environments (Seeker, 2D/3D Quadrotor, Walker2D) show that ray and generator masks achieve higher final rewards and faster convergence than both the unmasked PPO baseline and a simple action-replacement approach.

## Results

Across four continuous-control benchmarks, all three proposed action masking methods (ray, generator, distributional) achieve substantially higher deployment episode returns than the standard PPO baseline. On the Seeker Reach-Avoid task, the generator mask reaches +18.60 ± 22.02 versus −71.03 ± 19.67 for the baseline; on the 3D Quadrotor, the baseline fails to learn a meaningful policy (−1.00) while masking methods achieve −0.63 to −0.68. On Mujoco Walker2D, the ray mask achieves 1941.82 ± 992.83 compared to 1.19 ± 0.06 for the baseline, a >1500× improvement. The distributional mask was excluded from Walker2D due to ~170× slower per-step computation from the hit-and-run sampler.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: standard PPO (no masking) and the replacement agent from Krasowski et al. [19], which substitutes out-of-set actions with a uniform sample from the relevant action set. All three masking methods consistently outperform the PPO baseline across all four environments; the gap is most dramatic on Seeker (generator: +18.60 vs. −71.03) and Walker2D (ray: 1941.82 vs. 1.19), where the baseline either collides with obstacles or triggers frequent episode resets. Against the replacement baseline, the masking methods also win on every environment where both are evaluated: on Seeker the generator mask improves from −60.21 to +18.60, and on Walker2D the ray mask improves from 234.93 to 1941.82. On the 2D and 3D Quadrotor tasks, all methods (masking and replacement) cluster tightly around −0.23 to −0.68, with the baseline lagging at −0.80 and −1.00 respectively; here the advantage of masking is primarily in convergence speed and reliability (3 of 10 baseline runs on 2D Quadrotor never converge). The ray mask is the strongest overall performer, while the generator mask excels on Seeker; the distributional mask is competitive but suffers from cubic scaling in action dimensionality, making it infeasible for the 6-dimensional Walker2D task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the continuous action masking framework of Krasowski et al. [19], which was restricted to multidimensional intervals as relevant action sets, to general convex sets represented as zonotopes or polytopes. It builds on the well-established discrete action masking literature (games, motion planning, power systems) and the reachability-analysis toolbox from formal methods (zonotope containment, support functions, robust control invariant sets) to compute state-dependent relevant action sets. The three proposed methods—ray mask, generator mask, and distributional mask—each derive their policy gradient implications for PPO, with the ray and generator masks shown to reduce to the interval-based approach of [19] as a special case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is most relevant for safety-critical continuous-control applications (robotics, autonomous systems) where a verified safe action set can be computed from system dynamics and where sample efficiency is paramount due to expensive simulation or limited real-world training. Key limitations include the requirement for convex relevant action sets (excluding disjoint or non-convex feasible regions), the cubic scaling of the distributional mask making it infeasible beyond low dimensions, and the current restriction to on-policy PPO. Open questions include extending to off-policy algorithms (TD3, SAC), handling non-convex sets via hybrid or polynomial zonotope approaches, and determining the optimal trade-off between relevant action set tightness and computation time.
