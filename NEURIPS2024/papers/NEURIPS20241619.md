## Problem

Pay-per-token pricing for LLM text generation creates a moral hazard: firms can secretly substitute cheaper, lower-quality models since inference is internal and quality is stochastic. Standard contract theory breaks down when the principal lacks knowledge of the agent's true internal cost structure, leaving no principled way to design pay-for-performance schemes that guarantee quality regardless of cost uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an n×m distribution matrix F (each row is the quality-score distribution of a candidate model under a fixed evaluator), a cost upper bound b ≥ c_n − c_1 (the principal's only knowledge of the agent's cost spread), and a target action index n. It produces an optimal contract t ∈ R^m_{≥0} mapping each quality score to a payment, which is equivalently a minimax composite hypothesis test ψ* ∈ [0,1]^m scaled by a budget factor B_r(ψ*, b) that depends on the chosen risk measure and the cost bound. The contract must incentivize the target model for every cost vector consistent with the bound b, not just the true one.

## Method

The core contribution is a direct correspondence between optimal cost-robust contracts and minimax composite hypothesis tests: the min-budget contract equals the minimax sum-optimal test (minimizing worst-case FP+FN) scaled by b/(1−R*), and the min-pay contract equals the minimax ratio-optimal test (minimizing worst-case FP/TP) scaled by b/(TP−FP). This generalizes the two-action min-budget result of Saig et al. (NeurIPS'23) to arbitrary action spaces and multiple optimality objectives. The proof proceeds by showing that the worst-case cost vector (all alternatives at zero cost, target at cost b) reduces the contract LP to the statistical LP for hypothesis testing, and that Lemma 1 (incentivizing against lower costs is harder) extends feasibility to all cost vectors in the ambiguity set. Under a Monotone Likelihood Ratio condition on F, the optimal contract takes a simple monotone threshold form, and the approximation ratio relative to the cost-aware optimum is exactly b/a where a is the true minimum cost gap. Empirically, cost-robust contracts sacrifice only 1.7–15% in objective value over cost-aware contracts across code generation and MT-Bench benchmarks.

## Results

On the MT-Bench multi-outcome (m=10) setting with monotone contracts, cost-robust contracts sacrifice only a marginal increase in objective values compared to cost-aware counterparts: a 15% increase in expected payment (min-pay), a 1.7% increase in budget (min-budget), and a 6.7% increase in variance (min-variance). For binary-outcome code generation tasks (MBPP and HumanEval), the cost-robustness penalty is consistently around 15% in expected payment. The optimal monotone cost-robust min-budget contract exhibits an intuitive three-level structure: zero pay for the lowest quality score, a fixed baseline for intermediate scores, and extra pay for the highest quality score.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead evaluates the cost of the proposed cost-robustness guarantee relative to the idealized cost-aware setting (where the principal knows exact inference costs). Across all three optimality objectives on MT-Bench, cost-robust contracts incur only a small penalty: 15% for min-pay, 1.7% for min-budget, and 6.7% for min-variance in the monotone setting. In the non-monotone setting (Table 5), the penalties are even smaller: 6.9% for min-pay, 8.7% for min-budget, and 6.5% for min-variance. The paper also quantifies the price of imposing monotonicity: requiring monotone contracts increases the min-pay objective by 388% relative to the non-monotone optimum (4.82 vs. 0.92 expected payment), while the min-budget and min-variance objectives see more moderate increases. For binary-outcome code generation, the key finding is that contract pricing depends on the relative gap between model pass rates rather than absolute task difficulty, with MBPP (easier task, smaller performance gap) yielding more expensive contracts than HumanEval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic contract design framework of Dutting et al. (EC'19) and the hypothesis-testing-to-contracts correspondence of Saig et al. (NeurIPS'24) in two key directions: (1) generalizing from two-action min-budget contracts to arbitrary action spaces with multiple optimality objectives (min-budget, min-pay, min-variance), and (2) introducing cost-robustness, a new robustness dimension where the principal only knows an upper bound on the agent's cost spread. The statistical connection (Theorem 1) unifies these generalizations by showing that optimal cost-robust contracts correspond to minimax composite hypothesis tests, with the scaling factor determined by the minimax risk. The paper also draws on the LLM evaluation literature (LLM-as-a-judge, code pass/fail evaluators) to ground the contract design in practical automated quality assessment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and growing market failure—moral hazard in LLM inference where firms can silently substitute cheaper models—and provides a principled, implementable solution with provable guarantees. The empirical finding that cost-robustness incurs only a 1.7–15% premium over cost-aware contracts makes the framework practically viable. Key limitations include reliance on benchmark distributions that may not reflect real-world prompt diversity, rough cost estimation from energy data, and restriction to a single target action. Open questions include learning contracts from finite data, extending to multi-target or sequential settings, and validating the framework in actual LLM marketplaces.
