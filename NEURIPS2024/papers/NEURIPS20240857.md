## Problem

Topological optimization via gradient descent on persistence diagrams produces extremely sparse gradients—only a handful of points in a point cloud receive non-zero updates—making convergence slow and the approach impractical for large-scale data. Moreover, these sparse gradients are defined only at the current observation, preventing reuse on new data (e.g., for black-box autoencoder latent spaces), blocking inversion for data generation, and limiting transferability across datasets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a point cloud X ∈ R^{n×d} (or a latent-space embedding from a pre-trained black-box autoencoder) and a topological loss ℓ (simplification, augmentation, or registration against a target persistence diagram), and produces an optimized point cloud or latent space satisfying the prescribed topological constraint. For scalability, the gradient is computed on a random s-point subsample (s ≪ n) while the resulting diffeomorphic vector field is evaluated at all n points in O(n) time per iteration. In the autoencoder setting, the output is additionally a fixed diffeomorphic flow φ: R^{d'} → R^{d'} that can be re-applied to new encoder outputs in linear time and inverted to push samples back through the decoder for data generation.

## Method

The core contribution is replacing the sparse vanilla topological gradient ∇L(X) with its diffeomorphic interpolation ṽ: R^d → R^d, defined as the minimum-norm element in a Gaussian-kernel RKHS that matches ∇L(X) at the critical points (the few indices where the gradient is non-zero). Concretely, ṽ(x) = Σ_{i∈I} ρ_σ(‖x−x_i‖) α_i with α = K^{-1}a, where K is the Gaussian kernel matrix over the critical points and ρ_σ(u) = exp(−u²/2σ²). Two theoretical guarantees are proved: (i) following the flow dX/dt = −ṽ_t(X(t)) still monotonically decreases the topological loss (Proposition 3.1), and (ii) the Lipschitz constant of ṽ is upper-bounded by a quantity depending on the kernel bandwidth σ, the condition number of K, and the total persistence of the current diagram (Proposition 3.2). Combined with subsampling, the gradient is computed on a small s-point sample while the diffeomorphic interpolation updates the full n-point cloud at each step, enabling optimization on point clouds with tens of thousands of points (e.g., the 35,947-vertex Stanford Bunny) where vanilla gradient descent is completely stuck. For black-box autoencoders, the sequence of per-iteration diffeomorphisms is composed into a single fixed flow φ that is learned once and then re-applied (or inverted) to arbitrary new data, enabling both topological regularization of latent spaces and interpretable data generation without re-running optimization.

## Results

On the COIL image datasets, diffeomorphic topological optimization of VAE latent spaces improves Pearson correlation scores between ground-truth rotation angles and latent-space angles across all five objects, with the most dramatic gain on Pig (0.17 → 0.76) and Vase (0.86 → 0.93). On the scHiC single-cell dataset, the augmentation loss improves correlation from 0.79 to 0.84, while the registration loss yields a modest gain (0.792 → 0.794). On the Stanford Bunny (n = 35,947 points), vanilla gradient descent with subsampling barely changes the point cloud after 1,000 epochs, whereas diffeomorphic gradient descent achieves meaningful loss reduction in 200 epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) vanilla topological gradient descent [4, 28], which updates only the sparse set of critical points, and (2) the 'big-step gradient' method (oineus) [34], which extends the gradient to a larger critical set. In the convergence-speed experiment (n = 200, Figure 4), both oineus and the proposed diffeomorphic interpolation significantly outperform vanilla gradients in loss decrease per iteration, with the combined use of oineus + diffeomorphic interpolation yielding the fastest convergence by a slight margin. However, oineus introduces a 10–20× per-iteration computational overhead, making the diffeomorphic approach the fastest in raw wall-clock time to reach convergence. In the large-scale Stanford Bunny experiment (n = 35,947, Figure 5), vanilla gradient descent with subsampling is effectively stuck after 1,000 epochs (barely any point cloud change), while the diffeomorphic method decreases the augmentation loss substantially within 200 epochs. For the black-box autoencoder task, the diffeomorphic flow is learned once and re-applied to new data in linear time, whereas both baselines require re-running gradient descent from scratch for every new dataset, and neither supports data generation by sampling the optimized latent space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the topological optimization framework of Carrière et al. [4] and the differential calculus on persistence barcodes of Leygonie et al. [28], which established that topological losses are generically differentiable but yield extremely sparse gradients. It departs from prior densification strategies—Nigmetov & Morozov's 'big-step gradients' [34] and Solomon et al.'s subsample-averaged gradients [39]—by replacing the sparse gradient entirely with a smooth diffeomorphism constructed via RKHS kernel interpolation (Gaussian kernel, following Younes [43]). The key conceptual shift is that the resulting vector field is defined on all of R^d, not just on the critical points of the current point cloud, enabling applications (black-box AE flow learning, data generation by flow inversion) that are intractable with pointwise gradient methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sparsity of topological gradients has been a fundamental practical bottleneck limiting topological optimization to small point clouds; this paper demonstrates that diffeomorphic interpolation combined with subsampling scales to 35K+ points (Stanford Bunny) where vanilla methods are completely stuck, and enables a one-time-learn, linear-time-reapply paradigm for black-box autoencoder latent spaces. The quantified Lipschitz constant bound provides theoretical control over the smoothness of the induced deformation. Open questions include stability of the interpolation under subsampling, adaptive bandwidth selection, convergence guarantees for the full diffeomorphic gradient descent, and extension to multiparameter persistent homology. A key limitation is that diffeomorphisms preserve topology, so the method cannot create or destroy loops—only deform them—constraining its applicability to topological augmentation or simplification tasks.
