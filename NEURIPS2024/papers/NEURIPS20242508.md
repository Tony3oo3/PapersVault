## Problem

The conventional wisdom in offline RL attributes poor performance to imperfect value function learning, motivating extensive research on better value estimation. However, it remains unclear whether value learning is truly the main bottleneck, or whether policy extraction from the value function and test-time generalization to out-of-distribution states are the more limiting factors. This paper asks: given an offline RL problem, is the bottleneck in the value function, the policy, or the policy's ability to generalize at deployment?

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on decoupled offline RL pipelines where a value function is first trained on an offline dataset of state-action sequences, then a policy is extracted separately. The data-scaling matrices vary the number of transitions allocated to value training (0.1M–10M) versus policy training independently, enabling disentanglement of the two stages. At test time, the OPEX and TTT methods consume the frozen Q function and the current state observation to produce an adjusted action (a single gradient step or iterative parameter update), rather than simply outputting the offline-trained policy's action unchanged.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical study using data-scaling matrices across 8 environments (state-based and pixel-based, locomotion, manipulation, goal-conditioned) to dissect three bottlenecks: value learning (B1), policy extraction (B2), and test-time generalization (B3). By comparing three value learning objectives (IQL, SARSA, CRL) with three policy extraction objectives (AWR, DDPG+BC, SfBC) under varying data budgets, they show that DDPG+BC consistently outperforms AWR because it can hill-climb the value function beyond the convex hull of dataset actions, whereas AWR is mode-covering and susceptible to overfitting from a small effective sample size. For the generalization bottleneck, they demonstrate via offline-to-online MSE tracking that training/validation MSEs saturate while evaluation MSE continues to improve, indicating in-distribution policy quality is already near-optimal. They then propose two test-time policy improvement techniques: OPEX, which adds a single value-gradient step to the sampled action during evaluation (a ← a + β∇ₐQ(s,a)), and TTT, which iteratively updates policy parameters on test-time states using a KL-regularized value maximization objective while keeping the Q function and offline policy frozen.

## Results

Across 16 task–value-algorithm settings (8 environments × 2 value learners), DDPG+BC outperforms or matches AWR in 15 out of 16 cases, with the largest gaps on highly suboptimal exploratory data (e.g., exorl-walker/IQL: DDPG+BC 191±6 vs. AWR 99±1; exorl-cheetah/SARSA: DDPG+BC 131±3 vs. AWR 78±1). SfBC sits between the two, typically closer to DDPG+BC. The two proposed test-time improvement methods (OPEX and TTT) further boost performance over vanilla IQL and SfBC on most of the eight evaluated tasks, confirming that test-time policy generalization is a major bottleneck.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is among three policy extraction objectives applied to the same frozen value function. DDPG+BC (Fujimoto & Gu, 2021) dominates AWR (Peng et al., 2019) in 15 of 16 settings, with the most dramatic wins on exploratory datasets (exorl-walker: 191 vs. 99; exorl-cheetah: 131 vs. 78 under SARSA), while AWR wins only on d4rl-hopper/IQL (53 vs. 52, within noise) and gc-roboverse/IQL (23 vs. 20). SfBC (Chen et al., 2023) consistently falls between the two, suggesting that sampling-based selection partially captures value information but cannot match gradient-based hill-climbing. On the value-learning axis, IQL (Kostrikov et al., 2022), SARSA (Brandfonbrener et al., 2021), and CRL (Eysenbach et al., 2022) produce broadly comparable performance when paired with the same policy extraction method, reinforcing the paper's thesis that the policy extraction choice, not the value learner, is the dominant bottleneck. The two proposed test-time methods (OPEX and TTT) outperform both vanilla IQL+AWR and SfBC on most tasks in Figure 8, with gains of 10–30+ points on antmaze and kitchen, further isolating test-time generalization as a key limiting factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the decoupled offline RL framework where a value function (IQL, SARSA, or CRL) is first trained independently of the policy, and then a policy is extracted via a separate objective (AWR, DDPG+BC, or SfBC). It departs from the dominant research narrative that has focused almost exclusively on improving value function quality (conservatism, pessimism, uncertainty estimation) by systematically showing that policy extraction and test-time generalization are often the binding constraints. The closest prior analysis is Fu et al. (NeurIPS 2022), which also examined value vs. policy accuracy, but was limited to D4RL locomotion tasks and did not identify test-time generalization as a bottleneck or study data-scaling properties. The paper introduces the data-scaling matrix as a diagnostic tool and proposes two new test-time policy improvement techniques (OPEX and TTT) that extend the DDPG+BC framework with on-the-fly value gradient adjustment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides practitioners with a concrete, empirically validated recipe: use DDPG+BC over AWR for policy extraction, prioritize high-coverage data over high-optimality data, and apply test-time policy improvement (OPEX/TTT) to mitigate the generalization bottleneck. It reframes a significant portion of the offline RL research agenda, suggesting that the community's heavy investment in value function improvements may be misdirected relative to the actual performance ceiling. Key limitations include the restriction to continuous action spaces (DDPG+BC is not straightforwardly defined for discrete actions), the use of MSE metrics as proxies for policy accuracy, and evaluation on a finite set of environments. Open questions include how to design policy extraction objectives that inherently generalize to out-of-distribution states, and whether the test-time improvement techniques scale to high-dimensional or multi-task settings.
