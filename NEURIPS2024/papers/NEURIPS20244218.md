## Problem

For SGD-style optimizers, the optimal learning rate scales monotonically (linearly or with square root) with batch size, but this relationship does not hold for Adam-style optimizers that use the sign of gradients. Previous approximations (e.g., square-root scaling from McCandlish et al. and Granziol et al.) fail to capture the true behavior: the optimal learning rate for Adam first rises, peaks, then falls as batch size grows—a 'surge' phenomenon—before eventually converging to a non-zero value at very large batch sizes. This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical derivation of this non-monotonic scaling law.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes as input the batch size B, per-parameter gradient statistics (mean μ_i and variance σ_i assumed Gaussian), and the Hessian matrix H of the loss function, and produces a closed-form expression for the optimal learning rate ε_opt(B), the peak batch size B_noise (where the surge reaches its maximum), and the expected loss improvement ΔL_opt(B). A key property is that B_noise is not a fixed constant but shifts to larger values as training progresses and the loss decreases, meaning the peak of the surge curve moves rightward over the course of training. In practice, B_noise can be estimated via a simple linear fit of 1/S vs. 1/E (steps vs. examples to reach a target loss) from a small number of grid-search runs, avoiding exhaustive hyperparameter search.

## Method

The paper approximates Adam-style optimizers as sign-of-gradient updates (θ_{i+1} = θ_i − ε·sign(G_est)), justified by showing that both the β→1 and β→0 limits of Adam reduce to this form. Under a Gaussian gradient assumption, it derives that both the expected value and covariance of sign(G_est) depend on batch size B (unlike SGD where only the covariance does), yielding a numerator that is first-order in B and a denominator that is second-order in B. This structural difference produces the closed-form scaling law ε_opt(B) = ε_max / [½(√(B_noise/B) + √(B/B_noise))], which peaks at B = B_noise and transitions to square-root scaling for B ≪ B_noise and to a constant for B ≫ B_noise. The paper further proves that the data/time efficiency trade-off (S/S_min − 1)(E/E_min − 1) = 1 still holds for Adam, confirming B_noise as the balance point between training speed and data efficiency. Extensive grid-search experiments across four workloads (CNN-FashionMNIST, ResNet18-TinyImageNet, DistilGPT2-ELI5, and MoE-RedPajama-v2) validate the surge shape and the rightward drift of B_noise during training.

## Results

The paper derives a closed-form scaling law for Adam-style optimizers: ε_opt(B) = ε_max / [½(√(B_noise/B) + √(B/B_noise))], which predicts a peak (surge) in optimal learning rate at B = B_noise, followed by a decline before asymptotic convergence to a non-zero value at very large batch sizes. Across four workloads (CNN-FashionMNIST, ResNet18-TinyImageNet, DistilGPT2-ELI5Category, MoE-RedPajama-v2), grid-search experiments confirm the predicted surge: the optimal learning rate rises, peaks near the estimated B_noise, then decreases as batch size grows further. The estimated B_noise shifts to larger values as training loss decreases (e.g., in ResNet18-TinyImageNet, the peak moves rightward from L=5.213 to L=4.633), consistent with the theoretical prediction that B_noise increases with training progress.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its derived Adam scaling law (Eq 2) against two prior approximations for Adam-style optimizers. McCandlish et al. [24] model Adam with a square-root scaling (α=0.5 in their general form ε_opt = ε_max/(1+B_noise/B)^α), which monotonically increases with batch size and fails to capture the observed peak and subsequent decline in optimal learning rate. Granziol et al. [23] similarly use α=0.5 from a random-matrix-theory perspective, yielding the same monotonic square-root behavior. In all four experimental workloads, the paper's curve correctly predicts the peak optimal learning rate and the downward trend beyond B_noise, while the α=0.5 baseline curves overestimate the optimal learning rate at large batch sizes. The paper also shows that for small batch sizes (B ≪ B_noise), its formula reduces to the same square-root scaling (Eq 4) used by [23, 24], confirming consistency in the small-batch regime but diverging in the critical mid-to-large batch range where the surge occurs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical large-batch training framework of McCandlish et al. [24]—which established the B_noise trade-off between training speed and data efficiency for SGD—to Adam-style optimizers, leveraging the sign-of-gradient approximation from Balles & Hennig [26]. The key departure is that prior work [24, 23] approximated Adam's scaling law as monotonic square-root scaling (α=0.5), but this paper shows that because both the numerator and denominator of the optimal learning rate depend on batch size (unlike SGD), the true relationship is non-monotonic: the optimal LR peaks at B_noise and then decreases. This 'surge' phenomenon is absent from all prior scaling-law formulations for Adam.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result corrects a widely-used approximation in practice: practitioners following square-root scaling for Adam will overestimate the optimal learning rate at larger batch sizes, potentially destabilizing training. The finding that B_noise (the peak) shifts rightward as training progresses suggests that static LR schedules are suboptimal and motivates adaptive batch-size/LR co-scheduling as future work. Limitations include reliance on a quadratic loss approximation and Gaussian gradient assumption, which may break down at the edge of stability or with very large learning rates; the experiments, while spanning CV and NLP, use relatively small models (up to a small MoE), leaving open whether the surge persists at frontier scale.
