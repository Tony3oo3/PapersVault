## Problem

Progress in sEMG-based human-computer interfaces has been hampered by the absence of large-scale public datasets with well-defined tasks and reproducible benchmarks. Existing sEMG datasets are small (fewer than 40 subjects), rely on clinical-grade hardware requiring extensive skin preparation, or focus on static hand poses rather than natural dynamic tasks. This paper introduces emg2qwerty, the largest public sEMG dataset for touch typing (108 users, 1,135 sessions, 346 hours), along with baselines and benchmarks that explicitly measure generalization across users and data-efficient personalization.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 32-channel differential sEMG signals (16 per wrist) sampled at 2 kHz with 12-bit depth and 20–850 Hz bandpass filtering, converted into 33-dimensional log-scaled spectral features computed every 8 ms over a 32 ms window. The output is a variable-length sequence of lower-case characters and basic punctuation, including backspace corrections, transcribed from the continuous sEMG stream. Unlike ASR, the output operates directly at the character level with no phoneme-to-grapheme mapping, and the backspace key introduces a history-modification operation absent in speech decoding.

## Method

The baseline adapts standard ASR components to sEMG: a Time-Depth Separable ConvNet (TDS) with a 1-second receptive field captures co-articulation effects from adjacent keystrokes, trained with CTC loss and a 6-gram modified Kneser-Ney character-level language model integrated via a custom backspace-aware beam-search decoder. Two key sEMG-specific design choices distinguish the approach: (1) Rotation-Invariance modules that average outputs over ±1 electrode channel shifts, combined with rotation augmentation, to handle band placement variability across sessions; and (2) temporal alignment jitter augmentation that randomly offsets left-right band synchronization by up to 60 ms. The benchmark protocol trains a generic model on 100 users, then evaluates zero-shot generalization (CER >50% on unseen users) and data-efficient personalization via fine-tuning on 8 held-out users, achieving 6.95% test CER with the language model.

## Results

On the emg2qwerty benchmark, the best configuration (personalized model finetuned from the generic model, with a 6-gram character-level language model) achieves a mean test Character Error Rate (CER) of 6.95% ± 3.61% across 8 held-out users, with the best individual user reaching 3.16% CER. Without personalization, the generic model is essentially unusable at 51.78% ± 4.61% test CER (with LM), confirming that cross-user domain shift is severe. Adding the language model consistently reduces CER by 2–4 points across all model configurations, and finetuning from the generic model outperforms training from random initialization by ~2.6 points on test CER with LM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external sEMG or ASR methods; instead it evaluates three internal configurations to isolate the effects of personalization and language modeling. The generic model (trained on 100 users, tested zero-shot on 8 unseen users) achieves >50% CER, rendering it unusable without user-specific data. Personalizing from random initialization reduces test CER to 9.55% (with LM), crossing the ~10% usability threshold. Finetuning the generic model with user-specific sessions further improves test CER to 6.95% (with LM), a 2.6-point gain over random-init personalization, demonstrating that generalizable cross-user representations are learned. The 6-gram character-level Kneser-Ney language model provides a consistent 2–4 point CER reduction across all three configurations, with the largest absolute gain (4.4 points) on the generic model. All reported improvements from personalization and LM inclusion are statistically significant (p < .005).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the standard ASR pipeline—TDS ConvNets (Hannun et al., 2019), CTC loss (Graves et al., 2006), SpecAugment (Park et al., 2019), and n-gram language model decoding—into the sEMG-to-keystroke transcription domain, drawing a structural analogy between speech recognition and typing transcription. It departs from prior sEMG HCI datasets (Amma et al., 2015; Du et al., 2017; Atzori et al., 2012) in three key ways: using consumer-grade dry-electrode hardware (sEMG-RD) rather than clinical arrays, recording natural continuous typing rather than static hand poses, and providing multi-session data per user at a scale (108 users, 1,135 sessions) an order of magnitude larger than existing benchmarks. The work also positions itself relative to intracortical BCI text/speech decoding (Willett et al., 2021, 2023; Metzger et al., 2023) as a non-invasive, broadly applicable alternative for high-bandwidth human-computer input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

emg2qwerty fills a critical gap as the largest public sEMG typing dataset, enabling systematic study of cross-user and cross-session domain shift, data-efficient personalization, and zero-shot generalization in neuromotor interfaces—problems that prior small-scale, single-session datasets could not address. The finding that a generic model is essentially unusable (>50% CER) on unseen users, yet its representations transfer effectively via finetuning (~7% CER), quantifies the personalization bottleneck and motivates research into domain adaptation and self-supervised pretraining for sEMG. Key limitations include the requirement of touch-typing skill, English-only content, physical keyboard use (versus keyboard-free deployment), and dependence on proprietary hardware, all of which constrain real-world applicability and leave open questions about force-level domain shift and on-device inference.
