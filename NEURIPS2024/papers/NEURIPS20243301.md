## Problem

Prior optimal teaching results for linear learners (SVM, perceptron) target individual biased learners, producing teaching sets tailored to specific optimization biases that may fail for other learners. This paper asks: what is the smallest dataset that unambiguously teaches a target policy to an entire family of consistent linear BC learners simultaneously, where each learner may have different biases? The answer requires teaching the hardest member of the family — the linear version space learner — which is fundamentally harder than teaching any single biased learner.

## Input / Output

The teacher receives an MDP (S, A, R, P, γ, μ), a linear feature function φ: S×A → R^d, and a target optimal deterministic policy π* induced by some w* ∈ R^d. Unlike prior linear teaching work where the teacher can construct arbitrary covariate vectors in R^d, here the teacher is restricted to selecting states from the fixed state space S, and the feature differences ψ_{sab} = φ(s,a) − φ(s,b) are determined by the environment. The output is a minimal subset D* ⊆ S×A of (state, action) pairs whose induced version space cone exactly matches the full-state version space, guaranteeing zero value risk for all consistent linear learners.

## Method

The key insight is that successful teaching is equivalent to covering all extreme rays of the primal cone cone(Ψ(D_S)) generated by feature difference vectors, reducing the infinite set-cover over inconsistent weights to a finite set-cover over extreme rays. The TIE algorithm operates in two stages: (1) an iterative LP-based elimination (MinimalExtreme) identifies a unique representative for each extreme ray by testing whether removing a candidate vector x changes the dual cone, and (2) a set-cover problem selects the minimum number of states whose induced feature differences cover all extreme rays. For |A| ≤ 2 the set cover is trivially solvable in poly-time, but for |A| > 2 it is NP-hard (via reduction from set cover), so Greedy-TIE applies the standard greedy set-cover heuristic yielding a log(|A|−1) approximation on teaching dimension with O((|S||A|)^3) runtime.

## Results

On the Pick the Right Diamond environment (n=6, 15,624 states), Greedy-TIE produces an instance-optimal teaching set of size 2, reducing the teaching cost from O(5^n) to a constant. On the Visual Programming in Maze environment, Greedy-TIE finds the optimal teaching set (demonstrating each of the n actions exactly once) across all maze sizes tested. On the Polygon Tower environment, TIE recovers the exact minimum demonstration set matching the ground-truth characterization in Proposition 18, with empirical running time of approximately O(n^5). The Greedy-TIE algorithm carries a log(|A|−1) approximation guarantee on the teaching dimension and runs in O((|S||A|)^3) time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Greedy-TIE against two baselines: Teach-All (demonstrate the target action in every state) and Teach-Random (sample states uniformly at random until a valid teaching set is formed). Greedy-TIE dramatically outperforms both baselines across all three environments. On Pick the Right Diamond (n=6), Greedy-TIE achieves a teaching dimension of 2 versus 15,624 for Teach-All, a reduction of over four orders of magnitude; Teach-Random also requires substantially more demonstrations (Figure 4c). On Visual Programming in Maze, Greedy-TIE finds the provably optimal teaching set (one demonstration per action) while both baselines require significantly more states (Figure 5b). On Polygon Tower, TIE recovers the exact minimum demonstration set established analytically in Proposition 18, while Teach-All uses all states and Teach-Random converges slowly (Figure 8d). The paper also notes that prior optimal-teaching results for individual learners (e.g., linear SVM requiring only 2 examples [20]) are not directly applicable because they produce learner-specific teaching sets that may be infeasible for the broader family of consistent linear BC learners.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the machine teaching literature from individual linear learners (SVM, perceptron, logistic regression studied by Liu et al. 2016 and Zhang et al.) to teaching an entire family of consistent linear BC learners simultaneously. The key conceptual departure is that prior work could exploit each learner's specific optimization bias to construct tailored teaching sets, whereas here the teacher must produce a single dataset valid for all consistent learners, reducing the problem to teaching the hardest member—the linear version space learner. The algorithmic core draws on convex geometry (extreme ray characterization of polyhedral cones) and set cover, building on the version space teaching framework of Goldman and Kearns (1995) but in a structured linear MDP setting rather than finite hypothesis classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that optimal family teaching reduces to covering extreme rays of a primal cone provides a clean geometric characterization that makes the problem tractable for |A| ≤ 2 and yields a log(|A|-1) approximation for larger action spaces. The dramatic teaching dimension reduction (e.g., from O(5^n) to 2 in the diamond example) highlights the practical value of structured feature representations in efficient teaching. Open questions include extending to stochastic policies, non-linear learners, and settings where the teacher must navigate the MDP to reach teaching states under budget constraints, which the authors identify as important but unresolved.
