## Problem

Existing fair data distillation and coreset methods optimize local properties (clustering cost, individual fairness) of representative samples but do not guarantee that the induced distribution remains close to the original dataset, risking significant downstream performance degradation without necessarily reducing bias. This paper asks whether one can generate fair synthetic representatives that provably bound downstream learning discrepancy while enforcing demographic parity.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular dataset of n samples (features X, discrete protected attributes D, discrete labels Y), a fairness violation tolerance ε, and a target coreset size m ≪ n. It produces m synthetic feature vectors X̂ (with D and Y values fixed a priori to match original proportions) and real-valued weights θ ∈ Δ_m that are used directly in downstream training (e.g., as sample-level loss weights). Unlike subset-based coresets, the output samples are new points in feature space rather than selections from the original data.

## Method

FWC formulates a joint optimization that minimizes the Wasserstein distance between the original empirical distribution and the weighted synthetic distribution subject to linear demographic parity constraints on the weights. The problem is reduced to a nested minimization over the synthetic feature vectors X̂, where the inner problem is a linear program over the transport plan P with fairness constraints expressed as 2|Y||D| linear inequalities. This is solved via a majority minimization (MM) algorithm: each iteration solves the inner LP (using a FairWASP variant with cutting-plane methods on the low-dimensional Lagrangian dual) to construct a convex surrogate, then minimizes that surrogate to update X̂, which decomposes into m independent weighted-centroid subproblems. Without fairness constraints, the algorithm provably reduces to Lloyd's algorithm for k-means/k-medians clustering, and the paper establishes monotone convergence to a first-order stationary point with finite termination under uniqueness conditions.

## Results

FWC achieves an average demographic disparity reduction of 53% compared to all baselines (without fairness preprocessing) and 18% (with fairness preprocessing) across four real datasets (Adult, German Credit, Communities and Crime, Drug), while maintaining competitive AUC. On the Adult dataset with the fairest setting (ε=0.01), FWC achieves DD=0.02±0.02 with AUC=0.67±0.10. When used as few-shot examples for LLMs, FWC reduces demographic disparity by 75% for GPT-3.5 Turbo (from 0.040 to 0.010) and by 35% for GPT-4 (from 0.42 to 0.27), at a modest accuracy cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FWC achieves equal or better fairness-utility tradeoffs (lower disparity at equal utility, or higher utility at equal disparity) against all five baselines across all four datasets. Compared to Fairlets (Backurs et al., ICML 2019) and IndFair (Chhaya et al., AISTATS 2022), FWC produces coresets with substantially lower Wasserstein distance to the original distribution (e.g., on German Credit at 10% coreset size: FWC ε=0.05 gives 0.48 vs. Fairlet K-Means at 7.47), indicating less distribution shift that harms downstream performance. Against K-Median Coresets (Bachem et al., AISTATS 2018), FWC matches or beats clustering cost on smaller datasets while enforcing demographic parity, whereas K-Median Coresets have no fairness guarantee. Classic K-Means (Lloyd, 1982) achieves the lowest clustering cost but the highest Wasserstein distance (e.g., 77.04×10⁶ on Adult at 1% coreset size vs. FWC's 3.90×10⁶), confirming that minimizing clustering cost alone does not preserve the original data distribution. K-Medoids (Park & Jun, 2009) similarly suffers from large distribution shift. Overall, FWC's average disparity reduction is 53% without fairness preprocessing and 18% with it, demonstrating that its Wasserstein-based objective directly translates to downstream fairness gains that local-property-based methods (clustering, coverage) do not achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

FWC extends the authors' own FairWASP framework [77] from fair pre-processing (reweighting existing samples) to fair coreset construction (generating new synthetic samples with weights), adapting the cutting-plane solver to the m≠n setting. The optimization is solved via the majority minimization (MM) framework [56, 39], which provides a convex surrogate for the nonconvex Wasserstein objective. Conceptually, FWC departs from prior fair coreset methods (Fairlets, IndFair, K-Median Coresets) that optimize local clustering or individual-fairness criteria by instead minimizing the global Wasserstein distance to the original distribution, with demographic parity constraints inherited from Calmon et al. [10]. The paper further shows that without fairness constraints, FWC reduces to Lloyd's algorithm for k-means/k-medians, unifying it with classical clustering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FWC provides the first principled bridge between fair data representation and downstream learning performance, with a Wasserstein-based generalization bound (Proposition 5.5) connecting coreset quality to both distributional fidelity and demographic parity on unseen data. The LLM application—using 16 weighted coreset samples as few-shot examples to reduce GPT-3.5/4 bias by 75%/35%—is timely but underdeveloped (single dataset, limited runs, inconsistent GPT-3.5 results). Key open questions include extending to other fairness metrics (equalized odds), analyzing privacy/explainability of the synthetic samples, and understanding why FWC samples are non-i.i.d., which blocks standard finite-sample learning bounds. The near-linear runtime scaling makes it practical for large tabular datasets, but the approach is currently limited to tabular data with discrete protected attributes and outcomes.
