## Problem

RL policies trained in simulation suffer from the reality gap when deployed on real robots. Domain Randomization, the most common mitigation, requires domain-specific expertise to set parameter ranges and produces conservative, suboptimal policies. Existing system-identification approaches that optimize simulator parameters against real data are unstable, demand large amounts of costly real-world trajectories, and struggle with high-dimensional control tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240212/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

EASI takes a small set of real-world state transitions (as few as 1 trajectory, ~10,000 transitions for Cartpole) collected by any rough controller (MPC, PID, RL, or even human operation — not the final policy), an initial parameter distribution, and a motion control policy for simulation rollouts. It outputs an optimized simulator parameter distribution (e.g., a Gaussian over physical parameters like PD gains and mass), which is then used to train a transferable RL policy. The output is a distribution rather than a single parameter vector, preserving robustness to residual uncertainty.

## Method

EASI frames simulator identification as a search problem solved by an adversarial co-evolution between an Evolutionary Strategy (ES) and a WGAN-style discriminator. The discriminator D(s, a, s') is trained with Wasserstein loss (weight-clipped to [-0.01, 0.01]) to score how closely simulation state transitions match real ones, avoiding the vanishing-gradient problem of standard GAN losses. The ES — specifically (μ/μ_I, λ)-ES with μ=150, λ=300 — samples 300 parameter vectors per generation, evaluates each by running a fixed policy in 300 parallel Isaac Gym environments, and uses the mean discriminator score over each trajectory as the fitness for selection, recombination, and mutation. This replaces the gradient-based generator of a standard GAN with a derivative-free evolutionary search, making the process far more stable and less sensitive to hyperparameters. The discriminator and ES co-adapt over generations: as ES finds parameters that produce more realistic transitions, the discriminator sharpens its discrimination, providing a tighter fitness signal. The entire search completes in under 10 minutes on a single RTX 4060 Ti.

## Results

On sim-to-sim transfer tasks, EASI consistently outperforms all baselines across four target environments. For Ant (Body Mass×0.25), EASI achieves 780.7±41.5 reward versus 588.9±65.9 for UDR and 327.7±15.9 for GARAT. For CartPole (Cart DoF P×0.25), EASI reaches 164.4±4.6 versus 116.8±13.0 (UDR) and 144.4±1.2 (GARAT). In the real-world Cartpole task, EASI reduces angle error from 2.65×10⁻² (UDR) to 1.67×10⁻² and cart velocity error from 1.63×10⁻¹ to 1.21×10⁻¹.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EASI is compared against UDR (Uniform Domain Randomization, Peng et al. ICRA 2018) and GARAT (Grounded Action Transformation, Desai et al. NeurIPS 2020), with FineTune as an additional ablation. EASI wins on every reported metric in Table 1: on Ant it achieves roughly 32% higher reward than UDR (780.7 vs. 588.9 at Mass×0.25) and dramatically outperforms GARAT (780.7 vs. 327.7, a 2.4× improvement), while on CartPole it leads UDR by ~41% (164.4 vs. 116.8) and GARAT by ~14% (164.4 vs. 144.4). In the real-world Cartpole experiment (Table 2), EASI reduces angle error by 37% (1.67 vs. 2.65 ×10⁻²) and cart velocity error by 26% (1.21 vs. 1.63 ×10⁻¹) relative to UDR. GARAT's performance degrades severely under larger parameter shifts (e.g., Ant Mass×0.5 drops to 228.4), whereas EASI maintains high performance (777.4), suggesting its parameter-distribution search is more robust to out-of-distribution target dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

EASI repurposes the GAN adversarial framework by replacing the neural-network generator with an Evolutionary Strategy, using the WGAN-style discriminator as a fitness function to evolve simulator parameter distributions. This departs from prior sim-to-real system identification work (SimGAN, TuneNet, Bayesian optimization approaches) that either uses gradient-based optimization or RL to adjust parameters, and from Domain Randomization which relies on hand-specified parameter ranges. The key conceptual move is framing parameter search as an adversarial game where ES 'fools' the discriminator, inheriting WGAN's smooth Earth-Mover distance metric while gaining ES's stability and hyperparameter robustness over gradient-based alternatives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: EASI converges in under 10 minutes on consumer hardware and can identify useful parameters from as few as one real-world trajectory, dramatically lowering the data barrier for sim-to-real transfer. However, the method still requires scenario-specific data collection, is sensitive to sensor noise and task-style variation, and has only been validated on relatively simple environments (Cartpole, Ant, Go2, Ballbalance). Open questions include whether the approach scales to high-dimensional manipulation tasks, whether it can handle significant unmodeled dynamics (e.g., contact-rich interactions), and whether the ES-as-generator framing generalizes beyond physics parameter identification to other sim-to-real subproblems like visual domain gaps.
