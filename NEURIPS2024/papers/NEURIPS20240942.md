## Problem

Data balancing is widely used to mitigate undesired dependencies between covariates, outcomes, and auxiliary factors for fairness or robustness, but its failure modes are poorly characterized and hard to predict. The paper shows that joint balancing does not simply 'remove' undesired dependencies from the causal graph of the task, and that combining it with other mitigation strategies like regularization can produce unexpected and harmful interactions.

## Input / Output

The method operates on training data sampled from P^t(X,Y,Z) where X are covariates (MNIST images with synthetic noise backgrounds, Amazon review text with synthetic token substitutions, or CelebA face images), Y is a binary outcome, and Z is a discrete auxiliary factor of variation. The covariates X are decomposed into three latent components (X_Z^⊥, X_Y^⊥, X_{Y∧Z}) whose causal relationships to Y and Z are determined by the task's causal Bayesian network. The output is a prediction model f(X) evaluated on risk-invariance, worst-group accuracy, equalized odds, and confounder-encoding metrics across a family of target distributions P that vary only the Y–Z association.

## Method

The paper introduces a causal Bayesian network framework to formally analyze joint data balancing, defining the balanced distribution Q(X,Y,Z) = P^t(X,Y,Z)·P^t(Y)P^t(Z)/P^t(Y,Z). It derives sufficient conditions (Proposition 4.2, Corollary 4.3) under which training on Q yields a risk-invariant and optimal model, requiring that X_Z^⊥ is a sufficient statistic for Y and is conditionally independent of Z given Y. Crucially, Proposition 5.1 proves that Q need not factorize according to the 'mutilated' graph G⁰ obtained by removing the undesired path, meaning balancing can alter statistical dependencies that were assumed fixed. The paper then shows that this factorization mismatch causes data balancing to interfere with MMD-based regularization: in causal tasks, balancing induces a spurious dependence between X_Z^⊥ and Z that makes the standard regularizer destroy useful signal, while in anti-causal tasks with additional confounders, regularization can force the model to rely solely on the confounder. A diagnostic procedure is provided to distinguish between failure modes (additional confounder, entangled data, poor representation) even without a full causal graph.

## Results

On CelebA (blond hair detection), data balancing (ERM on Q) improves worst-group accuracy from 0.314 to 0.674 and reduces equalized odds from 0.243 to 0.125 compared to ERM on the original confounded distribution, but a substantial gap remains between overall accuracy (0.839) and worst-group accuracy (0.674), confirming that balancing alone does not yield a risk-invariant model. On semi-synthetic MNIST tasks, joint balancing succeeds for purely spurious correlations (worst-group 0.836, equalized odds 0.018) but fails for entangled data (worst-group 0.000) and additional confounders (worst-group 0.555). The paper further shows that combining data balancing with MMD regularization can be counterproductive: in causal tasks, regularization on balanced data degrades risk-invariance relative to regularization on the original distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Veitch et al. [74] (MMD regularization for counterfactual invariance), the paper demonstrates a critical interaction: MMD on the original distribution P^t achieves near-perfect equalized odds (0.001) on CelebA but catastrophically low worst-group accuracy (0.146), while data balancing alone gives worst-group 0.674 with equalized odds 0.125. More importantly, the paper proves (Proposition 5.1) that data balancing does not correspond to removing edges in the causal graph, and empirically shows that applying MMD regularization on balanced data in causal tasks actually degrades risk-invariance compared to applying it on P^t (Figure 3a vs 3b). Against Kirichenko et al. [41] (last-layer retraining / pre-training for robustness), pre-training the VGG on ImageNet before fine-tuning on Q yields the best overall accuracy (0.874) and worst-group accuracy (0.726), but equalized odds remain non-trivial (0.111), indicating that improved representations help but do not fully eliminate the failure. The paper's key contribution over both baselines is the causal diagnostic framework: it identifies *why* each mitigation succeeds or fails based on the underlying causal graph structure, rather than treating them as interchangeable tools.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal framework of Makar et al. [48], which analyzed data balancing under a single anti-causal graph, to a family of causal Bayesian network structures (causal, anti-causal, with additional confounders, and with entangled signals). It builds on the regularization approach of Veitch et al. [74] and the entangled-signal analysis of Puli et al. [58], but departs from the common assumption in prior work that joint balancing is equivalent to 'dropping' undesired edges from the causal graph. The key conceptual departure is proving (Proposition 5.1) that the balanced distribution Q need not factorize according to the mutilated graph, which has downstream consequences for combining balancing with other mitigation strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Data balancing is widely deployed as a default pre-processing step for fairness and robustness, yet this paper shows it can be actively harmful when combined with regularization in causal tasks, and that its success depends on structural conditions (sufficient statistic, conditional independence) that are often violated in practice. The diagnostic framework (Section 6) offers practitioners a way to identify which condition is failing even without a full causal graph, though the framework's utility is limited by the need for domain knowledge about the data-generating process. Open questions include whether dynamic in-processing resampling avoids the identified failure modes, how conditions generalize beyond binary variables and correlation-shift families, and whether the causal-graph-based guidance is actionable in settings where the graph is unknown or only partially known.
