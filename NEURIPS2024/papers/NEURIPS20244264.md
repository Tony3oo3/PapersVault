## Problem

Self-supervised learning methods like CLIP, DINO, and SwaV employ data balancing (rescaling minibatch distributions to match target marginals) as a practical training step, yet the theoretical reason this intervention helps remains unclear. The paper asks: if the target marginals accurately represent the true data distribution, do balanced estimators have provably better statistical properties than their unbalanced counterparts, and if so, by how much?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244264/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a minibatch of n data points (images, image-caption pairs, or text-keyword pairs) along with fixed target marginal distributions P_X and P_Y (typically uniform over the support), and produces a balanced joint probability measure P_n^(k) after k alternating marginal-rescaling iterations. This balanced measure replaces the empirical distribution in the loss computation, so the gradient update is taken with respect to E_{P_n^(k)}[h_θ(X,Y)] rather than the raw minibatch average. In experiments, the trained model outputs zero-shot classification scores via cosine similarity between image and text embeddings on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and STL-10.

## Method

The paper formalizes data balancing as alternating KL-projections onto marginal constraints (equivalently, Sinkhorn-Knopp matrix scaling) and proves that the variance reduction from each nonlinear iteration corresponds to a linear orthogonal projection in L²(P) governed by conditional-mean Markov operators μ_X and μ_Y. The key technical result (Theorem 1) is a non-asymptotic MSE bound for the k-step balanced estimator that decomposes into an O(n^{-1}) first-order variance term σ_k²/n and an O(k⁶ n^{-3/2}) higher-order remainder, where σ_k² = Var(C₁…C_k h) and C_X, C_Y are the conditional debiasing operators. The variance gap σ₀² − σ_k² is shown to converge geometrically to a limit determined by the singular values {s_j} of the Markov operators (Proposition 3), with rate governed by the spectral gap s₂ < s₁. This yields a concrete, computable quantity for the statistical efficiency gain of balancing, improving on prior work (Albertus & Berthet, 2019) by replacing an exponential-in-k dependence with a polynomial one. The paper further shows that the CLIP loss is a nonlinear function of the balanced measure whose gradient has a linear form, unifying contrastive learning and self-supervised clustering under the same balancing framework.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is a non-asymptotic MSE bound for the balanced estimator: E[(φₙ⁽ᵏ⁾ − φ)²] ≤ (σ₀² − σ_gap²)/n + O(sᵏ/n) + Õ(k⁶/n^{3/2}), where σ_gap² > 0 quantifies the variance reduction and s = s₂ < 1 is the second singular value of the conditional mean operator. Empirically, on zero-shot image classification (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, STL-10), one iteration of balancing already improves average per-class recall over the standard CLIP objective, and multiple iterations yield further gains across all three text encoder backbones (GPT-2, BERT, CLIP) and both batch sizes (m=128, m=512). In the metadata curation experiment on ImageNet-Captions, balancing the keyword marginal reduces the long-tail distribution to less than 2 orders of magnitude spread and yields downstream improvements in the smaller batch regime (m=512).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods in a head-to-head table; instead, the primary comparison is between the standard (unbalanced) CLIP objective and its balanced variants. Across all three text encoder qualities (GPT-2, BERT, CLIP embeddings) and both batch sizes, one iteration of Sinkhorn balancing consistently improves zero-shot average per-class recall over the unbalanced CLIP loss, with additional iterations providing further gains (Fig. 3). In the metadata curation setting (Fig. 4), resampling the ImageNet-Captions dataset to balance the keyword marginal outperforms the original long-tailed distribution, particularly at batch size m=512. The theoretical comparison is against the direct empirical estimator φₙ⁽⁰⁾, whose MSE equals σ₀²/n; the balanced estimator achieves a strictly smaller first-order variance term (σ₀² − σ_gap²)/n whenever μ_X h̄ is not constant, with the gap determined by the spectral decay of the Markov operators μ_X and μ_Y.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the classical statistical literature on raking-ratio estimation and iterative proportional fitting (Sinkhorn-Knopp, 1967; Bickel et al., 1991; Albertus & Berthet, 2019) with modern self-supervised learning practices in CLIP, SwaV, and DINO, showing that the balancing steps embedded in these architectures are instances of the same alternating marginal-matching procedure. The key departure from prior work is the identification of variance reduction as the statistical benefit of balancing, which was previously understood only as an optimization device to prevent representation collapse. The non-asymptotic MSE bound improves on Albertus and Berthet (2019) by replacing an exponential-in-k dependence with a polynomial one, and the spectral characterization via Markov operator singular values provides a new analytical tool not available in the classical asymptotic treatments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical justification for a widely-used but conceptually opaque practice in foundation model training, reframing balancing from a heuristic optimization trick to a principled variance-reduction mechanism. The spectral gap condition gives a concrete, checkable criterion for when balancing will help, and the connection to Markov operator eigenvalue decay opens a path to designing data-aware balancing schedules. Open questions include the behavior under misspecified marginals (partially addressed in the appendix), extension to continuous sample spaces, and whether the variance reduction perspective can guide the choice of target marginals beyond uniformity. The empirical improvements, while consistent, are modest, suggesting the primary value is conceptual clarity rather than immediate performance gains.
