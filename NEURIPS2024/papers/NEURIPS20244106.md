## Problem

Detecting whether a distribution shift in production is harmful to a deployed model's error typically requires ground truth labels, which are unavailable in many real-world settings (e.g., credit scoring, medical diagnosis). Existing label-free approaches rely on disparate heuristics without theoretical guarantees, and batch-based shift detectors are ill-suited to sequential data streams where shifts may occur gradually and early detection is critical.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a stream of unlabeled production observations arriving sequentially, a pre-trained error estimator (a secondary supervised model predicting the primary model's error), and a labeled calibration set used to set an error threshold. It outputs a binary alarm signal at each time step indicating whether a harmful shift has been detected, along with the detection time. The key constraint is that production observations arrive one at a time without labels, so the method must make decisions incrementally using only the error estimator's predictions on each new observation.

## Method

The approach trains a secondary error estimator to predict the primary model's error, then calibrates a threshold on estimated errors by grid-searching quantile pairs to maximize selector power while keeping the false discovery proportion below a target. During production, it sequentially tracks the proportion of observations whose estimated error exceeds this threshold using a predictably-mixed empirical-Bernstein confidence sequence, and raises an alarm when the time-uniform lower bound of this proportion exceeds a fixed-time Hoeffding upper bound on the source proportion plus a tolerance. The key insight is that tracking a quantile (proportion above threshold) rather than the mean error is far more robust to imperfect error estimators, since the estimator only needs to preserve ordinal relationships between low- and high-error observations. Under a mild assumption that the false-discovery rate does not increase in production, the method provably controls the false alarm rate at α_source + α_prod across all time steps.

## Results

On natural distribution shifts (Folktables, 250 production datasets across 50 US states over 5 years), the proposed quantile detector achieves 0.48 power with 0.019 FDP and a mean detection time of 3727, compared to 0.01 power, 0.19 FDP, and detection time 4945 for the plug-in mean detector. On synthetic shifts across four tabular datasets (California Housing, Bike Sharing, HELOC, NHANES), the quantile detector achieves an aggregate power of 0.83 with FDP 0.11, versus 0.67 power and 0.41 FDP for the mean detector. In a batch-setting comparison against Detectron on NHANES at OOD size 8593, the proposed method achieves 0.74 power with 0.04 FDP, while Detectron reaches 0.98 power but with 0.60 FDP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the plug-in mean detector (Podkopaev and Ramdas [2022] with estimated errors), the proposed quantile detector dominates across all settings: on synthetic tabular shifts it achieves 0.83 power vs 0.67 with a 4× lower FDP (0.11 vs 0.41), and on natural Folktables shifts it achieves 0.48 power vs 0.01 with a 10× lower FDP (0.019 vs 0.19) and ~1200 steps earlier detection. The quantile detector also detects more harmful shifts in absolute terms (e.g., 48/62 on California vs 10/62; 961/2129 on Bike Sharing vs 57/2129). Against Detectron (Ginsberg et al., 2022) in a batch setting on NHANES, the proposed method trades some power (0.74 vs 0.98 at OOD size 8593) for dramatically better false alarm control (FDP 0.04 vs 0.60), and at smaller OOD sizes (100, 1000) Detectron produces 100% false alarms while the proposed method produces none. The paper also notes that Detectron's disagreement-based approach can produce false alarms even under benign shifts (illustrated in Figure 12), a failure mode not shared by the quantile detector.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Podkopaev and Ramdas (2022), who provided a sequential harmful shift detection framework with false alarm control but required ground truth labels in production. The key methodological departure is replacing the tracked mean error with a calibrated quantile-based proportion of high-error observations, using a secondary error estimator as a proxy. The calibration step—sweeping over quantile pairs to balance selector power against false discovery proportion—is novel and enables the use of even highly inaccurate estimators (R² < 0.3). The sequential testing machinery (confidence sequences, Hoeffding bounds) is inherited from the broader anytime-valid inference literature (Howard et al., Waudby-Smith and Ramdas), while the batch-setting comparison to Detectron (Ginsberg et al.) situates the work relative to disagreement-based shift detectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: most production ML deployments (medical diagnosis, credit scoring, fraud detection) lack immediate label access, making prior label-dependent methods inapplicable. The finding that quantile-based detection is far more robust to estimator inaccuracy than mean-based detection (power 0.83 vs 0.67, FDP 0.11 vs 0.41 across tabular datasets) provides a clear design principle for practitioners. Key limitations include Assumption 4.1 (that false-discovery probability in production does not exceed the source), which holds approximately but not exactly, and the method's reliance on a secondary model that must be retrained if the primary model changes. Open questions include interpretability of where in input space the shift occurs and whether the approach extends to multi-task or multi-model monitoring.
