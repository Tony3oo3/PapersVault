## Problem

Existing NAS benchmarks target convolutional architectures and image classification, leaving a gap for hardware-aware evaluation of decoder-only transformer language models across diverse devices. Moreover, prior hardware-aware benchmarks treat latency and energy as single-point measurements, ignoring the substantial heteroscedastic noise inherent in on-device profiling that can distort Pareto fronts and optimization trajectories. The computational cost of training even a single LM from scratch (millions of GPU-hours) makes exhaustive multi-objective NAS impractical without efficient surrogates.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes an architecture configuration encoded as a list of integers (embedding dimension, number of layers, per-layer head count, per-layer MLP ratio, and bias flag) along with a target device identifier (one of 7 GPUs or 5 CPUs) and a metric selection. Outputs include calibrated mean-and-std predictions for latency and energy (capturing the noisy measurement distribution), a perplexity estimate from either the supernetwork or an MLP predictor, and Pareto-optimal architecture sets summarized via hypervolume and the Empirical Attainment Function. The search space spans cardinalities from ~10^12 to ~10^48, but surrogates are trained on 10,000 uniformly sampled architectures per space, enabling queries in seconds on a single CPU.

## Method

HW-GPT-Bench constructs seven GPT-2-based supernetworks (124M–1.55B parameters) with RoPE positional embeddings and parallel residual connections, trained via the sandwich scheme (sampling largest, smallest, and two random sub-networks per batch) on OpenWebText. Perplexity is obtained by inheriting weights from the supernetwork for each sampled architecture, and an MLP surrogate (4 hidden layers, dim 128) is trained on 80% of the 10k evaluations to predict perplexity for unseen architectures. For latency and energy, the key distinction is that AutoGluon stacking ensembles are trained to predict both the first and second moments of the measurement distribution (up to 10 latency and 50 energy observations per architecture per device), yielding calibrated uncertainty estimates rather than point predictions. The benchmark then evaluates eight multi-objective NAS algorithms (NSGA-II, EHVI, MOREA, MOASHA, RSBO, LSBO, Local Search, Random Search) under a fixed 16-CPU-hour budget, aggregating results across 4 repeated runs via the Empirical Attainment Function to account for prediction noise.

## Results

For surrogate model comparison (Table 2), AutoGluon achieves the best accuracy across all metrics: MAE of 0.153 (H100 latency) and 1.80 (RTX 2080 energy), with R² of 0.999 and 0.904 respectively, and the lowest calibration error (MA Cal. 0.122 and 0.244). In the multi-objective NAS algorithm evaluation, EHVI and NSGA-II achieve the highest hypervolume under small evaluation budgets (200 surrogate evaluations), outperforming Local Search and Random Search. In the 3-objective setting (perplexity + RTX 2080Ti latency + A6000 energy), NSGA-II maintains strong trade-offs while Bayesian optimization methods (RSBO, LSBO, EHVI) underperform even Random Search and Local Search.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoGluon dominates all ensemble baselines (XGBoost, LightGBM, and mixed sklearn ensembles) on both accuracy and calibration: it achieves 3.7× lower MAE than Ensemble(XGB) on H100 latency (0.153 vs. 0.620) and 3.9× lower MA Cal. (0.122 vs. 0.481), while maintaining comparable R² (>0.99). For MO-NAS algorithms, NSGA-II and EHVI outperform Random Search and Local Search on hypervolume under tight budgets (200 evaluations), with EHVI showing the strongest early convergence. However, in the 3-objective setting, the advantage reverses: NSGA-II remains competitive while Bayesian optimization methods (RSBO, LSBO, EHVI) fall below even Random Search and Local Search, suggesting that model-based methods struggle with higher-dimensional objective spaces. MOREA and MOASHA show intermediate performance, consistently between the evolutionary and random baselines across devices and metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the surrogate NAS benchmark paradigm (Zela et al., 2022) and one-shot weight-sharing techniques (Han et al., 2020; Klein et al., 2024) to the specific domain of causal language modeling with decoder-only transformers. While prior hardware-aware NAS benchmarks (HW-NAS-Bench, HAT, EA-HAS-Bench) targeted CNNs, machine translation, or speech recognition, HW-GPT-Bench is the first to provide a calibrated surrogate benchmark for GPT-2-family architectures across 13 heterogeneous devices and 5 hardware metrics. The sandwich-scheme supernetwork training from Klein et al. (2024) is adopted directly, and AutoGluon is introduced as a surrogate for latency/energy prediction with explicit heteroscedastic uncertainty modeling, departing from prior benchmarks that relied on single-point measurements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HW-GPT-Bench provides a practical, low-cost testbed for evaluating multi-objective NAS algorithms on language models without the prohibitive cost of training thousands of architectures from scratch, enabling rapid iteration on MO-NAS methods. The calibrated uncertainty modeling for latency and energy is a methodological contribution that prior benchmarks lacked, and the finding that measurement noise can shift Pareto fronts underscores the importance of uncertainty-aware optimization. Limitations include restriction to the GPT-2 family (up to 1.55B parameters), a single training corpus (OpenWebText), and a relatively narrow architectural search space, leaving open whether the surrogate approach and architectural importance rankings transfer to larger or architecturally different LMs (e.g., LLaMA, Mistral).
