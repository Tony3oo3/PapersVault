## Problem

Existing object hallucination evaluation methods for LVLMs average CHAIR scores across a set of instructions, but different instructions produce descriptions of vastly different lengths, and hallucination rate is strongly correlated with description length. This causes the average-based framework to yield inconsistent evaluations for the same model under different instruction sets and unfair comparisons between models that naturally generate different-length descriptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243537/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an image, a set of N instructions (all prompting the LVLM to describe the image), and an LVLM as input. The LVLM generates one description per instruction using beam search (beam size 5), and CHAIR_I/CHAIR_S scores are computed against ground-truth objects. The output is a pair of metrics: LeHaCE(ℓ), the predicted hallucination rate at a user-specified description length ℓ (evaluated at 20, 40, 60, and 80 words), and LeHaCE_GR, the slope β quantifying how rapidly hallucination grows per additional word of description.

## Method

The core insight is that instructions affect hallucination only indirectly through their effect on description length, and that hallucination rate and description length exhibit a clear positive linear correlation (Pearson r > 0.6 for all 12 LVLMs tested). LeHaCE fits a linear regression — the Length-Hallucination Curve (LHC) — of the form LHC(ℓ) = β·ℓ + α over the (length, hallucination rate) pairs collected from N instructions. Evaluating LHC at a uniform target length ℓ yields a length-normalized hallucination score that is stable across instruction sets and fair across models, while the slope β serves as a second metric capturing the model's susceptibility to length-induced hallucination. The framework is metric-agnostic (built on CHAIR here) and requires only 4–5 instructions to achieve stable estimates, as verified by Relative Standard Deviation experiments showing LeHaCE consistently outperforms the average-based framework.

## Results

On MSCOCO, InstructBLIP achieves the lowest CHAIR_I at 20 words (2.35), while Gemini-Pro-Vision achieves the lowest at 40–80 words (5.22, 6.15, 7.07) and the lowest growth rate (0.05). On NoCaps, Qwen-VL achieves the best CHAIR_I at 40–80 words (10.69, 13.01, 15.33) and LLaVA achieves the lowest growth rate (0.09). The LeHaCE framework demonstrates superior stability over the average-based framework: with ≥5 instructions on MSCOCO, LeHaCE consistently yields lower RSD across all 12 LVLMs for both CHAIR_I and CHAIR_S. Disabling hallucinogenic words (e.g., 'in addition', 'including', 'such as') reduces average CHAIR_I from 8.86 to 7.86 on MSCOCO and from 15.44 to 14.73 on NoCaps without degrading CLIPScore or retrieval quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an evaluation-methodology contribution rather than a model comparison, so the primary 'baseline' is the average-based hallucination evaluation framework (ABF) that simply averages CHAIR scores across instructions. LeHaCE consistently outperforms ABF in stability: on MSCOCO with ≥5 instructions, LeHaCE achieves lower RSD than ABF for all 12 LVLMs on both CHAIR_I and CHAIR_S (e.g., for InstructBLIP CHAIR_I, RSD drops from 0.43 to 0.15 at 5 instructions); on NoCaps with ≥4 instructions, LeHaCE is superior across all models and metrics. The paper also validates its linear fitting assumption by showing that linear fitting significantly outperforms quadratic and cubic polynomial fitting, especially at low instruction counts (e.g., for VPGTrans CHAIR_I at 3 instructions, linear RSD is 0.17 vs. 16.25 for quadratic). Among the 12 evaluated LVLMs, InstructBLIP leads on short descriptions (CHAIR_I at 20 words: 2.35 on MSCOCO, 6.52 on NoCaps) but its high growth rate (0.14/0.18) causes it to fall behind Gemini-Pro-Vision (MSCOCO) and Qwen-VL (NoCaps) on longer descriptions. Disabling hallucinogenic words during generation reduces average CHAIR_I by ~1 point on both datasets without harming CLIPScore or T2I-CLIP retrieval, confirming that enumeration-triggered hallucination is a major driver of the length-hallucination correlation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the CHAIR metric (Rohrbach et al., 2018) as its underlying hallucination measurement, wrapping it in a new length-normalized evaluation framework. It departs from the average-based evaluation paradigm adopted by POPE (Li et al., 2023) and other prior methods, which simply average hallucination scores across a set of instructions. The key conceptual departure is the finding that instructions affect hallucination only indirectly through their influence on description length, motivating the use of a fitted length-hallucination curve (linear regression) to evaluate at a controlled length rather than averaging. The identification of 'hallucinogenic words' (enumeration phrases like 'in addition,' 'such as') as a mechanistic driver of length-dependent hallucination is a novel empirical contribution not present in prior hallucination evaluation or mitigation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LeHaCE framework addresses a practical pain point: inconsistent hallucination scores across instruction sets that generate descriptions of different lengths, which undermines fair model comparison. The identification of hallucinogenic words as a simple, actionable mitigation (disabling them reduces hallucination without hurting quality) offers an immediate practical tool for LVLM developers. Limitations include focus on object hallucination only, evaluation on 256-image subsets, and testing of only one proprietary model. Open questions include whether the linear length-hallucination relationship holds for other hallucination types (attribute, relation) and whether the hallucinogenic-word mechanism generalizes beyond English or beyond beam search decoding.
