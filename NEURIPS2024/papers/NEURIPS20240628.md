## Problem

Quantitative judgment aggregation in social choice has traditionally assumed that judgments are reported by subjective agents, limiting its applicability to ranking prediction where 'judgments' arise from objective processes like race results. This paper bridges social choice and learning-to-rank by showing that relative performance data from past contests can be treated as quantitative relative judgments, and by studying the computational complexity of aggregating them under general loss functions—a question left open by prior work that only considered the linear (ℓ₁) case.

## Input / Output

The input is a set of m weighted quantitative relative judgments, each of the form (a, b, y) stating that candidate a outperforms candidate b by y units, derived from pairwise score differences in past contests. The output is a single-dimensional score vector x ∈ ℝⁿ, where x_i represents candidate i's latent quality; ordinal predictions come from comparing x_i values, and quantitative predictions come from x_a − x_b. A key structural constraint is that the constraint matrix A has exactly two non-zero entries per row (±w_i^{1/p}), which the algorithm exploits to achieve near-linear time.

## Method

The paper formulates QRJA as minimizing Σ w_i·f(|x_{a_i} − x_{b_i} − y_i|) and focuses on the ℓ_p subclass where f(t) = t^p. For p ≥ 1, the key algorithmic insight is to write the problem as an ℓ_p regression min‖Ax − z‖_p, take the Lagrangian dual to obtain a constrained maximization over flows (Aᵀf = 0, ‖f‖_q ≤ 1), and then solve the dual via ℓ_p-norm min-cost flow using the near-linear-time algorithm of Chen et al. (2022); a primal solution is then recovered in O(m) time from the dual's gradient. For p = 1, the problem is cast as a linear program and reduced to min-cost flow with O(n) vertices and O(m) edges. For p < 1, the paper proves NP-hardness (and no FPTAS) via a reduction from Max-Cut that forces integral solutions through carefully chosen weights. Empirically, ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ QRJA are evaluated on seven real-world contest datasets (chess, F1, marathons, Codeforces, cross-tables) against mean/median, Borda, Kemeny-Young, and matrix factorization baselines, with a Lewis-weights-based subsampling algorithm shown to reduce the number of judgments to ~0.4× with minimal performance loss.

## Results

Both ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ QRJA are consistently among the top-performing methods across all seven datasets (Chess, F1, Marathon, Codeforces, Cross-Tables, F1-Full, Codeforces-Core) on both ordinal accuracy and quantitative loss metrics. The paper reports results via figures (Figure 4 for the four main datasets, Figure 7 for three additional) rather than numerical tables, but states that QRJA is 'never significantly worse than the best-performing algorithm on any of the tested datasets.' On F1-Full, where contestant scores have large variance, ℓ₁ QRJA substantially outperforms both ℓ₂ QRJA and Matrix Factorization, demonstrating greater robustness. Subsampling experiments (Algorithm 1) show that reducing the number of judgments to 40% of the original incurs only minor performance loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ QRJA against five baselines: Mean, Median, Borda, Kemeny-Young, and Matrix Factorization (R1 MF, Koren et al. 2009). QRJA wins or ties on most datasets for both ordinal accuracy and quantitative loss, while Mean and Median are competitive only on Marathon and underperform on F1 and Codeforces. Borda and Kemeny-Young (ordinal-only methods) perform well on F1 but fail to leverage quantitative score differences on other datasets. Matrix Factorization (R1 MF) is the closest competitor, achieving performance 'comparable to QRJA' on all four main datasets for both metrics; however, QRJA offers a key interpretability advantage since its single variable per contestant directly represents contestant strength, unlike MF's latent factors. On F1-Full (high-variance scores), ℓ₁ QRJA clearly outperforms both MF and ℓ₂ QRJA, while on the dense Codeforces-Core dataset, all methods perform well and MF loses its typical advantage. Overall, QRJA and MF are the only two methods that are never significantly worse than the best on any dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the quantitative judgment aggregation framework introduced by Conitzer et al. (2015, 2016), which studied only the linear loss f(t)=t, to the general ℓ_p family f(t)=t^p. Algorithmically, it builds on Zhang et al. (2019)'s reduction of ℓ₁ QRJA to minimum-cost flow and leverages the recent almost-linear-time min-cost-flow breakthrough of Chen et al. (2022) to achieve near-linear running time for all p≥1. Conceptually, the paper's key departure is reframing QRJA from a social-choice problem (aggregating subjective human judgments) to a ranking-prediction problem (aggregating objective contest results as 'judgments'), thereby bridging the social choice and learning-to-rank communities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, interpretable alternative to matrix factorization for ranking prediction: each contestant receives a single scalar 'quality' score, making the model transparent in ways that latent-factor models are not. The almost-linear-time algorithm is a significant theoretical contribution, but it relies on highly complex 'galactic' min-cost-flow algorithms, leaving open the practical question of whether simpler near-linear algorithms exist for ℓ_p QRJA with p≠1,2. Empirically, QRJA matches matrix factorization in accuracy across seven real-world datasets, suggesting the interpretability gain comes at no predictive cost. Open questions include extending the framework to handle strategic agents, incorporating temporal dynamics, and developing more practical solvers for the general p case.
