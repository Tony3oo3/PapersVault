## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks for history (e.g., MMLU) test only high-school-level knowledge that is heavily biased toward Western and recent periods, leaving LLMs' expert-level, globally balanced historical understanding unmeasured. The Seshat Global History Databank provides a structured, expert-curated representation of what is known and inferred about 600+ past societies across all world regions, but had never been used to benchmark LLMs. This paper introduces HiST-LLM, a benchmark that tests whether LLMs can distinguish not only factual presence/absence but also whether that knowledge is directly evidenced or inferred from indirect evidence.

## Input / Output

Each question specifies a polity name, a time frame, a variable (e.g., 'writing', 'professional military officers'), and its sub-category, asking the model to select from four options: Present, Absent, Inferred Present, or Inferred Absent. The model is prompted to provide a short evidence summary and reasoning before outputting a single letter (A–D). Performance is reported as 4-choice balanced accuracy (random = 25%) and a 2-choice variant restricted to Present/Absent (random = 50%), each with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, broken down by time bin, UN region, and variable category.

## Method

The benchmark converts 36,000 Seshat data points (383 variables across 600+ polities and 2,700+ scholarly references) into multiple-choice questions, with the critical design choice of including 'inferred present' and 'inferred absent' as distinct answer options to test whether models can distinguish direct evidence from indirect inference. Prompts use personification (instructing the model to act as a history expert), a multi-shot format with four category-specific chain-of-thought examples tailored to each of the 11 variable themes, and temperature 0 with a fixed seed for OpenAI models. Seven models (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o, Llama-3-70B, Llama-3.1-70B FP8, Llama-3.1-8B FP8, Gemini-1.5-flash) are evaluated via API calls that prevent data leakage. Statistical significance of performance differences across time, region, and category is assessed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate standard errors of balanced accuracy.

## Results

On the HiST-LLM benchmark (4-choice format: Present, Absent, Inferred Present, Inferred Absent), balanced accuracy ranges from 33.6% (Llama-3.1-8B) to 46.0% (GPT-4-Turbo), compared to 25% for random guessing. In the simplified 2-choice format (Present vs. Absent only), scores range from 57.6% (Llama-3.1-8B) to 63.2% (GPT-4o), versus 50% random. GPT-4-Turbo achieves the highest adjusted balanced accuracy of 28.0% (4-choice), while GPT-4o leads in 2-choice adjusted balanced accuracy at 26.4%. All models significantly outperform random but fall well short of expert-level comprehension.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks seven LLMs (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-4o, Llama-3-70B, Llama-3.1-70B FP8, Llama-3.1-8B FP8, Gemini-1.5-flash) against each other and against random guessing (25% for 4-choice, 50% for 2-choice). GPT-4-Turbo leads in 4-choice balanced accuracy (46.0%), outperforming GPT-4o (44.7%) by 1.3 points and the next-best Llama-3.1-70B (40.8%) by 5.2 points. In the 2-choice format, GPT-4o achieves the highest score (63.2%), edging out Llama-3-70B and Llama-3.1-70B (both 62.6%) and GPT-4-Turbo (60.0%). Model size matters: GPT-4-based models hold a 10–15% advantage over the smaller GPT-3.5-turbo and Llama-3.1-8B. All models significantly exceed random performance, but none approach expert-level comprehension, with the best adjusted balanced accuracy (28.0% for GPT-4-Turbo, 4-choice) indicating that even the strongest model gets only about 28% of the way from random to perfect. Regionally, GPT-4-Turbo leads in 6 of 8 UN regions, while performance is consistently weakest in Oceania and Sub-Saharan Africa across all models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM knowledge benchmarking tradition established by MMLU (Hendrycks et al.) and domain-specific benchmarks like LegalBench, but departs from them in two key ways: it targets expert/graduate-level historical knowledge rather than high-school-level facts, and it introduces the evidenced-versus-inferred distinction as a first-class evaluation dimension. The underlying Seshat Global History Databank (collected since 2011) had previously been used for cliodynamic hypothesis testing but not for LLM evaluation; this work repurposes its structured, reference-linked data as a benchmark. Unlike prior global history datasets (D-PLACE, Ethnographic Atlas) that focus on small-scale societies and lack temporal dynamics or source attribution, Seshat covers 600+ polities across all UN regions with explicit uncertainty coding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic benchmark of LLMs' expert-level global history knowledge, revealing that even the best model (GPT-4-Turbo) achieves only 46% balanced accuracy in the 4-choice format—far below expert comprehension. The counterintuitive finding that performance is higher for earlier periods (pre-3000 BCE) than recent ones challenges the assumption that better-documented eras yield better model performance, suggesting LLMs struggle with the nuance and contested interpretations characteristic of recent scholarship. The publicly released 36,000-point dataset with scholarly references offers a foundation for training or fine-tuning models on global history, though the English-language source bias and the acknowledged lower-bound nature of the benchmark mean that true global coverage—particularly for Sub-Saharan Africa and Oceania—remains an open challenge requiring collaboration with Global South institutions.
