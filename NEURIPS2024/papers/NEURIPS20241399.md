## Problem

A pervasive claim in the ML literature holds that AUPRC is a superior evaluation metric to AUROC for class-imbalanced binary classification, yet this assertion is rarely supported by rigorous theoretical justification and is often made without citation or misattributed to sources that do not argue this point. This paper demonstrates that the claim is not only unsupported but actively harmful: AUPRC can unduly favor model improvements in higher-prevalence subpopulations, exacerbating algorithmic disparities in multi-group deployment settings.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on continuous probability scores from a binary classifier, binary ground-truth labels, and optional subpopulation attributes (e.g., sex, race). The theoretical results characterize how AUROC and AUPRC decompose over the induced distribution of positive-sample scores and the model's firing rate at each threshold. Empirically, the paper produces per-group AUROC/AUPRC values, signed fairness gaps between subgroups, and Spearman correlations between overall metric values and per-group gaps across 20 random data splits, revealing how metric choice mediates disparity amplification as a function of prevalence ratio.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-theorem theoretical framework. Theorem 1 shows that AUROC and AUPRC differ only in their weighting of false positive rates: AUROC minimizes expected FPR uniformly over positive samples, while AUPRC weights FPR at each score threshold by the inverse of the model's firing rate P(f(x)>τ), making it preferentially optimize high-score regions. Theorem 2 formalizes this via 'incorrectly ranked adjacent pairs' (mistakes), proving that correcting any single mistake improves AUROC by a constant amount but improves AUPRC monotonically with the score at which the mistake occurs. Theorem 3 then shows that under calibrated models with sufficiently disparate subgroup prevalences, the mistake whose correction maximally improves AUPRC will almost surely lie entirely within the higher-prevalence subgroup. Empirical validation uses two synthetic optimization procedures (greedy mistake-fixing and constrained random score permutations) and real-world XGBoost experiments on four fairness datasets (Adult, COMPAS, LSAC, MIMIC-III) with per-group weighting grids and 50-run random hyperparameter searches, confirming that AUPRC selection correlates more strongly with per-group AUROC gaps as prevalence disparity increases.

## Results

In synthetic optimization experiments (n₁=n₂=200, 50 steps; and n₁=n₂=100, 25 steps with random permutations), optimizing by AUPRC produces a clear per-group AUROC divergence favoring the high-prevalence subgroup (P(y=1|a=1)=0.05 vs. P(y=1|a=2)=0.01), while AUROC optimization improves both groups together. On four real-world fairness datasets (Adult, COMPAS, LSAC, MIMIC-III) with sex and race as sensitive attributes, the difference in Spearman's ρ between (AUROC gap vs. overall AUPRC) and (AUROC gap vs. overall AUROC) is positive and grows with prevalence ratio, with a statistically significant correlation between prevalence gap and AUPRC bias amplification (Spearman's ρ = 0.905, p = 0.002). A literature review of over 1.5 million arXiv papers identified 424 publications asserting that AUPRC is superior to AUROC under class imbalance, of which 167 provided no citation and 135 cited sources that do not actually make this claim.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it compares two evaluation metrics—AUROC and AUPRC—as criteria for model selection and optimization. The central finding is that AUPRC is not universally superior under class imbalance: it preferentially optimizes high-score regions and, critically, unduly favors higher-prevalence subpopulations (Theorem 3), whereas AUROC treats all mistakes equally across the score spectrum. In the real-world XGBoost experiments across Adult, COMPAS, LSAC, and MIMIC-III, selecting models by overall AUPRC yields a larger signed AUROC gap between high- and low-prevalence groups than selecting by overall AUROC, with the effect strengthening as the prevalence ratio increases. The paper concludes that AUROC is the more appropriate default metric for context-agnostic model comparison, high-false-negative-cost screening, and equitable multi-population resource allocation, while AUPRC is justified only in specific settings where high-score false positives are disproportionately costly and no fairness constraints apply (e.g., small-molecule screening).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first rigorous theoretical characterization of how AUROC and AUPRC differ in their treatment of model mistakes, rather than building on a specific prior method. It engages critically with the foundational Davis & Goadrich (2006) analysis of the PR-ROC relationship, arguing that the common interpretation—that AUPRC is universally superior under class imbalance—misreads the original contribution, which concerned curve informativeness rather than area-based model comparison. The fairness analysis (Theorem 3) connects the metric-selection literature to the algorithmic fairness literature by showing that a widely recommended evaluation choice can systematically amplify subpopulation disparities, a link not previously formalized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a claim so deeply embedded in ML practice (textbooks, LLM assistants, 424+ papers) that its refutation has immediate practical consequences for model selection in healthcare, drug discovery, and fairness-critical deployments. The literature review revealing that 167 of 424 papers made the claim without citation and 135 cited sources that never actually argued it underscores a systemic attribution problem in the field. Open questions include extending the analysis beyond two subpopulations, relaxing the perfect-calibration assumption in Theorem 3, and examining whether post-hoc calibration of AUPRC-optimized models mitigates the identified disparities. The practical guidance in Section 4 (when to use which metric) is likely to influence future evaluation protocols, though the paper's focus on binary classification and tabular data limits immediate generalization to multi-class or continuous-output settings.
