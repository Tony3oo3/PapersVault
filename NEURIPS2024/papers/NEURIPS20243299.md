## Problem

Sparse Mixture of Experts architectures scale parameter counts efficiently but are discontinuous due to top-k routing, causing training instability and requiring auxiliary techniques like load-balancing losses. Existing continuous MoE alternatives either cannot scale to large expert counts or are incompatible with autoregressive decoding. This paper asks whether a continuous MoE design can simultaneously match sparse MoE in parameter scaling, training stability, and autoregressive compatibility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243299/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a batch of text sequences (C4, GPT-2 tokenizer, context length 256, batch size 256) and produces standard next-token predictions. A key operational constraint is that tokens are grouped across different sequences in the batch by their position within each sequence, so the architecture inherently requires batched inference and cannot perform unbatched generation natively. The authors address this limitation via transition tuning, which converts a pretrained MoT model into a standard Token Choice (sparse MoE) model for unbatched deployment.

## Method

Mixture of Tokens (MoT) replaces the second half of Transformer FFN layers with a continuous expert layer that processes a weighted mixture of tokens from different sequences rather than individual tokens. For each expert, a softmax over the tokens in a position-aligned group yields mixing weights; the expert receives the weighted sum of those tokens as its input, and each token's residual update is a weighted combination of all expert outputs using its own mixing weights as coefficients. Because the number of experts equals the group size, total FLOPs per token remain constant while parameter count scales with the number of experts, mirroring sparse MoE. The design requires no top-k operation, no load-balancing losses, and no token dropping, and it is fully compatible with autoregressive training and generation. A further scaling axis is introduced by feeding multiple smaller mixtures per expert (more experts with proportionally reduced hidden dimension), and a transition tuning procedure allows a pretrained MoT model to initialize a sparse Token Choice model that recovers performance in 10% of the original pretraining steps.

## Results

On C4 language modeling, the best MoT model (MoT-Medium/32E/16) reaches the final loss of the dense Transformer baseline in only 33% of the training steps, representing a 3× compute speedup. In bfloat16-only training, MoT-Medium/32E achieves a final loss of 3.661 (±0.007) versus 3.728 (±0.044) for Expert Choice-Medium/32E, demonstrating superior low-precision stability. On zero-shot downstream tasks, MoT-Medium/32E/16 outperforms Transformer-Medium on PIQA (65.8 vs. 60.2), HellaSwag (33.3 vs. 27.3), and ARC-e (39.6 vs. 35.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the dense GPT-2 Transformer baseline [Radford 2019], MoT achieves a 3× training speedup (reaching the same final loss in one-third of the steps) and improves zero-shot accuracy on all three downstream benchmarks by 2.2–6.0 points. Against Expert Choice [Zhou 2022], MoT is slightly worse in mixed-precision training (3.442 vs. 3.420 final loss) but clearly more stable under bfloat16-only training (3.661 vs. 3.728), with a much tighter variance (±0.007 vs. ±0.044). Against Token Choice / Switch Transformers [Fedus 2022], MoT with an increased number of smaller experts (e.g., 32E/16) matches the perplexity of the best sparse MoE configuration (Figure 6), while avoiding the top-k routing operation entirely. Overall, MoT trades a small perplexity gap in mixed-precision settings for substantially better low-precision robustness and simpler training (no load-balancing losses or token dropping).

## Relation to Prior Work

MoT positions itself as the natural 'soft' counterpart to sparse Mixture of Experts, drawing an explicit analogy to the historical shift from hard to soft attention in Transformers. It builds directly on the Switch Transformer (top-1 routing) and sparsely-gated MoE lineage but replaces the discrete top-k expert selection with a continuous mechanism: each expert receives a weighted mixture of tokens drawn from *different* examples in the batch, rather than a single token. This distinguishes it from prior continuous MoE work (e.g., Puigcerver et al. 2023, which mixes patches within a single image, or Hazimeh et al. 2021, which uses differentiable top-k), as cross-example aggregation is what enables autoregressive compatibility without information leakage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3× training speedup over dense Transformers while matching sparse MoE perplexity is practically significant, and the demonstrated robustness to low-precision training addresses a known pain point in large MoE training. However, all experiments are conducted at 77M–162M parameter scale, leaving open whether the stability advantages and scaling behavior hold at the multi-billion-parameter regime where MoE instabilities are most acute. The requirement for batched inference (mitigated by transition tuning) and the substantial memory footprint of MoT layers are practical constraints that may limit adoption. Future work on scaling to larger models, distillation, and other modalities would strengthen the case for this architecture.
