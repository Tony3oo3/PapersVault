## Problem

Existing human-AI collaborative agents (BCP, FCP, SP) are single stationary policy networks that cannot adequately track the non-stationary dynamics of human partners who switch strategies, vary in initiative, or change collaboration styles mid-episode. The paper identifies that while human behavior is non-stationary, the underlying meta-tasks within a collaborative context (e.g., 'place onions in pot,' 'deliver soup') remain relatively stable, yet no prior method exploits this structure by maintaining and selecting among multiple specialized policies.

## Input / Output

CBPR consumes a sliding window of the most recent l human state-action tuples (maintained in a FIFO queue) plus the episodic return at episode boundaries to update two complementary beliefs. The 96-dimensional observation vector encodes each player's position, facing direction, and relative distances to game objects. The output is a discrete policy selection from a pre-trained library of meta-task-playing (MTP) agents, which then generates the AI's action at each timestep; the selected policy can change every timestep as the integrated belief shifts.

## Method

CBPR extends Bayesian Policy Reuse to human-AI collaboration by replacing the single adaptive policy with a library of stationary MTP agents, each trained via PPO to optimally cooperate with one specific rule-based meta-task. During online play, an intra-episode belief (updated from the recent human action queue via likelihood ratios over meta-task models) is fused with an inter-episode belief (updated from episodic returns via Bayes' rule) using a time-decaying weight ρ^t, so that in long episodes the system relies primarily on recent behavioral evidence. The optimal MTP policy is then selected by maximizing the probability of exceeding average utility under the integrated belief. The framework is formalized as a Non-Stationary MDP decomposed into stationary sub-MDPs (one per meta-task), with theorems guaranteeing almost-sure convergence of the response policy and asymptotic optimality relative to the true best response.

## Results

In the Overcooked benchmark, CBPR consistently achieves higher mean episodic rewards than all three baselines (BCP, FCP, SP) across four game layouts under dynamic policy switching, varied partner skill levels, and real-human collaboration. In the skill-level experiments (Figure 4), CBPR's advantage is most pronounced with lower-skilled partners, where it substantially outperforms FCP and SP. In the user study with 25 real human participants (Figure 5), CBPR yields significantly higher rewards than baselines in most layout comparisons (one-sided Mann-Whitney U test, p < 0.05 to p < 0.001). The ablation in Table 1 confirms that including an 'others' meta-task category is critical: without it, CBPR's mean reward for low-skill partners collapses from 689.8 to 40.0 in the Soup Coordination layout.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CBPR outperforms all three baselines across the board. Against BCP (Carroll et al., 2019), which relies on a stationary human model trained via behavioral cloning, CBPR shows significantly higher episodic rewards in all four layouts; BCP is particularly weak in non-separated layouts (Cramped Room, Coordination Ring) where it occasionally becomes immobilized. Against FCP (Strouse et al., 2021), which trains against a diverse policy pool but remains a single stationary policy, CBPR achieves consistently higher mean rewards, especially with lower-skilled partners, while FCP exhibits greater reward fluctuations. Against SP (Silver et al., 2017), which has an inherent training advantage in the skill-level evaluation (its checkpoints are the very partners used for evaluation), CBPR still wins with lower-skilled partners; SP sometimes opts not to cooperate, yielding zero reward. In the real-human study, CBPR significantly outperforms both FCP and SP, which lose their inherent advantage when facing genuine human non-stationarity.

## Relation to Prior Work

CBPR extends Bayesian Policy Reuse (BPR) from Rosman et al. [2016]—originally designed for multi-task transfer and competitive settings—into the human-AI collaborative domain, incorporating the intra-episode belief mechanism from Chen et al. [2022] to track policy switches within a single episode. It departs fundamentally from prior human-AI collaboration methods (BCP, FCP, SP, HSP, MEP), which all converge to a single stationary policy network, by instead maintaining a library of meta-task-specific policies and using Bayesian inference to select among them at each timestep. The key conceptual shift is from modeling the non-stationary human directly to identifying the discrete meta-tasks that underpin human decision-making and matching them one-to-one with specialized agents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled alternative to the dominant 'train one adaptive policy against a diverse pool' paradigm, arguing that multiple specialized agents outperform a single generalist when facing non-stationary partners—a framing that could influence how collaborative agents are architected more broadly. However, the approach's reliance on manually designed rule-based meta-tasks limits its applicability to domains where such decomposition is feasible (e.g., power grid dispatching, autonomous driving), and the paper itself identifies automatic meta-task discovery as a critical open problem. The theoretical guarantees (convergence and optimality under NS-MDP) add rigor, though the optimality proof contains an acknowledged gap and relies on the assumption that the policy library is exhaustive.
