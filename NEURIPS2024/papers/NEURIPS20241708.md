## Problem

Existing LLM alignment evaluations rely on NLP benchmarks that do not capture how emotions influence decision-making, yet human choices in ethical and strategic contexts are well-documented to be heavily emotion-driven. No prior work has systematically examined how emotional states alter LLMs' ethical judgments or strategic behavior in game-theoretical settings, nor whether LLMs' emotional responses align with those of humans.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241708/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes an LLM (temperature fixed at 0) and injects a system prompt combining a game description (environment, rules, co-player framing) with one of five Ekman basic emotions delivered via three prompting strategies: simple (emotion only), co-player-based (emotion attributed to the opponent), and external-based (emotion from an external source). Outputs are structured per game type—binary ethical labels, percentage budget splits, accept/reject decisions, cooperate/defect actions, or token contribution amounts—and are scored against human behavioral benchmarks (e.g., 28.35% mean Dictator offer, 41% mean Ultimatum offer) using relative directional changes rather than absolute values, enabling cross-model and cross-language comparison.

## Method

The EAI framework is a prompt-chaining evaluation harness with three modular components: (1) Game Description providing minimal context and rules without personality traits to isolate emotional effects; (2) Emotion Prompting that prepends a predefined emotional state to the system prompt using one of three causal-attribution strategies; and (3) Game-Specific Pipelines that govern one-shot ethical/bargaining rounds or multi-round repeated games with iterative memory updates (opponent's last move, accumulated rewards, and the LLM's self-reported internal emotion each round). The framework is instantiated across five game types (ETHICS classification, MoralChoice, StereoSet, Dictator/Ultimatum, Prisoner's Dilemma, Battle of the Sexes, Public Goods) and evaluated on ten LLMs spanning proprietary (GPT-3.5/4/4o, Claude 3) and open-source (LLaMA-2, Mixtral, OpenChat, GigaChat, Command R+) models in five languages. The key analytical contribution is a game-theoretic alignment metric that compares the *direction and magnitude* of LLM behavioral shifts under each emotion against established human experimental results, revealing that larger, more aligned models (GPT-4) deviate most from human emotional responses while mid-sized models (GPT-3.5, LLaMA-70B) show closer alignment, and that primary pretraining language significantly modulates emotional comprehension.

## Results

In the Dictator Game (English), GPT-3.5 offers 33% (vs. human 28%) and GPT-4 offers 50%, while in the Ultimatum Proposer role GPT-3.5 offers 35% (vs. human 41%) and GPT-4 offers 48%. Acceptance rates as Responder range from 23% (LLaMA-2 70B) to 82% (OpenChat-7b), with humans showing no single benchmark. In ethics benchmarks, GPT-4 is least affected by emotional prompting (slight overall increase in implicit ethics accuracy), while LLaMA-2 models show the largest degradation under 'anger' and 'fear'. In repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, 'anger' and 'fear' increase deflection rates (especially for larger models), while 'happiness' consistently increases cooperation, aligning with human experimental findings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks LLM behavior against human experimental data from Kahneman et al. [64], Engel [65], Hoffman et al. [66], and Larney et al. [51] for bargaining games, and against Mengel [43], Eimontaite et al. [44], Proto et al. [45], and Cooper et al. [46] for repeated games. GPT-3.5 (English) achieves the closest alignment to human offered shares (33% vs. 28% Dictator; 35% vs. 41% Ultimatum), while GPT-4 is systematically over-generous (50%/48%) and less emotionally responsive, deviating from human emotional patterns. Compared to Brookins & DeBacker [55] and Horton [53], who found LLMs more cooperative than humans in Prisoner's Dilemma, this paper confirms higher LLM cooperation but shows that emotional prompting can reverse this, with 'anger' and 'fear' driving deflection rates above human levels. For Battle of the Sexes, GPT-4 uniquely exhibits the alternating strategy typical of humans [46] only under emotional prompting, whereas other models (including those in [52]) persistently choose their preferred action. In the Public Goods game, proprietary models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4) shift toward Free Rider under negative emotions, while OpenChat-7b maintains the highest cooperation—contrasting with the more uniformly cooperative behavior reported in [53, 54]. GigaChat (Russian) outperforms all other models in Russian-language emotional alignment (36%/40% offered shares vs. human 28%/41%), demonstrating that primary pretraining language is a critical factor not addressed in prior LLM game-theory evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously separate research threads: LLM evaluation in game-theoretical settings (Akata et al., Horton, Brookins) and emotional prompting effects on LLM performance (Li et al.). It departs from both by being the first to inject discrete emotional states (following Ekman's taxonomy) into strategic game and ethical decision-making evaluations, using the prompt-chaining mechanism from Akata et al. as its interaction backbone. The EAI framework extends the game-theoretical evaluation paradigm by adding an emotional axis and three prompting strategies (simple, co-player-based, external-based), while the ethics evaluation extends TrustLLM/ETHICS/MoralChoice benchmarks by conditioning them on emotional context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLM emotional biases systematically diverge from human emotional responses—particularly that positive emotions can decrease cooperation and that the most aligned model (GPT-4) is least emotionally human-like—raises urgent questions about whether current alignment techniques inadvertently suppress the very emotional reasoning that makes human decision-making predictable. The framework provides a concrete basis for emotional alignment benchmarks, but the authors acknowledge limitations: no multi-agent LLM-vs-LLM or LLM-vs-human experiments, a limited set of five emotions, and the open question of whether emotions are internally responsible for controlling generation in autoregressive LLMs. The practical implication is that deploying LLMs in autonomous strategic or ethical roles without emotional alignment safeguards is premature.
