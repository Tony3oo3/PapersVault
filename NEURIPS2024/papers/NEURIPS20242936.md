## Problem

Geometric deep learning models are widely deployed in scientific applications but face distribution shifts between training (often simulation-based) and deployment (real experimental) regimes. No prior benchmark has systematically evaluated GDL models across multiple scientific domains, multiple shift types (conditional, covariate, concept), and multiple levels of OOD data availability, leaving practitioners without guidance on which methods to use under which conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242936/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each data sample is a 3D point cloud where points carry spatial coordinates plus task-specific features: dummy all-ones features for particle tracking (Track), atom-type categorical features for materials (QMOF), or molecular conformer features for drug molecules (Drug00D-3D). A k-nearest-neighbor graph is constructed from pairwise spatial distances to enable message passing. Outputs are binary class labels (signal vs. background for Track, binding affinity for Drug) evaluated with ACC or AUC, or continuous band-gap values for QMOF evaluated with MAE.

## Method

GeSS introduces a causal data model that decomposes input X into a causal part X_c and an independent part X_i (conditionally independent given X_c), enabling a principled taxonomy of shifts: I-conditional (shift in P(X_i|X_c)), C-conditional (shift in P(X_c|Y)), covariate (shift in P(X)), and concept (shift in P(Y|X_c)). The benchmark spans 6 datasets across HEP, materials science, and biochemistry, with 10 curated shift scenarios (e.g., pileup level changes, signal momentum changes, DFT fidelity levels, molecular scaffold/size/assay changes) crossed with 3 levels of OOD information access (No-Info, O-Feature, Par-Label), yielding 30 experiment settings. Each setting evaluates 3 GDL backbones (EGNN, DGCNN, Point Transformer) against 11 algorithms: 6 OOD generalization methods (ERM, VREx, GroupDRO, DIR, LRI, MixUp), 2 domain adaptation methods (DANN, DeepCoral), and 3 transfer learning variants (fine-tuning with 100/500/1000 OOD labels). Source domains are partitioned into subgroups aligned with the shift mechanism to support group-based methods like GroupDRO and VREx.

## Results

Across 30 settings (10 shift cases × 3 OOD info levels) with 3 GDL backbones and 11 algorithms, no single method dominates all shift types. For I-Conditional Shift (Pileup), MixUp achieves the best OOD accuracy (89.41% on PU50, 82.29% on PU90 with EGNN). For C-Conditional Shift (Signal), DANN (a DA method) excels, reaching 77.36% ACC on z'₁₀→2μ with EGNN, a +9.4% improvement over ERM (70.72%). For Concept Shift (Fidelity), GroupDRO achieves the best No-Info MAE (0.996 on HSE06, EGNN), while TL₁₀₀₀ reaches 0.625 MAE when the label distribution gap is large. For Covariate Shift (Size/Scaffold), VREx and MixUp are competitive, with VREx achieving 65.01 AUC on Size (EGNN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark reveals that no algorithm is universally best: MixUp and LRI lead on I-Conditional (Pileup) shift, DANN dominates C-Conditional (Signal) shift (outperforming ERM by 9.4% on z'₁₀→2μ with EGNN), GroupDRO is strongest for Concept (Fidelity) shift at the No-Info level, and TL₁₀₀₀ achieves the best overall OOD performance under Concept shift when the marginal label distribution gap is large (HSE06 case: 0.625 MAE vs. ERM's 1.099). For Covariate Shift (Size/Scaffold), VREx and MixUp marginally outperform ERM (by ~0.1–0.3 AUC), while DA methods (DANN, DeepCoral) show no consistent advantage. Notably, TL₁₀₀ can underperform ERM in low-shift scenarios (e.g., 64.08% vs. 65.98% on Signal τ→3μ), illustrating catastrophic forgetting with limited labels. DANN's advantage is specific to geometric changes in the causal component X_c (track curvature in Signal shift) and does not transfer to I-Conditional shifts where only the independent component X_i varies (Pileup). GroupDRO's effectiveness depends on whether source-domain subgroup splits informatively reflect the target shift: it helps when splits correspond to distinct signal decay types but hurts when splits are based on a proxy (point count for Pileup).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distribution-shift benchmarking tradition (WILDS, OoD-Bench, GOOD, DrugOOD) to the geometric deep learning setting in scientific applications, a gap the authors explicitly identify. It builds directly on DrugOOD [35] for the biochemistry datasets, adapting 2D molecular representations to 3D point clouds suitable for GDL backbones (EGNN, DGCNN, Point Transformer). Conceptually, it unifies three previously separate research threads—domain generalization (No-Info), domain adaptation (O-Feature), and transfer learning (Par-Label)—under a single causal data model that decomposes the input into causal and independent components, enabling a finer-grained shift taxonomy (I-conditional, C-conditional, covariate, concept) tailored to scientific data generation processes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first comprehensive GDL-specific distribution-shift benchmark spanning three scientific domains (HEP, materials science, biochemistry) with 30 experimental settings, this work provides domain practitioners with actionable guidance on which method to deploy given their shift mechanism and OOD data availability. Key open questions include whether the causal data model assumptions (X_i ⊥ Y | X_c) hold broadly across scientific applications, how capturing higher-order geometric invariants (angles, torsions) affects OOD generalization, and whether the shift taxonomy transfers to other GDL architectures beyond the three tested. The finding that no universal best method exists across shift types underscores the need for shift-aware method selection rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.
