## Problem

Existing time-series representation learning methods preserve the original temporal order of time-steps, assuming it is optimal for the downstream task. However, non-adjacent segments in real-world time-series often exhibit strong dependencies that architectures with limited receptive fields (CNNs) or position-dependent attention (Transformers) struggle to capture. The paper asks whether a task-optimal rearrangement of time-series segments could enable more effective representation learning.

## Input / Output

S3 takes a time-series tensor of shape T×C (length T, C channels) and outputs a rearranged tensor of the same shape, making it a drop-in replacement at the input of any downstream model. If T is not divisible by the number of segments n, the first T mod n time-steps are truncated and re-prepended to the output to preserve shape. The layer is modular and can be stacked (controlled by hyperparameter φ), with each subsequent layer using a multiplier θ to increase segment count for finer-grained shuffling.

## Method

S3 operates in three steps: (1) Segment splits the input into n non-overlapping segments of τ = T/n time-steps; (2) Shuffle reorders segments using a learnable vector P, where higher values indicate higher priority in the reordered sequence. The key technical challenge is making discrete permutation differentiable: the authors construct an n×n matrix Ω with each row j placing p_k at position σ_j (the argsort index), convert it to a binary mask Ω̃, and perform a Hadamard product with a repeated-segment matrix U to create a gradient path through P while executing exact discrete reordering. (3) Stitch concatenates the shuffled segments and combines them with the original input via a learned weighted sum (implemented as Conv1D or MLP), preserving both the new arrangement and original temporal information. The shuffling parameters P are updated end-to-end with the backbone network, adapting to the specific task and data without any intermediate loss. A hyperparameter λ allows P to be higher-dimensional (n^m) with summation over leading dimensions to increase expressiveness, and stacking multiple S3 layers with multiplier θ enables multi-granularity shuffling at negligible computational cost (typically <100 added parameters).

## Results

On time-series classification, S3 yields average accuracy improvements of 3.89% (UCR), 5.25% (UEA), 32.03% (EEG), 9.68% (EEG2), and 5.18% (HAR) across four baselines, with the largest single gain being 39.59% (InfoTS on EEG). For univariate forecasting, S3 reduces MSE by up to 68.71% (Informer on ETTh1, H=720) and MAE by up to 24.10% (Informer on ETTh1, H=168). For multivariate forecasting, the peak MSE reduction reaches 51.22% (Informer on ETTm1, H=228). On anomaly detection, SoftCLT+S3 achieves F1 of 0.7498 on Yahoo and 0.6892 on KPI, outperforming the respective baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S3 consistently outperforms all four classification baselines (TS2Vec, DSN, InfoTS, SoftCLT) across every dataset, with the most dramatic gains on EEG (19–40% relative improvement) where the baselines struggle most. For forecasting, Informer benefits the most from S3, achieving up to 68.71% MSE reduction on ETTh1 (H=720) and 51.22% on ETTm1 (H=228), while PatchTST shows the smallest gains (1–3% MSE reduction), suggesting S3 is most impactful when the backbone has limited long-range dependency capture. LaST and CoST show moderate improvements (3–10% MSE reduction on average). On the pre-trained MOMENT foundation model, S3 improves PTB-XL classification accuracy from 0.7176 to 0.7552 (+5.24%) and reduces loss by 13.31%, confirming that S3 is effective even when the backbone is frozen. S3 also improves anomaly detection F1 for both TS2Vec (0.7188→0.7196 on Yahoo) and SoftCLT (0.7399→0.7498 on Yahoo, 0.6890→0.6892 on KPI), with the SoftCLT gain being more pronounced.

## Relation to Prior Work

S3 carves new conceptual ground by challenging the near-universal assumption in time-series deep learning that the original temporal ordering of time-steps is optimal for representation learning. While prior work like PatchTST [32] and SCINet [21] partition sequences into patches or subsequences, they preserve the relative order of those pieces; S3 instead learns a task-specific permutation of segments via differentiable sorting [41,42,43], a mechanism borrowed from the differentiable ranking literature but repurposed for temporal reordering. The weighted-sum stitching with the original sequence echoes residual connections, and the overall design is deliberately architecture-agnostic, positioning S3 as a complementary module rather than a replacement for CNNs, Transformers, or contrastive encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature with negligible parameter overhead (7–579 parameters vs. hundreds of thousands in baselines) makes S3 immediately adoptable by practitioners working on time-series classification, forecasting, or anomaly detection without redesigning their pipelines. The observation that S3 smooths the loss landscape and reduces training-loss variance by ~37% suggests it may act as an implicit regularizer, an effect worth further theoretical investigation. Open questions include whether the learned segment permutations carry interpretable structure (e.g., aligning with seasonal cycles), whether S3 generalizes to imputation or video time-series, and whether the large gains on Informer (up to 68%) reflect a genuine architectural synergy or partly compensate for a weakly-tuned baseline.
