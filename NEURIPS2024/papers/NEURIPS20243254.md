## Problem

Existing SDS-based text-to-3D generation methods produce visually realistic shapes but ignore physical stability, causing generated models to topple in physics simulations or fail to stand when 3D-printed. This gap is critical for downstream applications in interactive gaming, embodied AI, robotics, and fabrication, where self-supporting objects are a basic requirement. Prior attempts to incorporate physics either rely on indirect GAN-based approaches with limited expressibility or treat stability as a post-processing step that degrades text alignment.

## Input / Output

The method takes a free-form text prompt as input and produces a textured triangular mesh that is guaranteed to be self-supporting under gravity, frictional contact, and mild perturbations. The output mesh is directly compatible with rigid-body physics simulators (validated via IPC) and 3D-printable without additional support structures. The pipeline is agnostic to the coarse-stage generator, accepting any SDS-based framework's implicit representation (density field, SDF, or tetrahedral SDF) as its starting point.

## Method

Atlas3D is a plug-and-play refinement module that augments existing SDS-based text-to-3D pipelines with four physics-inspired loss terms optimized jointly with the SDS objective. The standability loss backpropagates through a differentiable rigid-body simulator (Warp, semi-implicit Euler) to penalize rotational deviation of the mesh after a fixed simulation duration, while the stable equilibrium loss—inspired by the potential well concept—penalizes configurations where the center-of-mass height decreases under small sampled perturbations (20 random rotation axes in the xy-plane at a fixed angle). Two geometry regularizers (normal consistency and bottom Laplacian) prevent mesh distortion and enforce flat contact surfaces. The two-stage training first generates a coarse shape with any SDS-based method, then refines it using a tetrahedral SDF representation (DmTet) with Nvdiffrast rendering and Stable Diffusion v2.1 guidance, computing the expensive standability loss only every 10 iterations to limit computational overhead.

## Results

On 107 text prompts evaluated with the Magic3D baseline, Atlas3D reduces the Time-Averaged Rotation Deviation (TRD) from 0.389 to 0.060, a 6.5× improvement in standability. CLIP score is nearly preserved (25.356 vs. 25.781), indicating minimal degradation in text alignment. In real-world 3D-printed user studies (800 trials), Atlas3D achieves a 92.25% standing success rate versus 7% for the baseline, and with a robotic arm (64 trials) the success rate is 90.6% vs. 6.25%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Magic3D [35], Atlas3D achieves a dramatic 6.5× reduction in TRD (0.060 vs. 0.389), confirming that the physics-based refinement stage is the dominant factor in standability. However, Atlas3D trades a small amount of perceptual quality: CLIP score drops by ~0.4 points and Elo (GPT-4o) drops by ~58 points, suggesting minor texture/shape distortion from the joint optimization. In real-world validation, the gap is stark: 92.25% vs. 7% success in human placement trials and 90.6% vs. 6.25% in robotic arm trials. Against MVDream [69], Atlas3D serves as a plug-in refinement that improves geometric stability while preserving the multi-view diffusion quality of the coarse stage (qualitative comparison in Fig. 5). Compared to the post-processing method Make-it-stand [59], Atlas3D's joint optimization better preserves text alignment and handles multi-contact scenarios (e.g., two-legged standing) that Make-it-stand cannot address because it assumes a fixed supporting surface.

## Relation to Prior Work

Atlas3D extends the SDS-based text-to-3D generation pipeline (DreamFusion, Magic3D, MVDream) by injecting a differentiable rigid-body simulation loss directly into the optimization loop, treating the generated mesh as a rigid body under gravity and frictional contact. Prior physics-aware 3D generation efforts (Mezghanni et al.'s GAN-based stability predictor, Wang et al.'s CFD-guided vehicle synthesis, Ni et al.'s four-leg object reconstruction) either relied on indirect/offline physics signals or were restricted to narrow object categories; Atlas3D departs by using an online differentiable simulator (Warp) as a first-class loss term within the SDS gradient, making it a plug-and-play refinement stage orthogonal to the underlying generation backbone. The stable equilibrium loss, inspired by the potential-well concept, replaces expensive multi-perturbation simulation with a single analytic check on center-of-mass height, drawing from classical mechanics rather than learned predictors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a practical gap between visually appealing text-to-3D outputs and their real-world deployability in robotics, simulation, and 3D fabrication, with the 92% vs. 7% user-study success rate and 90.6% vs. 6.25% robot-arm success rate demonstrating that physics-constrained generation transfers directly to physical manipulation. Key limitations include the rigid-body assumption (no deformable objects), restriction to SDS-based backbones, and potential mesh distortion from unconstrained vertex optimization; the authors note that latent embeddings or skeleton rigging could mitigate the latter. Open questions include generalization to image-to-3D, non-diffusion generation methods, and whether the physics refinement stage can be made faster than the current ~36-minute per-model refinement cost.
