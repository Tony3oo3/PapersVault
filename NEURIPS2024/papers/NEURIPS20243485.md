## Problem

Hand-held low-light photographs simultaneously suffer from low visibility, sensor noise, and camera motion blur, making them unsuitable for direct NeRF training. Existing low-light NeRF methods assume no camera motion, while deblurring NeRF methods assume adequate lighting, and naively combining image-based enhancement with NeRF fails because these degradation factors are highly coupled. The paper identifies that noise is always sharp regardless of camera shake (due to independent sensor noise generation), revealing an implicit degradation order that can be exploited for sequential decoupling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243485/cropped_page1_idx5.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 20–25 hand-held low-light RGB images at 1120×640 resolution (most pixel intensities below 50, 80% containing camera shake) along with COLMAP-estimated camera poses. Training is fully unsupervised: the brightness-scaled noisy blurry images themselves serve as the supervision signal via an inverse degradation model (adding predicted noise and applying predicted blur to the rendered NeRF). At inference, only the Scene-NeRF is retained to render clean, sharp, normally-illuminated novel views; the Noise-Estimator and blur kernel are discarded.

## Method

LuSh-NeRF sequentially removes degradation factors following the implicit order (low visibility → noise → blur). The Scene-Noise Decomposition (SND) module introduces a Noise-Estimator network (same MLP structure as the Scene-NeRF but without volume rendering, outputting only the mid-ray sample) that learns view-inconsistent noise, while a multi-view consistency loss computed via GIM image matching on rendered views forces the Scene-NeRF to retain only view-consistent scene information. The Camera Trajectory Prediction (CTP) module predicts rigid camera motion trajectories using a DFT-based low-pass filter to identify low-frequency (noise-robust) image regions; gradients from high-frequency noisy rays are detached to prevent noise from corrupting blur kernel estimation. The two modules are co-optimized end-to-end in an iterative fashion: SND provides a cleaner scene for CTP's trajectory prediction, while CTP's sharpened output aids SND's denoising. Ablations confirm that denoising before sharpening (Denoise → Sharp) is more robust than the reverse order.

## Results

On the proposed LOL-BlurNeRF synthetic dataset (5 scenes), LuSh-NeRF achieves the best average LPIPS of 0.2914 and the best average PSNR of 19.31 dB, outperforming all 8 baseline combinations. The closest competitor, LEDNet+NeRF, achieves PSNR 18.40 and LPIPS 0.3204, though the paper notes LEDNet was specifically trained on the same LOL-Blur synthetic data, making this an unfair comparison. The paper emphasizes LPIPS as the key metric since PSNR/SSIM can be misleading when methods produce different color contrasts and brightness levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LuSh-NeRF outperforms all 8 baseline combinations on average LPIPS (0.2914), the metric the authors designate as most informative for perceptual quality. Against the strongest baseline LEDNet+NeRF (PSNR 18.40, SSIM 0.6488, LPIPS 0.3204), LuSh-NeRF wins on PSNR (+0.91 dB) and LPIPS (−0.029) but trails on SSIM (0.5853 vs. 0.6488); the authors note this comparison is unfair since LEDNet was trained on the same LOL-Blur synthetic data. Against the deblurring-NeRF baselines (PairLIE+DP-NeRF: PSNR 14.81, LPIPS 0.3344; URetinexNet+DP-NeRF: PSNR 16.74, LPIPS 0.4046), LuSh-NeRF gains 4.5–2.6 dB in PSNR and 0.04–0.11 in LPIPS, showing that jointly modeling noise and blur within the NeRF framework is substantially more effective than sequential image-level preprocessing. Against the low-light-NeRF baselines (Restormer+LLNeRF: PSNR 16.42, LPIPS 0.4419; MPRNet+LLNeRF: PSNR 14.01, LPIPS 0.4661), LuSh-NeRF improves PSNR by 2.9–5.3 dB and LPIPS by 0.15–0.17, confirming that camera-motion handling is critical even when low-light enhancement is applied. The three-stage pipeline baseline (LLFormer+MPRNet+NeRF: PSNR 18.14, LPIPS 0.3973) is the closest on PSNR but still lags LuSh-NeRF by 1.17 dB and 0.106 in LPIPS, underscoring the benefit of multi-view-consistent, in-the-loop denoising and deblurring.

## Relation to Prior Work

LuSh-NeRF sits at the intersection of two previously separate NeRF sub-areas: low-light NeRF reconstruction (LLNeRF, Aleth-NeRF) and motion-blur NeRF (DP-NeRF, Deblur-NeRF). It builds directly on NeRF's volume rendering as the scene representation and on DP-NeRF's Rigid Blurring Kernel for camera-motion modeling, but departs from both by introducing a sequential decoupling strategy grounded in the observation that sensor noise is always sharp regardless of camera shake. The Scene-Noise Decomposition module is a novel contribution that exploits multi-view consistency (via GIM image matching) to separate a noise field from the scene field, while the Camera Trajectory Prediction module adapts DP-NeRF's kernel prediction with a DFT-based low-pass mask to suppress noise interference during blur estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to jointly address low visibility, sensor noise, and camera motion blur in NeRF training, a combination that is common in real hand-held nighttime photography but previously unaddressed. The implicit-ordering insight (low visibility → noise → blur) provides a principled decomposition that could inform other multi-degradation restoration tasks. However, the evidence base is limited: the dataset contains only 10 scenes (5 synthetic, 5 real), training is slow due to dual NeRF optimization and multi-ray rendering, and the authors acknowledge that noise with similar characteristics across views may be misclassified as scene information. Open questions include scalability to larger scene collections, robustness to more extreme degradation levels, and whether the sequential ordering generalizes to other degradation combinations.
