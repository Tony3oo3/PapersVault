## Problem

Evaluating ML models under severe distribution shifts is infeasible when labeled data suffers selection bias (e.g., clinical labels only exist for screened patients), and naively sampling unlabeled inputs is prohibitively expensive. Existing active learning heuristics are myopic and poorly suited to batched, few-horizon evaluation, while REINFORCE policy gradients suffer from prohibitively high variance in the combinatorial action space of batch selection. The paper asks: can we formulate adaptive labeling as a planning problem and solve it with low-variance, auto-differentiable policy gradients?

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial labeled dataset (biased, covering only a subset of the deployment support), a pool of unlabeled inputs, a set of evaluation inputs drawn from the target deployment distribution, and a prediction model to evaluate. At each of T periods, it outputs a batch of K inputs to label (selected from the pool), then updates a posterior belief over the data-generating function. The final output is the variance of the model's performance metric (e.g., MSE) under the posterior after all T batches are collected. The UQ module (GP or Ensemble+) must support differentiable posterior updates, and the batch size K and horizon T are fixed a priori.

## Method

The paper formulates adaptive labeling as a Markov Decision Process where states are posterior beliefs over the data-generating function f, actions are K-subset selections from a pool, and the objective is to minimize the posterior variance of model performance after T periods. The key algorithmic contribution is a pathwise policy gradient estimator that auto-differentiates through simulated roll-outs: at each step, pseudo-labels are drawn from the current posterior, the posterior is updated (via closed-form GP updates or differentiable gradient descent through torchopt for deep ensembles), and the resulting performance variance is backpropagated to the policy parameters. Batch selection is made differentiable via soft K-subset sampling (a Gumbel-based continuous relaxation from Xie & Ermon 2019), replacing the discrete combinatorial action with a smooth relaxation controlled by a temperature parameter. The simplest instantiation—a one-step lookahead policy that optimizes only the next period's expected posterior variance—already outperforms active learning heuristics and REINFORCE. A theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows the pathwise estimator has bounded MSE while REINFORCE's MSE is unbounded as the policy becomes peaked.

## Results

On the Ensemble+ toy setting (4 clusters, 1 horizon, batch size 2), Autodiff 1-lookahead achieves a variance of the MSE estimate of 1697.1 and an error of 45.27, substantially outperforming Random Sampling (variance 7129.8, error 136.2), Uncertainty Sampling Static (variance 10852, error 162.2), and Uncertainty Sampling Sequential (variance 8585.5, error 144). On the GP-based synthetic and eICU experiments (10 horizons, batch size 5), Autodiff 1-lookahead consistently reduces posterior variance of MSE across all horizons and yields the lowest error in estimated MSE relative to the population oracle, with pathwise gradients providing more robust optimization than REINFORCE across all time steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two families of baselines: active learning heuristics (Uncertainty Sampling Static and Sequential, from [1]) and REINFORCE-based policy gradients [38], plus Random Sampling. On the GP-based synthetic and eICU experiments, Autodiff 1-lookahead substantially outperforms both active learning heuristics across all 10 horizons in terms of posterior variance reduction and MSE estimation error; the active learning baselines are sometimes even worse than random sampling in these small-horizon batched settings. Compared to REINFORCE, Autodiff 1-lookahead achieves similar final performance but with significantly more robust optimization across all horizons, as pathwise gradients have lower variance than score-function estimates. In the Ensemble+ toy setting (Table 1), Autodiff 1-lookahead and REINFORCE tie exactly (both achieving variance 1697.1 and error 45.27), while both dramatically outperform the active learning heuristics by roughly 4–6× in variance and 3× in error. The paper attributes the advantage of planning over myopic heuristics to the ability to account for information overlap within a batch and to align the sampling objective with the actual evaluation metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the adaptive data selection problem from active learning (which aims to improve model performance over many batches) into a planning problem for model evaluation under out-of-support distribution shift, where the objective is minimizing posterior variance of model performance over a few batches. It draws on Bayesian optimization's knowledge gradient as a conceptual ancestor for one-step lookahead, on Gittins indices for the MDP-over-posterior formulation, and on differentiable simulator / pathwise policy gradient literature (Suh et al., Mora et al.) for the core optimization technique. The K-subset sampling relaxation from Xie and Ermon provides the differentiable batch-selection mechanism, while the UQ module is deliberately agnostic, supporting both closed-form GPs and approximate deep ensembles (Ensemble+, Epistemic Neural Networks).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically critical gap: clinical and safety-critical models are evaluated on data that systematically excludes the populations where they matter most, and random labeling is prohibitively expensive. The finding that even a single planning step substantially outperforms well-studied active learning heuristics (which can be worse than random in small-horizon batched settings) suggests that the planning perspective is underexploited in evaluation contexts. Key open questions include maintaining posterior consistency as data accumulates (a limitation the authors acknowledge), scaling the auto-differentiation pipeline to multi-step roll-outs with deep UQ modules, and extending beyond the small-scale experiments (≤500 pool points, K=5) demonstrated here.
