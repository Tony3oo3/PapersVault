## Problem

In non-exemplar class-incremental learning, old class samples are unavailable, making it difficult to balance old and new classes when conflicts arise. Existing methods address these conflicts only retrospectively once new tasks arrive, but the base phase training allows classes to spread across the entire embedding space, leaving no reserved room for future classes. This paper tackles the gap by proposing a prospective strategy that prepares the model for future conflicts in advance rather than reacting to them.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images at varying resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 64×64 for TinyImageNet, 224×224 for ImageNet) and outputs a single class label from the cumulative set of all previously seen classes. A critical constraint is that at each incremental phase, only the current task's training data is accessible; no old samples are stored or replayed, and the model must classify without knowing the task identity of a test sample. The only persistent memory is a set of per-class prototypes (class means in feature space) computed after each phase.

## Method

PRL introduces two complementary mechanisms. In the base phase, a Preemptive Embedding Squeezing (PES) loss computed over mini-batches enforces intra-class concentration (maximizing cosine similarity within a class) and inter-class reserved separation (minimizing cosine similarity across classes), proactively reserving embedding space for unknown future classes. In the incremental phase, a Prototype-Guided Representation Update (PGRU) strategy projects both new-class features (extracted by the frozen previous-phase model) and saved old-class prototypes into a shared latent space via an undercomplete autoencoder, then enforces orthogonality between new features and old prototypes in that latent space. An alignment loss (MSE) then pulls the current model's embedding space toward the latent space, so that the orthogonality constraint indirectly guides the current model to embed new classes in the reserved region. The full training objective combines cross-entropy, knowledge distillation, prototype rehearsal, and the PGRU loss, and the approach is plug-and-play with existing NECIL baselines such as PRAKA, PASS, and IL2A.

## Results

PRL reports average incremental accuracy (A_T) across four datasets. On CIFAR-100, it achieves 71.26% (5 phases), 70.17% (10 phases), and 68.44% (20 phases). On TinyImageNet, it reaches 58.12%, 57.24%, and 54.51% for the same phase settings. On ImageNet-Subset, PRL scores 72.85%, 71.54%, and 66.88%, and on the full ImageNet-1K (10 phases) it attains 62.74% ± 0.34. Overall, PRL improves over the prior SOTA (NAPA-VQ) by 1.4% on CIFAR-100 and 6.0% on TinyImageNet, with a 3.6% average gain on ImageNet-Subset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRL outperforms all 11 baselines across every dataset and phase setting where comparisons are available. Against the closest prior SOTA, NAPA-VQ [52], PRL gains +0.82 to +1.13 pp on CIFAR-100, +2.57 to +3.86 pp on TinyImageNet, and +2.04 to +2.93 pp on ImageNet-Subset. The margin over PRAKA [10] (the baseline pipeline PRL builds upon) is larger: +1.24 to +2.58 pp on CIFAR-100 and +4.63 to +4.80 pp on TinyImageNet, confirming that both PES and PGRU contribute meaningfully. Against structure-based methods SOPE [11] and SSRE [7], PRL leads by 4.4–6.6 pp on CIFAR-100 and 2.6–4.4 pp on TinyImageNet. On the large-scale ImageNet-1K (10 phases), PRL reaches 62.74%, surpassing POLO [51] (61.53%) and SOPE [11] (60.20%) by 1.2 and 2.5 pp respectively. The plug-and-play experiments (Table 7) further show that adding PRL to IL2A [38] and PASS [6] yields average gains of 2.7% and 2.3% on CIFAR-100, demonstrating compatibility with other NECIL pipelines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the PRAKA pipeline (Shi & Ye, ICCV 2023) for its baseline, which itself extends the prototype rehearsal mechanism from PASS (Zhu et al., CVPR 2021) with augmented asymmetric knowledge aggregation. The key conceptual departure is shifting from a retrospective conflict-resolution paradigm—where old-new class overlaps are addressed only after new data arrives—to a prospective one: the base phase proactively squeezes embeddings to reserve space (PES), and the incremental phase uses a latent-space projector to guide new class features away from stored prototypes (PGRU). This connects to the embedding-space regularization line (Chaudhry et al. 2020, Zhou et al. 2022, Shi et al. 2022) but is specifically tailored to the exemplar-free NECIL setting where no old data is available at test time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play compatibility demonstrated with IL2A and PASS makes PRL a practical add-on for existing NECIL pipelines, which is valuable given the privacy and storage constraints that motivate the exemplar-free setting. The consistent 1–4% gains across four datasets and multiple phase settings, along with the balanced confusion-matrix and stability-plasticity analyses, provide strong evidence that prospective space reservation is a meaningful design principle. The authors acknowledge a key open question: since the number and distribution of future classes are unknown, how to rationally allocate the reserved space remains unresolved, and the fixed hyperparameters (λ=0.5, γ=0.1) may not transfer optimally to very long task sequences or highly imbalanced class distributions.
