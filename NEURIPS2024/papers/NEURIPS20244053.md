## Problem

Interactive segmentation methods are evaluated using a baseline click strategy (clicking the center of the largest error region) that does not reflect how real users actually click, leading to overestimated real-world performance. The paper addresses this by collecting 475K real user clicks via crowdsourcing and building a clickability model that simulates realistic click distributions, enabling a more faithful benchmark of interactive segmentation methods.

## Input / Output

The clickability model takes an RGB image (416×416), a ground truth object mask, and a segmentation error mask (false-positive or false-negative) as input, and outputs a single-channel clickability map where each pixel encodes the probability of being clicked. The benchmark then samples clicks from this distribution, partitioning them into 10 probability-mass groups (G1–G10) to evaluate segmentation methods per group, reporting NoC20@90, NoF20@90, and IoU-AuC20 along with robustness metrics (ΔSB, ΔGR, ΔHH).

## Method

The clickability model adapts SegNeXt-B (MSCAN-B backbone) by feeding the original image through the network while concatenating the ground truth mask with the error mask as an additional input channel (inspired by Conv1S), followed by three convolutional layers to encode mask features and min-max normalization to produce a probability map. Training uses Kullback-Leibler divergence loss between predicted and ground-truth clickability distributions, with clickability maps constructed by placing unit mass at click positions, applying Gaussian smoothing (σ=5 selected via ablation), multiplying by a soft error mask to constrain clicks within the error region, and normalizing. The RClicks benchmark evaluates 11 methods (33 checkpoints) by sampling one click per round from each of 10 probability groups over 20 interaction rounds, then computing per-group and aggregate statistics to measure both average quality and robustness to click position variation. A crowdsourcing study on Toloka AI collected clicks using an Object CutOut display mode (validated via ablation against text descriptions using KS, PL1, and Wasserstein distance metrics) to minimize presentation bias.

## Results

The RClicks benchmark reveals that the standard baseline click strategy underestimates real-world annotation time by 5–29% (ΔSB) across 11 interactive segmentation methods on DAVIS, COCO-MVal, and TETRIS. Robustness varies dramatically: annotation time differences between the most and least probable clicking groups (ΔGR) range from 3% (SAM 2.1 Hiera-T on TETRIS) to 79% (GPCIS on COCO-MVal). The clickability model outperforms all baselines (uniform, distance transform, saliency) on TETRIS validation, achieving KS=0.55, PL1=0.40, WD=0.08, NSS=9.11, and PDE=4.69×10⁻⁵. No single method achieves both the lowest NoC20@90 and the smallest ΔGR simultaneously across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 11 interactive segmentation methods (33 checkpoints) using both the standard baseline click strategy and realistic clicks sampled from the clickability model. SAM 2 Hiera-H achieves the lowest NoC20@90 on DAVIS (4.39±0.23) and the smallest ΔGR on TETRIS (4.87%), making it the most robust method overall. CFR-ICL (ViT-H) achieves the best NoC20@90 on TETRIS (2.12±0.34) and COCO-MVal (2.70±0.63), but its ΔGR of 14.33% on TETRIS indicates moderate sensitivity to click position. SimpleClick ViT-H (C+L) achieves the best NoC20@90 on COCO-MVal (2.57±0.54) with a low ΔGR of 10.83%, but underperforms on DAVIS (5.00±0.42). GPCIS and CDNet show the largest ΔSB values (up to 26.43% and 15.15% respectively), meaning the baseline strategy most overestimates their real-world performance. The key finding is that no method simultaneously minimizes both NoC20@90 and ΔGR across all three datasets: SAM-like models are more robust (lower ΔGR) while CFR-ICL and SimpleClick achieve lower average NoC but with higher variance across clicking groups.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the TETRIS benchmark [14], which first demonstrated that real users do not always click at the center of the largest error region as assumed by the standard baseline strategy. It draws conceptually from saliency prediction (using TranSalNet as a baseline and adopting the fixation-to-probability-map construction paradigm) to formulate click prediction as a spatial probability estimation problem. Architecturally, the clickability model adapts SegNeXt [46] with an additional mask-conditioned input branch inspired by Conv1S/RITM [19], but repurposes the segmentation backbone for a fundamentally different output: a per-pixel click probability map rather than a segmentation mask.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RClicks provides the first comprehensive, learned-click benchmark for interactive segmentation, revealing that the standard baseline evaluation overestimates real-world annotation speed by 5–29% and that annotation time varies by up to 79% across realistic clicking patterns. The finding that no current method simultaneously achieves optimal performance and robustness across all datasets is a practical warning for deployment. Key limitations include the clickability model being trained only on TETRIS (potentially domain-specific), reliance on a single crowdsourcing platform (demographic bias), and restriction to click-only interactions, leaving open questions about generalization to medical/satellite imagery and to other prompt modalities like scribbles or contours.
