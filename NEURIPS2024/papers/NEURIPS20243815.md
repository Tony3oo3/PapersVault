## Problem

Convergent cross mapping (CCM) is the standard tool for causal discovery in coupled dynamical systems, but its test statistic is difficult to interpret, it does not explicitly learn a cross map function, and it is prone to false positives when shadow manifold reconstructions are imperfect. The paper asks whether the geometric structure of shadow manifolds—specifically the vector fields that encode their dynamics—can be exploited to yield a more principled and robust causality test.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243815/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two time series and requires as input both the delay-embedding vectors (shadow manifold points) and their estimated time derivatives (tangent vectors) at each time step, typically obtained via finite differences or Savitzky-Golay filtering. It produces a single scalar test statistic per causal direction: the Pearson correlation between the actual vector field on the target manifold and the push-forward of the source vector field through the learned cross map's Jacobian. The statistic is bounded in [-1, 1] and equals 1 in the ideal case of a perfect cross map, making it directly interpretable as a cosine-similarity measure.

## Method

TSCI reframes the CCM cross-map test in the language of differential geometry: if a differentiable cross map F exists between two shadow manifolds, then the Jacobian J_F at each point must map the velocity vector field on the source manifold to the velocity vector field on the target manifold (Lemma 2.1.1). The algorithm learns a differentiable function F (via MLP, Gaussian process regression, splines, or k-nearest-neighbors local linear regression), computes J_F at each data point, pushes forward the source tangent vectors, and scores causality as the correlation between the pushed-forward vectors and the actual target vector field. This is geometrically more principled than CCM's ambient-space prediction accuracy because tangent vectors live in a centered linear space, so correlation is a natural similarity measure. The approach is model-agnostic with respect to the cross map regressor and can be dropped into existing CCM pipelines (including latent CCM with neural ODEs) as a replacement for the prediction-based test statistic.

## Results

On the Double Pendulum system (Table 1), Latent TSCI(MLP) achieves the highest correlation scores for both true causal directions: 0.915±0.006 for Z→X and 0.612±0.053 for Z→Y, compared to 0.737±0.019 and 0.475±0.043 for Latent CCM. On the Rössler-Lorenz system (Fig. 3), TSCI provides visibly better separation between true and false causal directions across coupling strengths C=0 to 3, with the true-direction statistic concentrated near 1 while the false direction remains near 0. TSCI also maintains correct causal identification at lower signal-to-noise ratios than CCM when additive noise or periodic trends corrupt the observations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CCM (Sugihara et al., 2012), TSCI achieves superior separation between true and false causal directions on the Rössler-Lorenz system, with the true-direction statistic more tightly concentrated near 1 and the false direction more tightly near 0, reducing false positives especially at moderate coupling strengths. Against Latent CCM (De Brouwer et al., 2020) on the Double Pendulum, TSCI(MLP) improves the true-positive score for Z→X from 0.737 to 0.915 and for Z→Y from 0.475 to 0.612, while keeping false-positive scores at similarly low levels. Granger causality (Granger, 1969) fails completely, incorrectly inferring bidirectional causality for all C>0 on the Rössler-Lorenz system. Among pairwise causal discovery methods, RECI (Blöbaum et al., 2018) consistently selects X→Y even at C=0 with very weak coefficients (≈−0.02 to −0.04), IGCI (Daniušis et al., 2010) fails to detect any causal edge with near-zero scores, and ANM (Hoyer et al., 2008) detects bidirectional causality for all C>0, confirming that these methods are ill-suited to coupled dynamical systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Convergent Cross Mapping (CCM) framework of Sugihara et al. (2012), which uses Takens' theorem to reconstruct shadow manifolds from time series and detect causal links via cross maps. The key conceptual departure is in Step 2 of CCM: rather than measuring prediction accuracy of the cross map in ambient space (which can yield false positives due to manifold geometry), TSCI checks whether the *dynamics*—specifically the velocity vector fields—are consistent under the cross map's Jacobian pushforward. The method is explicitly designed as a model-agnostic drop-in replacement, compatible with CCM extensions like latent CCM (De Brouwer et al., 2020) that use neural ODEs for state reconstruction, and it subsumes the k-nearest-neighbor regression of original CCM as a special case (Algorithm 2).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CCM is widely used in ecology, neuroscience, and environmental science for causal discovery where Granger causality fails, but its test statistic is difficult to interpret and prone to false positives. TSCI provides a geometrically principled, interpretable alternative (cosine similarity of tangent vectors, bounded by 1) that is more robust to spurious causation, particularly at low coupling strengths and under signal corruption. Key limitations remain: the method still requires well-constructed shadow manifolds, the general synchrony problem (where strong coupling makes the cross map invertible, yielding bidirectional detection) is a topological inevitability, and validation is limited to relatively low-dimensional toy systems. Open questions include scalability to high-dimensional or partially observed systems and extension to discrete-time or stochastic dynamical systems.
