## Problem

It remains unclear how task similarity in input features versus readout patterns differentially affects knowledge transfer and retention in continual learning, and how these two axes of similarity interact with common mitigation algorithms such as activity gating and weight regularization. Previous teacher-student analyses either conflated feature and readout similarity or did not characterize their interaction with specific algorithms, leaving open when continual learning is fundamentally difficult and which methods can reliably address it.

## Input / Output

The model consumes input vectors x ∈ R^{N_x} generated from a low-dimensional latent variable s ∈ R^{N_s} (with N_s ≪ N_x) via task-specific mixing matrices A_μ, and produces a linear regression output y = Wψ(x). Task similarity is controlled by two independent parameters: ρ_a (element-wise correlation between consecutive A matrices, governing feature similarity) and ρ_b (correlation between B matrices, governing readout similarity). Performance is reported as transfer performance Δε_TF (error reduction on task 2 from prior learning) and retention performance Δε_RT (error preservation on task 1 after learning task 2), both averaged over random task realizations under a given (ρ_a, ρ_b) pair.

## Method

The paper develops a linear teacher-student model with a low-dimensional latent structure that analytically decouples the effects of feature similarity (ρ_a) and readout similarity (ρ_b) on transfer and retention. Closed-form expressions (Eq. 4) reveal that high feature similarity combined with low readout similarity is catastrophic for both transfer and retention, while the opposite combination is benign, and that transfer depends non-monotonically on ρ_a under fixed ρ_b. The paper then derives analytical solutions for how random and adaptive activity gating, plasticity gating, input soft-thresholding, and weight regularization (in both Euclidean and Fisher information metrics) interact with task similarity, showing that Fisher information metric regularization achieves near-perfect retention regardless of (ρ_a, ρ_b) without harming transfer, while its diagonal approximation (as in EWC) and Euclidean regularization are far less robust. Numerical validation on permuted MNIST with an explicit four-dimensional latent structure and a one-hidden-layer ReLU network confirms the theoretical predictions, including a layer-wise approximation of the Fisher metric that outperforms both the diagonal approximation and Euclidean regularization.

## Results

In the linear teacher-student model with low-dimensional latent variables, the vanilla model achieves average transfer Δε_TF = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 and average retention Δε_RT = 43/60 ≈ 0.717 under a uniform prior on task similarity (ρ_a, ρ_b ∈ [0,1]). Weight regularization in the Fisher information metric achieves near-perfect retention (Δε_RT → 1) regardless of task similarity while preserving vanilla-level transfer, provided N_s ≪ N_x(1−ρ_a²). Random activity gating at the optimal average level α = 3/4 improves average transfer to ≈ 0.188 but reduces retention to ≈ 0.855, illustrating a fundamental transfer–retention tradeoff. Numerical validation on Permuted MNIST with latent variables confirms the asymmetric, non-monotonic dependence: at (ρ_a, ρ_b) = (1.0, 0.0) both transfer and retention fall below chance, while at (0.0, 1.0) retention remains high with zero transfer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper analytically and numerically compares five continual learning strategies against the vanilla model. Full Fisher information metric regularization (Synaptic Intelligence, Zenke et al. 2017) is the clear winner: it achieves perfect retention (Δε_RT → 1) independent of task similarity while preserving vanilla-level transfer, a property that the diagonal approximation (EWC, Kirkpatrick et al. 2017) loses because the diagonal makes the metric full-rank even when the true FIM is low-rank. In the Permuted MNIST experiments, the layer-wise Fisher approximation consistently outperforms both the diagonal (synapse-wise) approximation and Euclidean regularization across all task similarity settings (Figs. 7F–H), though the diagonal approximation performs closer to the layer-wise Fisher than to Euclidean due to hidden-layer sparsity. Task-dependent activity gating (Masse et al. 2018) improves retention at the cost of transfer under random gating, but adaptive gating based on a probe trial mitigates this tradeoff, particularly when readout similarity is high. Plasticity gating (Özgün et al. 2020) and input soft-thresholding (Srivastava et al. 2013) are shown to have no effect on either transfer or retention at the over-parameterized limit (N_s ≪ N_x), making them ineffective in this regime despite their potential utility in under-parameterized settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the teacher-student framework for continual learning analysis (Lee et al., ICML 2021; Evron et al., COLT 2022) by introducing a low-dimensional latent variable structure that decouples feature similarity (ρ_a) from readout similarity (ρ_b), a distinction prior works either conflated or addressed only numerically. It then derives closed-form expressions for transfer and retention under this disentangled similarity, and systematically analyzes how activity gating, plasticity gating, activity sparsification, and weight regularization (Euclidean and Fisher information metric) interact with each similarity dimension. The Fisher information metric analysis builds directly on EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., PNAS 2017) and Synaptic Intelligence (Zenke et al., ICML 2017), but provides the first analytical characterization of when their diagonal approximation fails relative to the full metric in a continual learning setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear analytical criterion for when continual learning is fundamentally difficult (high feature similarity + low readout similarity) and when it is benign (the opposite), which has direct implications for benchmark design and algorithm selection. The finding that Fisher information metric regularization achieves near-perfect retention regardless of task similarity, while its diagonal approximation and Euclidean variants are much less robust, offers concrete guidance for practitioners choosing regularization strategies. Key limitations include the linear model assumption, analysis restricted to two tasks, and validation on a single dataset (permuted MNIST); the paper acknowledges that hidden layers in deep networks enable distinctive adaptations for feature vs. readout similarity that the linear theory cannot capture, and that the diagonal approximation performs better in practice than predicted due to sparse hidden-layer activity. Open questions include extending the framework to multi-task sequences, nonlinear architectures, and understanding how biological circuits implement the Fisher-metric-like flexibility the theory predicts.
