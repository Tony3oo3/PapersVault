## Problem

Regret-based UED algorithms produce curricula at the frontier of student capability but do not guarantee diversity in the student's experiences, limiting generalization in large environment design spaces. Existing approaches to quantifying environment novelty—POET (parameter-encoding distance), EPOET (rank-normalized performance vectors), and DIPLR (Wasserstein distance on occupancy distributions)—suffer from domain-specificity, prohibitive computational cost, or lack of curriculum-awareness, making them unsuitable for scalable, general novelty-driven curriculum design.

## Input / Output

The method consumes state-action pairs collected from the student's trajectories across past curriculum levels, stored in a fixed-window FIFO buffer (Γ) to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. State representations vary by domain: LSTM hidden states in Minigrid, 24-dimensional proprioceptive vectors in BipedalWalker, and CNN-extracted features from 96×96 RGB images in CarRacing. The output is a per-level novelty score (negative mean log-likelihood under the fitted GMM) that is combined with a regret score via weighted rank-based prioritization to produce level replay probabilities.

## Method

CENIE models the student's accumulated state-action space coverage using a Gaussian Mixture Model fitted via k-means++ initialization and EM optimization. A candidate level's novelty is computed as the negative mean log-likelihood of its state-action pairs under this GMM, so levels inducing unfamiliar experiences receive higher novelty scores. To adapt to the evolving coverage distribution, multiple GMMs with varying component counts K are fitted at each update and the best is selected by silhouette score, creating a pseudo-online model that dynamically adjusts complexity. The novelty score is integrated with regret through a weighted replay probability P_replay = α·P_N + (1−α)·P_R, where both scores are converted to probabilities via rank-based prioritization with a tunable temperature β. This mechanism is deployed atop PLR⊥ and ACCEL to produce PLR-CENIE and ACCEL-CENIE, which achieve state-of-the-art zero-shot transfer across Minigrid, BipedalWalker, and CarRacing.

## Results

Across three UED benchmarks, CENIE-augmented algorithms achieve state-of-the-art zero-shot transfer performance. In CarRacing (20 F1 tracks), PLR-CENIE achieves a mean return of 553±32, surpassing DIPLR (499±20) and PLR⊥ (392±28). In BipedalWalker, ACCEL-CENIE reaches ~55% of optimal performance and expands state-action space coverage from 42.5% (ACCEL) to 47.6%, while PLR-CENIE expands coverage from 43.4% (PLR⊥) to 55.3%. In Minigrid, PLR-CENIE achieves ~50% solved rate on the out-of-distribution PerfectMazeLarge (51×51), compared to <10% for PLR⊥, matching ACCEL's performance despite using a random generator instead of mutation-based editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLR-CENIE consistently outperforms or matches the best baseline on all 20 CarRacing F1 tracks, achieving a mean return of 553±32 versus DIPLR's 499±20 and PLR⊥'s 392±28. In Minigrid, ACCEL-CENIE outperforms ACCEL in all 8 held-out test environments, and PLR-CENIE outperforms PLR⊥ in 7 of 8, with the gap widening dramatically on the out-of-distribution PerfectMazeLarge (~50% vs <10% solved rate). In BipedalWalker, ACCEL-CENIE outperforms ACCEL in 5 of 6 test environments and PLR-CENIE outperforms PLR⊥ in all except Stump. DR, Minimax, and PAIRED are substantially outperformed across all domains (e.g., CarRacing mean returns of 214, 99, and 105 respectively vs. 553 for PLR-CENIE). The ablation model PLR-CENIE† (novelty-only, no regret) also outperforms PLR⊥ in CarRacing (465±42 vs. 392±28), confirming that novelty alone provides a meaningful improvement over regret-only prioritization, though the combined novelty+regret objective yields the strongest results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the UED literature by introducing a complementary novelty objective alongside the dominant regret-based curriculum design metric. It departs from prior novelty quantification methods (POET's parameter-encoding distance, EPOET's rank-normalized vectors, DIPLR's Wasserstein distance) by proposing CENIE, which measures novelty through the student's state-action space coverage modeled via GMMs—making it domain-agnostic, curriculum-aware, and scalable. The GMM-based density estimation draws conceptual inspiration from curiosity-driven RL (count-based exploration, neural density models) and ALP-GMM's adaptive component selection, but repurposes these tools for environment-level novelty scoring rather than intrinsic reward shaping. CENIE is integrated as a plug-in into existing regret-based UED algorithms (PLR⊥ and ACCEL), preserving their generation mechanisms while augmenting their level replay prioritization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CENIE addresses the regret stagnation problem where purely regret-driven curricula halt learning prematurely by locking the agent into a comfort zone of irreducibly high-regret but uninformative levels. The framework's generality (domain-agnostic, algorithm-agnostic) makes it a practical addition to any UED pipeline, and the empirical gains across three structurally different domains (discrete sparse-reward, continuous dense-reward, image-based) suggest broad applicability. Key open questions include: whether the fixed 0.5-0.5 novelty/regret weighting can be adapted dynamically, how GMM-based coverage estimation scales to very high-dimensional or noisy observation spaces (the 'noisy TV' vulnerability), and whether GMM likelihood scores can directly guide level generation rather than only prioritizing replay. The ablation finding that novelty alone sometimes matches regret-based selection hints at a deeper relationship between coverage and learning potential that remains unexplored.
