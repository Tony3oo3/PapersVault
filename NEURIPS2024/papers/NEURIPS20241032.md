## Problem

Despite the proliferation of ethics reviews, dataset documentation templates, and licensing guidelines in machine learning, no comprehensive overview exists of how dataset management practices actually vary across the community. This absence of a systematic account hinders the ability to diagnose fundamental tensions in provenance, distribution, ethics, and licensing that arise as datasets grow in scale and complexity.

## Input / Output

The review consumes 238 full dataset papers (including supplemental materials) from the NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks track spanning 2021–2023, with 2023 papers sampled to reach qualitative saturation (80 of 193). Each paper is annotated along four structured dimensions—provenance (categorized into post hoc, ad hoc, synthesis, and annotation subtypes), distribution (hosting platform, access mechanism, datasheet presence), ethical disclosure (thematic coding of harms and broader impacts), and licensing (license type, placement location, copyright clarity)—producing a per-paper categorical schema plus aggregate statistics and year-over-year trends.

## Method

The authors establish a coding schema by jointly annotating 30 randomly selected papers, then independently annotate the remaining 208 papers with periodic cross-checking and iterative category revision until consensus is reached on edge cases. Provenance is assessed by classifying each dataset's construction approach (post hoc collection, ad hoc instrumentation, synthesis/transformation, or human annotation) and evaluating traceability within each category. Distribution is catalogued by hosting site, access restrictions, and datasheet inclusion; ethical disclosures are thematically coded (privacy/PII, representativeness, out-of-context misuse); and licensing is recorded by license type, where license information appears (paper body, appendix, hosting site), and copyright ownership clarity. The full annotation dataset is deposited in the Texas Data Repository to support reproducibility and future automation of the review.

## Results

Across 238 NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks papers (2021–2023), 57% (136) were built post hoc from existing data sources, yet provenance details (filtering, curation, access method) were often ambiguous. Only 40% (96) included ethical disclosures, and datasheet adoption fluctuated (48% in 2021, 62% in 2022, 53% in 2023). For licensing, CC BY was the most common license (61 papers), followed by CC BY-NC-SA (40), while 36 papers provided no explicit license at all. License information placement was inconsistent: 67% appeared only in appendices/supplemental materials, 23% in the main body, and 16% on the hosting site.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a systematic review rather than a method paper, this work does not benchmark a proposed method against baselines. Instead, it contrasts dataset management practices across four dimensions. On provenance, ad hoc datasets (72 papers) were naturally more traceable than post hoc ones (136 papers), where ambiguous language (e.g., 'exporting' vs. 'scraping') and missing curation details made provenance difficult to verify. On distribution, personal/lab websites (44 papers) and cloud drives (Google Drive, 32; Baidu Drive, 7) dominated over structured platforms like Zenodo (34) and Hugging Face (20) that support metadata and version control. On ethical disclosure, privacy/PII was the most discussed theme, but only 40% of papers addressed any ethical concern, and discussions rarely extended beyond data content to broader consent or contributor rights. On licensing, 72 papers used CC licenses that prohibit commercial use, while 36 papers left licensing entirely unspecified, and software licenses (MIT, Apache, GPL) were applied to datasets despite legal scholars questioning their suitability for data copyright.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first comprehensive, multi-aspect systematic review of dataset management practices at a major ML venue, filling a gap the authors identify in prior work that has examined individual components (e.g., dataset cards on Hugging Face [42], data provenance auditing [28], or ethics disclosures in specific subfields [1]) but not the full landscape of provenance, distribution, ethics, and licensing together. It draws on established frameworks—Longpre et al.'s provenance definition, Ajmani et al.'s ethical disclosure taxonomy, and the FAIR principles—as analytical lenses, and contextualizes its findings alongside data-sharing infrastructures from adjacent fields (biomedical archives, social science data repositories). The paper departs from prior dataset-card analyses by treating hosting infrastructure, version control, and license placement as first-class management concerns rather than documentation afterthoughts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The review provides concrete, actionable evidence that the ML community lacks standardized data infrastructure, with practical implications for conference organizers, publishers, and funding agencies who can now target specific gaps (e.g., mandating DOIs, structured metadata fields, or uniform license disclosure locations). Key open questions include how to build and enforce such infrastructures across heterogeneous dataset types, how to resolve copyright ambiguity for post hoc datasets without stifling sharing, and how to scaffold ethical reflection for authors who struggle to foresee secondary impacts. Limitations include the focus on a single venue's D&B track, the exclusion of main-conference dataset papers, and the omission of downstream harms (copyright violations, harmful content propagation), leaving the full lifecycle of dataset impact unexamined.
