## Problem

Job skill demand forecasting research is hindered by the absence of comprehensive, publicly accessible datasets: existing studies rely on closed-source data, focus exclusively on occupation-level granularity, and use scale-dependent metrics (MSE/RMSE) that obscure model performance on low-frequency skills. Additionally, the dynamic labor market introduces structural breaks in skill demand time series that are rarely evaluated in forecasting benchmarks.

## Input / Output

The forecasting task takes K=6 months of historical multivariate skill demand time series (all 2,324 skills at a given granularity) and predicts H=3 months of future demand values. Granularities range from coarse (L1-occupation, 14 categories) to fine (Company, 521 entities, or Region&L2-O combinations), with data split 9:1:2 chronologically. The dataset itself is constructed from millions of job advertisements via NER-based skill extraction, quantifying monthly demand as the count of postings requiring each skill.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a multi-granularity dataset construction pipeline: collecting public job ads, training an NER model to extract skill terms, standardizing them into a 2,324-word skill dictionary, and computing monthly demand counts at five granularity levels (L1-occupation, L2-occupation, Region&L1-O, Region&L2-O, Company). For benchmarking, 16 models across six architecture families (statistical, RNN, Transformer, MLP, GNN, Fourier) are evaluated using four metrics—MAE, RMSE, SMAPE, and RRMSE—where the latter two address the long-tail distribution of skill demand values. A Chow test identifies structural breaks in skill demand series, and a separate evaluation on those series reveals that FiLM (Fourier-based denoising) is most robust to distributional shifts, while PatchTST and Koopa excel specifically on low-demand skill prediction.

## Results

On the Job-SDF dataset, FiLM achieves the best MAE and RMSE across most granularities (e.g., MAE 12.95 / RMSE 117.17 at L1-Occupation; MAE 1.14 / RMSE 14.01 at Region&L2-O; MAE 1.17 / RMSE 15.87 at Company level). PatchTST achieves the best SMAPE in most settings (e.g., 34.70% at L1-Occupation, 13.50% at Region&L2-O), indicating superior accuracy for low-demand skills. Performance degrades significantly at finer granularities: RRMSE rises from ~37–51% at L1-Occupation to ~77–148% at Company level. When structural breaks are present (detected via Chow test), all models suffer substantial degradation, with FiLM remaining the most robust (SMAPE 38.96% at L1-Occupation vs. 43.78% for LSTM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FiLM (Fourier-based) dominates on MAE/RMSE across nearly all granularities, outperforming the next-best SegRNN by ~7% MAE at L1-Occupation (12.95 vs. 12.28) and by a wider margin at finer levels (e.g., 1.14 vs. 1.05 RMSE at Region&L2-O). PatchTST and SegRNN are the strongest RNN/Transformer alternatives, with PatchTST achieving the best SMAPE (34.70% at L1-Occ) for low-demand skill prediction, while SegRNN's segment-wise iteration gives it the best RMSE at L1-Occupation (108.28). Standard Transformer variants (Transformer, Autoformer, Informer, Reformer, FEDformer) all underperform, with Autoformer and FEDformer showing the worst SMAPE (>69% at L1-Occ), likely because their long-range attention mechanisms are ill-suited to the short 6-month lookback. DLinear is a surprisingly competitive linear baseline (MAE 16.61 at L1-Occ), while TSMixer collapses at finer granularities (MAE 13.96 at Company vs. 1.17 for FiLM). Graph-based models CHGH and Pre-DyGAE perform poorly due to design mismatches: CHGH requires supply-side data absent from Job-SDF, and Pre-DyGAE targets binary skill-occupation prediction rather than demand quantification. Koopa, while mid-pack on MAE/RMSE, excels on SMAPE for low-demand skills (37.84% at L1-Occ), and NStransformer also benefits from its non-stationarity handling. Prophet is the weakest statistical baseline (MAE 29.15 at L1-Occ), confirming that seasonal/holiday decomposition is not the primary driver of skill demand variation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first publicly available, multi-granularity dataset for job skill demand forecasting, addressing a critical gap where prior work (ARIMA-based [28], RNN-based [29], DyGAE-based [30, 52]) relied on closed-source data limited to occupation-level granularity. Conceptually, it repurposes the standard multivariate time-series forecasting framework—applying statistical, RNN, Transformer, MLP, GNN, and Fourier-based models—as a benchmark on this new dataset rather than proposing a novel architecture. The structural break evaluation via the Chow test and the use of scale-independent metrics (SMAPE, RRMSE) to assess low-demand skill prediction represent methodological extensions beyond the MSE/RMSE-only evaluation common in prior skill demand studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By releasing a comprehensive, CC-licensed dataset spanning 2,324 skills across 52 occupations, 521 companies, and 7 regions, this work enables reproducible research and cross-model comparison that was previously impossible in the skill demand forecasting community. The finding that FiLM's denoising-based approach outperforms complex Transformer architectures across granularities and remains robust to structural breaks suggests that for shorter, noisy labor-market time series, frequency-domain regularization may be more effective than attention-based long-range modeling. Open questions include how to incorporate skill relationship graphs (predecessor-successor, prerequisite) as structural priors, how to handle the extreme sparsity of low-frequency emerging skills (e.g., LLMs), and whether cross-granularity transfer (e.g., from occupation to company level) can improve fine-grained predictions.
