## Problem

While LLMs are increasingly deployed in decision-support contexts, their intrinsic decision-making tendencies under uncertainty remain inadequately understood. Existing studies have examined LLM rationality and social behavior but lack a quantitative framework grounded in behavioral economics to measure risk preference, probability weighting, and loss aversion without presetting behavioral assumptions. This gap is particularly acute when socio-demographic features are embedded, as it is unclear whether LLMs mirror human-like biases or introduce novel distortions.

## Input / Output

The framework sends three series of multiple-choice lottery prompts (14, 14, and 7 games respectively) via API to each LLM, optionally prefixed with a demographic persona description; the LLM responds with a single integer switching point per series indicating where its preference flips from option A to B. From 300 such responses per model per condition, the method estimates three scalar parameters (σ for risk preference, α for probability weighting, λ for loss aversion) and runs OLS regressions to quantify how each of 10 socio-demographic features shifts these parameters across models.

## Method

The paper adapts the TCN model (Tanaka, Camerer, Nguyen 2010), which unifies Expected Utility Theory and Prospect Theory, as the first behavioral-economics framework applied to LLMs without presetting their utility function shape, thereby avoiding the circular reasoning of testing PT assumptions with PT itself. Three series of multiple-choice-list lotteries elicit switching points: Series 1 and 2 (positive outcomes) constrain σ and α, while Series 3 (gains versus losses) constrains λ. At each switching point, the TCN utility function generates inequalities in the parameters, which are solved iteratively by narrowing feasible intervals until all inequalities are simultaneously satisfied. OLS regression with binary-encoded demographic features then quantifies each model's sensitivity to foundational (age, gender, education, marital status, living area) and advanced (sexual orientation, disability, race, religion, political affiliation) characteristics, revealing model-specific bias patterns such as Claude-3-Opus's heightened risk aversion for sexual-minority and disabled personas.

## Results

In the context-free setting, all three LLMs exhibit risk aversion (σ > 0), with ChatGPT-4-Turbo showing the highest risk aversion (σ = 0.6031), Gemini-1.0-pro moderate (σ = 0.4959), and Claude-3-Opus the lowest (σ = 0.3085), compared to the human benchmark of σ = 0.48. For probability weighting, Claude (α = 0.7613) and Gemini (α = 0.8759) overweight small probabilities (α < 1), aligning with human behavior (α = 0.69), while ChatGPT underweights them (α = 1.1819). Loss aversion varies dramatically: Claude shows extreme loss aversion (λ = 6.3160), Gemini moderate (λ = 2.3333), and ChatGPT the lowest (λ = 1.4786), versus the human mean of λ = 3.47. When demographic features are embedded, ChatGPT shifts toward riskier decisions (σ drops to ~0.24–0.26), Claude's loss aversion drops sharply (λ from 6.32 to ~2.2–2.6), and Gemini becomes more conservative (σ rises to ~0.75).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing LLM evaluation methods; instead, it benchmarks three commercial LLMs against human behavioral economics norms derived from the TCN model literature. Against the human benchmark (σ = 0.48, α = 0.69, λ = 3.47), Gemini-1.0-pro is closest on risk preference (σ = 0.4959) and Claude-3-Opus is closest on probability weighting (α = 0.7613 vs. 0.69), while ChatGPT-4-Turbo deviates most on probability weighting (α = 1.1819, underweighting small probabilities contrary to human norms). Among the three LLMs, Claude-3-Opus shows the most extreme loss aversion (λ = 6.32, nearly double the human mean) and the lowest risk aversion, while ChatGPT shows the opposite pattern (highest risk aversion, lowest loss aversion). When demographic features are embedded, the inter-model gaps widen: Claude's loss aversion drops by ~66% (6.32 → 2.17), ChatGPT's risk aversion drops by ~60% (0.60 → 0.24), and Gemini's risk aversion increases by ~51% (0.50 → 0.76), revealing model-specific sensitivity patterns to demographic context.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the TCN model (Tanaka, Camerer, Nguyen 2010) and the Holt-Laury multiple-choice-list experimental design—both originally developed for human behavioral economics—to systematically quantify LLM decision-making parameters (σ, α, λ) without presupposing EUT or PT rationality. It departs from prior LLM evaluation work (game-theory experiments, economic rationality scores) that either assumed LLMs behave as rational agents or applied PT value functions without first verifying LLM baselines, which the authors argue risks circular reasoning. The demographic-embedding dimension extends prior bias-detection work (e.g., Gupta et al. 2023 on persona-assigned LLMs) by quantifying *how much* specific identity features shift measurable decision parameters rather than merely flagging qualitative bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first parameterized, model-agnostic benchmark for LLM decision-making under uncertainty, revealing that while all three LLMs exhibit broadly human-like risk aversion and loss aversion, inter-model variation is substantial (e.g., Claude's loss aversion λ ≈ 6.3 vs. ChatGPT's ≈ 1.5). The demographic-embedding results—particularly Claude's increased risk aversion when assigned sexual-minority or disability identities—raise urgent questions about whether LLMs should mirror or correct societal biases, and what their 'natural' preferences should be absent demographic context. Key limitations include evaluation of only three models in a single (financial) domain, and the difficulty of directly comparing LLM outputs to human subjects for sensitive demographic groups, leaving open whether observed disparities reflect genuine LLM bias or artifacts of prompt framing.
