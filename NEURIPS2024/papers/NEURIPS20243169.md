## Problem

Neural Episodic Control's Differentiable Neural Dictionary (DND) was inspired by episodic memory at the functional level but lacked a direct theoretical connection to the associative memory models (Hopfield Networks) that implement such memory. No explicit link had been made between Hopfield Networks and control algorithms, and existing evaluation criteria for associative memory conflate memorization with dataset-level generalization, making it difficult to assess whether a model truly recalls a specific memory or merely reproduces dataset statistics.

## Input / Output

The DND stores image observations as keys and associated values (Q-estimates in the original RL setting, or the images themselves in the associative memory experiments) as values. For capacity tests, half-masked images serve as cues; for retrieval tests, independent zero-mean Gaussian noise of controllable variance is added pixelwise. The output is a reconstructed image in the same pixel space as the stored memories (28×28 grayscale for MNIST, 32×32 RGB for CIFAR-10, 64×64 RGB for Tiny ImageNet), and correctness is judged either by an absolute pixel-difference threshold or by the novel memorization criterion requiring the output to be closer to the target memory than to any other stored pattern.

## Method

The paper proves that DND retrieval decomposes into the three canonical UHN operations—similarity (inverse squared Euclidean or Manhattan distance kernel), separation (a novel k-Max function that keeps only the top-k similarity scores and normalizes them), and projection—making DND a single-shot instance of the Universal Hopfield Network framework. To derive energy functions, a continuous approximation of the discrete k-Max separation is constructed using a thresholded sigmoid whose threshold Θ is itself updated by a second sigmoid-based rule, yielding two Lyapunov functions of the recall dynamics. Empirically, the authors benchmark Euclidean versus Manhattan similarity and Max, k-Max, Softmax, and Identity separation functions on capacity and noisy-retrieval tasks across three datasets, and introduce a memorization criterion (output must be closer to the correct memory than to all others) that reveals k=1 (Max) is optimal for true recall, while higher-k functions only appear superior under the absolute threshold by exploiting dataset statistics.

## Results

On capacity (half-masked input, absolute threshold), Manhattan similarity with Max separation achieves 0.835 on MNIST, 0.451 on CIFAR-10, and 0.669 on Tiny ImageNet, consistently outperforming the Euclidean counterpart (0.739, 0.220, 0.223). Under the absolute criterion, higher-k separations (5-Max: 0.887 on MNIST) can exceed Max, but under the novel memorization criterion, Max (k=1) always achieves the best performance across all three datasets. For retrieval under noise, Manhattan + Max reaches 0.672 on MNIST and 0.627 on CIFAR-10, while Softmax separation generally outperforms k-Max across most conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external associative memory baselines but instead ablates the DND-as-UHN configuration across similarity functions (Euclidean vs. Manhattan) and separation functions (Max, 2-Max, 5-Max, 10-Max, 50-Max, Identity). Manhattan distance consistently outperforms Euclidean distance: on CIFAR-10 capacity, Manhattan+Max (0.451) more than doubles Euclidean+Max (0.220), and on Tiny ImageNet the gap is even larger (0.669 vs. 0.223). Under the absolute accuracy criterion, higher-k separations beat Max on MNIST (5-Max: 0.887 vs. Max: 0.835), but this advantage disappears under the memorization criterion where Max always wins. The Softmax separation function, compared in Section 3.4, generally outperforms k-Max across most dataset/criterion combinations, though k-Max retains an O(log n) computational advantage over Softmax's O(n) cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected threads: the Differentiable Neural Dictionary (DND) from Neural Episodic Control (Pritzel et al., ICML 2017), a non-parametric memory for reinforcement learning, and the Universal Hopfield Network (UHN) framework (Millidge et al., ICML 2022), which unifies classical and modern associative memory models under a common similarity-separation-projection structure. The authors show that DND retrieval is mathematically an instance of UHN, with its Euclidean kernel as the similarity function and its k-nearest-neighbor selection as a novel 'k-Max' separation function. They further introduce a continuous sigmoid-based approximation of the k-Max separation to satisfy the differentiability requirements of the UHN energy function, and propose a memorization criterion that evaluates recall relative to all stored patterns rather than against a fixed absolute threshold.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical unification gives Neural Episodic Control a formal energy-based interpretation and opens the door to importing any UHN instance (e.g., Softmax-based models) into episodic control agents, and vice versa. The memorization criterion is a practical contribution that exposes how high-k or identity separation functions can appear to perform well under absolute thresholds by merely reproducing dataset statistics rather than recalling specific memories—a confound that likely affects prior evaluations of associative memory models. Key open questions include whether the Manhattan-distance advantage and the memorization-vs-generalization tradeoff observed on image reconstruction transfer to actual RL sample-efficiency benchmarks, and whether the derived Lyapunov functions can be exploited to design more stable or capacity-optimal episodic controllers.
