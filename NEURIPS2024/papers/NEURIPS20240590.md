## Problem

In sensitive decision-making contexts, explanation providers may manipulate local explanations (e.g., LIME, Anchors, SmoothGrad) to appear more favorable than the underlying model warrants. While prior work has shown explanations can be gamed, no formal characterization existed for whether a third-party auditor with only black-box access to predictions and explanations could detect such manipulation. This paper asks: how many pointwise queries are needed to verify that local explanations are faithful to the model, and is this feasible in practice?

## Input / Output

The auditor receives three objects: (1) n i.i.d. data points from the underlying distribution, (2) the black-box classifier's binary predictions on those points, and (3) the local explanation (a region R_x and a simple local classifier g_x) provided for each point. The auditor outputs an estimate of the explainability loss L_γ(E,f) — the fraction of points whose local classifier deviates from the true classifier by more than a user-specified threshold γ. Critically, the auditor has no access to model parameters, training data, or the explanation algorithm's internals; all interaction is through pointwise evaluations only.

## Method

The paper introduces a general formalism for local explanations as pairs (R_x, g_x) and defines an explainability loss measuring how often g_x disagrees with f over R_x. The core technical contribution is matching upper and lower bounds on the sample complexity of auditing, governed by a new quantity called locality λ — the minimum probability mass of the local regions across the data distribution. The lower bound (Theorem 4.1) uses a reduction argument constructing a distribution over classifiers where observing fewer than Ω(1/λ^{1-O(ε)}) points in any region provides zero information about which classifier was chosen, forcing any auditor to fail with constant probability. The upper bound (Theorem 4.2) shows a simple split-sample algorithm (validate explanations on X₁ using points from X₂) succeeds with Õ(1/λ) samples. The key practical consequence, demonstrated via a high-dimensional spherical example (Theorem 5.1), is that gradient-based and LIME-style explainers have exponentially small locality in the data dimension, rendering black-box auditing infeasible for modern high-dimensional settings.

## Results

The paper establishes matching (up to log factors) upper and lower bounds on the sample complexity of auditing local explanations. The lower bound (Theorem 4.1) shows that any auditor requires at least Ω(1/(max(ε₁,ε₂) · λ^(1−O(ε)))) queries, while the upper bound (Theorem 4.2) shows that Õ(1/ε₂² + 1/(λγ²ε₁²)) queries suffice, where λ is the locality of the explainer. Theorem 5.1 demonstrates that for a simple high-dimensional distribution (three concentric spheres), any locally linear explanation at a point on the outer sphere must either incur local loss ≥ 1/6 or have local mass ≤ 3^(1−d), implying exponentially small locality in the data dimension d.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or benchmark comparisons. The central comparison is between the lower bound (Theorem 4.1) and upper bound (Theorem 4.2), which match in their inverse dependence on locality λ (up to log factors and tolerance-parameter exponents), confirming that λ is the fundamental limiting factor. The paper then contrasts the theoretical framework against three practical explanation families: gradient-based methods (SmoothGrad, LIME) are shown to have exponentially small locality in high dimensions via Theorem 5.1; Anchors explicitly maximizes local mass for tabular data but abandons this for images, where its effective locality is also extremely small; and SHAP is noted as not fitting the local-explanation framework at all. The practical implication is that in high-dimensional settings, no collective of users with only pointwise query access can audit explanations, motivating the need for a more powerful third-party auditor with model-level access.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing the problem of auditing local explanations in a restricted black-box setting, where an auditor only observes pointwise predictions and explanations. While prior auditing work (Yadav et al., Bhatt et al., Oala et al.) focuses on auditing the model itself, and faithfulness evaluation work (Leavitt and Morcos, Zhou et al.) typically requires access to the model internals, this paper studies a more constrained and realistic scenario. The closest prior work is Dasgupta et al. (2022), which considers a similar auditing framework but is restricted to discrete settings where fidelity is checked via identical-explanation matching; this paper extends the analysis to continuous settings where local explanations must approximate the model over a region. A key conceptual departure is introducing 'locality' (the probability mass of the local region) as a first-class quantity governing auditability, a property that prior explainability work has largely ignored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that auditing local explanations requires sample complexity inversely proportional to locality, and that practical explainers (gradient-based, LIME, Anchors on images) have exponentially small locality in high dimensions—implies that pointwise explanations are fundamentally unverifiable without model access. This has direct regulatory implications: if the EU AI Act requires explanations to be verifiable, the paper suggests that merely providing pointwise predictions and explanations is insufficient, and that a more powerful third-party auditor with deeper model access is necessary. Open questions include what precise level of model access would enable meaningful auditing, how to extend the framework to methods like SHAP that don't fit the local-region paradigm, and whether certifiable explanation methods (Bassan and Katz, 2023) can circumvent these fundamental limitations.
