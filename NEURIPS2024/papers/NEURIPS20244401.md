## Problem

Existing LLM watermarking schemes are designed to be robust to editing, resistant to watermark stealing, and publicly detectable, but these very design properties have not been studied as potential attack surfaces. This paper shows that each common design choice introduces a fundamental trade-off: robustness enables piggyback spoofing, multiple keys enable watermark removal, and public detection APIs enable both removal and spoofing attacks.

## Input / Output

The attacks operate on watermarked text from LLaMA-2-7B or OPT-1.3B under KGW, Unigram, or Exp watermarks, with the attacker receiving top-5 token probabilities at each generation position and access to a detection API returning z-scores or p-values. The piggyback spoofing attack requires only a single query to the watermarked LLM plus post-hoc token editing; the multi-key removal attack requires O(l) queries for a sentence of length l; and the detection-API attacks require O(l) detection queries. The proposed DP defense outputs a noisy detection score where the Gaussian noise is seeded by a PRF of the input sentence, preventing attackers from averaging multiple queries to reduce the noise.

## Method

The paper develops three attack families with theoretical feasibility bounds. First, piggyback spoofing exploits watermark robustness by inserting toxic or inaccurate tokens into watermarked text, with Theorem 1 providing a tight bound showing that more robust watermarks allow proportionally more token insertions (s/l ≤ (z²−T²)/T²). Second, multi-key watermark removal exploits the unbiasedness property: by querying the watermarked LLM n times under different keys and building a frequency histogram, the attacker estimates the unwatermarked distribution, with Theorem 2 quantifying the success probability (e.g., 71% with n=13, γ=0.5). Third, detection-API attacks use the returned top-k probabilities and detection scores to greedily select tokens that either minimize (removal) or maximize (spoofing) the watermark confidence. As a defense, the paper proposes adding PRF-seeded Gaussian noise to detection scores, achieving (ε,δ)-differential privacy that reduces spoofing ASR to 0% while dropping detection accuracy by only 1%.

## Results

The piggyback spoofing attack exploits watermark robustness to insert a median of >20% toxic tokens into watermarked content while retaining detection confidence (z-score above threshold 4 in 94.17% of cases), with 92% of results manually verified as fluent but inaccurate (ASR > 90%). For the multiple-keys trade-off, using 7+ keys reduces watermark stealing ASR to near zero but enables the paper's removal attack to achieve >97% ASR; with 3 keys, removal ASR is 36.2% versus 13% stealing ASR. Attacks exploiting public detection APIs achieve 85–100% ASR for both removal and spoofing with only ~3 queries per token across KGW, Unigram, and Exp watermarks. The proposed DP defense (σ=4) reduces spoofing ASR to 0% while dropping detection accuracy by only 1% (98.2% → 97.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the watermark stealing attack of Jovanović et al. [14], which requires ~2.2 million tokens to estimate the watermark pattern. With a single key, [14] achieves 91% ASR for spoofing, matching their reported results. The paper demonstrates that the standard defense against [14]—using multiple watermark keys—introduces a new vulnerability: the paper's removal attack achieves 36.2% ASR with just 3 keys (vs. 13% for [14]'s stealing) and >97% ASR with 7+ keys (vs. ~0% for [14]). Thus, the paper's method 'wins' in the sense that it reveals the multiple-keys defense is a double-edged sword: it neutralizes [14] but simultaneously enables a simpler, lower-cost removal attack requiring only O(l) queries per sentence. The paper also shows that query-rate limiting can reduce [14]'s ASR significantly, suggesting a practical mitigation that addresses both attack vectors simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the three dominant decoding-based LLM watermarking schemes (KGW, Unigram, Exp) and the differential privacy literature, but reframes the security analysis by showing that the very design choices intended to harden watermarks—robustness to edits, multiple keys against stealing, and public detection APIs—create new, simpler attack vectors. It departs from prior watermark-stealing attacks (which require millions of queries to estimate the watermark pattern) and paraphrasing-based removal attacks (which require trained oracle models) by demonstrating that O(1) or O(l) queries suffice when exploiting inherent design properties. The piggyback spoofing attack is a novel conceptual contribution: it repurposes the robustness guarantee (formalized as (ε,δ)-robustness) as an attack surface rather than a defense property.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental and previously unarticulated trade-off triangle in LLM watermarking: robustness, resistance to stealing, and public detectability each undermine one of the other two, meaning no single design choice is free. The DP-based defense (reducing spoofing ASR to 0% with only ~1% accuracy drop) provides a concrete, deployable mitigation, but the paper explicitly leaves open the harder problem of defending against removal attacks via detection APIs. Open questions include whether these trade-offs generalize to semantics-based watermarks, image watermarks, and whether signature-based fragile watermarks can complement robust ones without introducing their own vulnerabilities.
