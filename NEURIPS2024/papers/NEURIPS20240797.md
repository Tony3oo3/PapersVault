## Problem

Conventional attribution methods for supervised ML are not directly applicable to in-context learning (ICL) because ICL involves no parameter updates, is sensitive to demonstration ordering, treats each demonstration as a multi-token sequence rather than a single data point, and demands real-time efficiency for large foundation models. The paper addresses the question of how to efficiently attribute and interpret which ICL demonstrations are helpful or harmful for a model's prediction without requiring gradient access or multiple model queries.

## Input / Output

The method requires a single forward pass of a white-box transformer to extract hidden states at a chosen middle layer, specifically at the token position immediately before each label token (the 'target position'), and uses the corresponding one-hot label vectors. It produces a scalar influence score per demonstration—either test-influence (relative to a specific query) or self-influence (for outlier/noisy-label detection)—which can then drive demonstration reordering, curation by removing low-scoring demos, or noisy label ranking. An optional Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projection compresses the ~4096-dimensional embedding to ~1000 dimensions, yielding a 10× wall-time speedup with negligible accuracy loss.

## Method

DETAIL reformulates the classical influence function on the internal kernelized ridge regression that transformers implicitly perform during ICL, using the transformer's hidden state as the kernel feature mapping m(x) and the label as the regression target. The DETAIL score (Eq. 6) is the inner product of the test-point loss gradient with the regularized influence of each demonstration, where the Hessian is the Gram matrix of projected embeddings plus a λI regularization term, and the weight vector β has a closed-form solution. To handle the severe over-parameterization (d ≈ 4096 vs. t ≈ 20 demonstrations), they apply ℓ₂ regularization and a random projection to a lower-dimensional space, reducing the matrix inversion to (t−1)×(t−1) for the Gram matrix or d′×d′ for the influence matrix. For self-influence, they reuse each demonstration's own embedding on both sides of the influence equation to avoid position-sensitivity artifacts, requiring only one forward pass for all demonstrations. The entire computation runs in 4–10 seconds on a single L40 GPU for a 7B LLM, making it practical for on-the-fly prompt optimization.

## Results

On the demonstration curation task (removing 10 of 20 demonstrations), DETAIL achieves the highest test accuracy across all four datasets: 74.7% on Subj, 60.7% on SST-2, 55.5% on Rotten Tomatoes, and 41.2% on AG News, while running in only 4–10 seconds per ICL dataset on a single L40 GPU. For demonstration reordering, DETAIL-based ordering improves test accuracy by 1.4–3.0% over random ordering (up to 3.0% on Subj with 3 corrupted demonstrations). DETAIL scores computed on the white-box Vicuna-7b transfer to the black-box GPT-3.5, yielding a 17.9% average accuracy improvement over random curation and a 2–3% improvement in reordering with corrupted demonstrations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DETAIL outperforms all eight baselines on test accuracy across all four datasets in the curation task. Gradient-based methods (IG and LIME) perform close to random removal (within 1–2%), confirming that token-level attribution generalizes poorly to demonstration-level attribution, while also being 50–120× slower. The influence-based method of Nguyen & Wong [48] achieves moderate accuracy (58.3% on Subj) but is 10–20× slower than DETAIL. The concurrent work of Vinay et al. [53] has comparable wall time (~7–11s) but lags DETAIL by 5–15% accuracy on every dataset. Datamodel [15] is the slowest baseline (713–997s) and underperforms DETAIL by 5–7%. BERT-Score is fast (~3s) but model-agnostic, resulting in 8–15% lower accuracy than DETAIL. For noisy demonstration detection, DETAIL substantially outperforms the leave-one-out (LOO) score [19], which performs near random, while being ~10× faster. Attention-based attribution [6] performs comparably to random removal, further validating that DETAIL's kernel-regression formulation is better suited to demonstration-level attribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: classical influence-function-based data attribution (Koh & Liang, 2017) and the theoretical understanding of ICL as an internal optimization process (Von Oswald et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). By reformulating the influence function on a kernelized ridge regression that mirrors the transformer's internal optimizer, DETAIL adapts a well-studied attribution tool to a setting where no parameters are updated and demonstrations are token sequences rather than scalar data points. It departs from prior demonstration-attribution efforts (e.g., [43, 54, 73]) that rely on multiple LLM queries or human annotation, and from token-level methods (IG, LIME) that the paper shows generalize poorly to demonstration-level attribution, by requiring only a single forward pass and operating at the demonstration granularity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DETAIL provides a practical, seconds-scale tool for ICL practitioners to reorder, curate, and debug demonstrations without re-querying the model, with demonstrated transferability from white-box to black-box LLMs (e.g., Vicuna-7b scores applied to GPT-3.5). The main limitation is the need for white-box access to hidden states, partially mitigated by the transferability result. Open questions include whether the approach extends to chain-of-thought prompting, whether it generalizes reliably to non-transformer architectures (Mamba results were inconsistent), and whether the BERT-embedding combination (which achieved the best curation accuracy) can be formalized more rigorously.
