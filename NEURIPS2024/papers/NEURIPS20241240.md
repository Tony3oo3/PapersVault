## Problem

Analytical DRR renderers are fast but model X-ray attenuation as purely isotropic, ignoring anisotropic effects like Compton scattering that degrade image fidelity. Physics-based Monte Carlo simulations capture these effects accurately but require >10⁸ photon iterations, making them impractical for real-time intraoperative use or differentiable inverse problems. Prior 3DGS-based DRR methods (X-Gaussian, GaSpCT) either restrict radiance to a direction-independent function or apply generic view-dependent spherical harmonics without accounting for the specific physics of high-energy photon transport.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D CT volume (e.g., 512×512×500 voxels) and a set of training view angles, and is supervised on DRRs pre-rendered by physics-based simulators (DeepDRR for scatter-inclusive data, TIGRE for scatter-free data). It produces a compact 3DGS representation (~42K Gaussians vs. 93M voxel values) that renders monochromatic 2D DRRs at novel view angles in ~7 ms per image. For the registration task, the same differentiable renderer outputs a 6-DoF pose estimate (3D rotation + 3D translation) optimized against a real intraoperative X-ray.

## Method

DDGS decomposes the radiosity contribution of each 3D region into two spatially distinct Gaussian sets: isotropic Gaussians (g^iso) with a direction-independent radiance c^iso = sigmoid(b^iso · f^iso), and direction-dependent Gaussians (g^dir) with an anisotropic radiance c^dir = sigmoid(Y_{1..L}(θ,φ) · B^dir f^dir) using spherical harmonics without the degree-0 term. Unlike standard 3DGS where isotropic and anisotropic components are co-located in the same Gaussian, DDGS relaxes this constraint, allowing each set to specialize (isotropic for bulk attenuation, anisotropic for scattering near bones/interfaces). Initialization uses a radiodensity-aware dual sampling (RADS) strategy: marching-cubes points at material interfaces seed the isotropic set, while radiodensity-weighted random voxel centroids seed both sets. Both Gaussian sets are jointly rasterized to correctly handle mutual occlusion, and the model is trained with a weighted L1+SSIM loss against physics-based DRR targets.

## Results

On NAF-CT (scatter-free DRRs), DDGS achieves an average PSNR of 44.47 and SSIM of 0.986, outperforming 3DGS (44.21 / 0.985) and X-Gaussian (43.72 / 0.984). On CTPelvic1K (with Compton scattering), DDGS reaches 39.08 PSNR and 0.986 SSIM, a clear margin over 3DGS (37.47 / 0.980) and X-Gaussian (38.04 / 0.983), while using fewer Gaussians (42,625 avg vs. 48,183 and 47,396). For 2D/3D registration on Ljubljana, DDGS reduces mean TRE from 2.14 mm (DiffDRR) to 0.80 mm and cuts total optimization time from 432 s to 121 s, with per-frame DRR rendering at 7.5 ms vs. 30.9 ms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDGS consistently outperforms both 3DGS [Kerbl et al., 2023] and X-Gaussian [Cai et al., 2025] on novel-view DRR synthesis across all four datasets. On scatter-free NAF-CT, the gains are modest (+0.26 dB PSNR over 3DGS, +0.75 dB over X-Gaussian on average), but on CTPelvic1K with Compton scattering the advantage widens to +1.61 dB over 3DGS and +1.04 dB over X-Gaussian, while also using ~12% fewer Gaussians than 3DGS. For 2D/3D pose registration on Ljubljana, DDGS reduces mean target registration error from 2.14 mm (DiffDRR [Gopalakrishnan et al., 2022]) to 0.80 mm and cuts total optimization time by 3.6× (121 s vs. 432 s), with per-frame DRR rendering at 7.5 ms vs. 30.9 ms. Against DiffDRR on real X-ray comparison (Table S2), DDGS achieves higher mean PSNR (25.61 vs. 24.16) and SSIM (0.459 vs. 0.404). On CTPelvic1K registration (Table 6), DDGS attains the lowest rotation error (0.067–0.152°) and translation error (1.09–2.18 mm) across all three test scans, beating both 3DGS and X-Gaussian.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) framework to X-ray DRR generation, building directly on X-Gaussian [7] and GaSpCT [25], which were the first to apply 3DGS to CT-based novel-view synthesis. The key departure is twofold: (1) a direction-disentangled radiance model that splits each Gaussian's contribution into isotropic (Beer-Lambert absorption) and anisotropic (Compton-scattering-like) components assigned to *separate* Gaussian sets, rather than the co-located spherical-harmonics decomposition used in natural-imaging 3DGS; and (2) a radiodensity-aware dual initialization (marching-cubes at material interfaces + density-weighted sampling in homogeneous regions) that replaces the uniform or random sampling used by prior 3DGS-for-DRR methods. The training targets are produced by physics-informed simulators (TIGRE for scatter-free, DeepDRR for scatter-inclusive), positioning DDGS as a learned surrogate for Monte Carlo photon transport.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DDGS closes a practical gap between expensive Monte Carlo X-ray simulation (10⁸+ photon histories per image) and fast but physically naive analytical DRR renderers, enabling near-real-time DRR generation (≈7 ms per frame) that is competitive with or superior to analytical methods in both image fidelity and downstream registration accuracy. The compact representation (~43K Gaussians vs. ~93M voxels) makes intraoperative 2D/3D registration feasible on a single GPU, with a 3.5× speedup over DiffDRR-based pose optimization and substantially lower target registration error. Open questions include whether the direction-dependent term can capture multi-bounce scattering (the authors point to N-dimensional Gaussians as a path forward), how the method degrades when only scatter-free training DRRs are available, and whether the quality ceiling imposed by noisy input CTs can be broken. Clinical validation of the generated images remains an important next step before deployment.
