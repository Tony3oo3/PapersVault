## Problem

Early-exit neural networks (EENNs) accelerate inference by allowing intermediate layers to produce predictions, but the critical question of when it is 'safe' to exit has lacked a principled, distribution-free solution. Prior work (CALM) applied risk control only to prediction quality in language modeling, using the Learn-then-Test framework which is overly conservative and cannot account for overthinking. This paper asks: can risk control be adapted to EENNs to guarantee both prediction and uncertainty quality at user-specified risk levels, across diverse tasks, with minimal calibration data?

## Input / Output

The method is entirely post-hoc: it takes a pre-trained EENN with fixed weights and a small i.i.d. calibration set (as few as n≈100 samples) and produces a single risk-controlling exit threshold λ that governs when the network stops computing. The underlying EENN's input/output varies by task (RGB images for classification/segmentation, text for language modeling, noise for diffusion), but the risk control procedure only needs the model's per-exit confidence scores and, optionally, ground-truth labels on the calibration set. The output is the early-exit prediction (class label, segmentation mask, generated text, or image) produced at the first layer where confidence exceeds the calibrated threshold, defaulting to the full model if no early exit satisfies the risk constraint.

## Method

The paper formalizes EENNs as risk-controlling predictors by defining two relative risks—performance gap risk (supervised) and consistency risk (unsupervised)—that measure the difference between early-exit and full-model outputs for both predictions and predictive distributions (via Brier score). The key technical insight is relaxing the original Conformal Risk Control requirement from monotone losses to monotone risks, which corresponds to the weaker and empirically satisfied assumption of marginal monotonicity across EENN exits rather than per-sample conditional monotonicity. For high-probability guarantees, the Waudby-Smith-Ramdas bound is adapted to permit losses in [-B,B] rather than [0,1], allowing the framework to 'reward' samples where early exits outperform the final layer (overthinking). The resulting CRC and UCB thresholds are selected as the minimum λ satisfying the risk constraint, and the paper demonstrates that UCB yields substantially larger efficiency gains than LTT under identical guarantees, particularly in small-sample regimes.

## Results

On ImageNet with n=100 calibration samples, risk control in expectation (CRC) at ε=0.05 yields a model-average of ~61% fewer layers evaluated, while risk control with high probability (UCB) achieves ~46% fewer layers. On Cityscapes semantic segmentation, efficiency gains reach up to ~62% (mean exit layer reduction) at ε=0.1 with UCB. For language modeling on CNN/DM, UCB outperforms the LTT-based CALM baseline in efficiency gains under identical high-probability risk control guarantees, with the advantage most pronounced at small calibration sizes (n=100). Even with as few as 100 calibration samples, CRC exit thresholds reach near-optimal exiting, approaching the ideal risk line.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is CALM (Schuster et al., NeurIPS 2022), which applies the Learn-then-Test (LTT) framework for risk control in early-exit language modeling. The proposed UCB adaptation consistently outperforms CALM's LTT approach in efficiency gains under the same high-probability risk control guarantee (Eq. 5), as shown on both CNN/DM text summarization (Fig. 2) and IWSLT machine translation for speculative decoding (Fig. 6). The advantage is particularly pronounced at small calibration set sizes (n=100), where LTT's reliance on the Hoeffding-Bentkus bound forces overly conservative thresholds by requiring non-negative losses (clamping negative relative losses to zero), whereas UCB with the relaxed WSR bound (ℓ∈[-B,B]) can 'reward' samples where early exits outperform the final layer (overthinking). The secondary comparison is between CRC (risk control in expectation, Prop. 1) and UCB (risk control with high probability, Prop. 2): CRC permits faster exiting due to its weaker guarantee, achieving ~61% vs ~46% average layer reduction on ImageNet at ε=0.05, but the gap narrows substantially at n=1000. Against the naive empirical threshold (Eq. 9), both CRC and UCB provide distribution-free safety assurances that the empirical approach lacks, while already coinciding in threshold selection for n≈100 due to the small B/(n+1) correction term.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conformal risk control (CRC) and upper confidence bound (UCB) frameworks from Angelopoulos et al. [4] and Bates et al. [8] to the early-exit neural network setting, with the key technical relaxation of replacing monotone-loss assumptions with the weaker monotone-risk assumption (justified by marginal monotonicity of EENNs). The most directly related prior work is CALM (Schuster et al. [65]), which first applied Learn-then-Test (LTT) risk control to early-exiting in language modeling; this paper departs from CALM by substituting UCB for LTT (yielding less conservative thresholds), relaxing loss bounds from [0,1] to [-B,B] to account for overthinking, and adding distributional (Brier-score) risk control alongside prediction-quality control. The Waudby-Smith-Ramdas betting bound [75] is adapted with a relaxed variance parameter to accommodate the now-symmetric loss range.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a lightweight, post-hoc, distribution-free recipe for selecting EENN exit thresholds with finite-sample safety guarantees, validated across five diverse tasks (classification, segmentation, language modeling, diffusion, speculative decoding) with as few as ~100 calibration samples. Key open questions include extending from a single shared threshold to per-layer thresholds, achieving exit-conditional (rather than marginal) risk control, controlling tail risks via quantiles, and handling test-time distribution shift. The practical finding that UCB outperforms the more complex LTT framework under the same guarantees—especially in small-sample regimes—offers actionable guidance for practitioners deploying EENNs in safety-critical settings.
