## Problem

Users of sequential decision-making agents in partially observable settings need to express safety and behavioral preferences, but existing approaches rely on error-prone reward engineering that can produce unintended behavior (wireheading) and do not guarantee user alignment. The paper addresses the open question of how to formally express user constraints over the belief state and whether the resulting parameterized policies can be optimized with convergence guarantees, since the parameter space is continuous and the expected cost function is non-convex.

## Input / Output

The method takes a goal-oriented POMDP (gPOMDP) defined over first-order logic functions, a parameterized belief-state query (BSQ) policy expressed as mutually exclusive if-then-else rules mapping belief-state probability thresholds to actions, and a finite horizon H. It produces a minimum expected cost partition in the n-dimensional parameter space, which corresponds to a set of parameter values yielding the optimal user-aligned policy. Unusually, although the parameter space is continuous (real-valued thresholds), the theoretical result shows it decomposes into finitely many disjoint convex intervals with constant expected cost, so the optimization is effectively over a finite discrete set of partitions rather than a continuous landscape.

## Method

The core insight is that the expected cost of a parameterized BSQ policy, while non-convex, is piecewise constant over the parameter space. This is proven by introducing strategy trees (capturing all rule-observation trajectories up to the horizon) and braids (sets of leaves reachable under a given parameter assignment), then showing that braids cannot be proper subsets of each other and their parameter intervals cannot overlap, yielding a finite partition of R^n. The Partition Refinement Search (PRS) algorithm exploits this structure: it starts with the full parameter domain as one partition, iteratively selects a partition to refine, samples a leaf via rollout, and splits the partition into sub-intervals where the sampled leaf is included versus excluded. Because braids have disjoint intervals, this hierarchical refinement converges to the true braid partition, and PRS is proven probabilistically complete—converging to the optimal user-aligned policy in the limit of infinite samples.

## Results

On the four evaluation problems, PRS-Bolt achieves the lowest expected cost among all user-aligned methods: 4.39±0.04 (Lane Merger), 18.19±0.82 (Graph Rock Sample), 8.52±0.00 (Spaceship Repair), and 19.81±2.09 (Store Visit). Compared to random parameter sampling (RCompliant), PRS reduces expected cost by 80% on Lane Merger (4.39 vs. 22.10), 70% on Graph Rock Sample (18.19 vs. 60.91), and 65% on Store Visit (19.81 vs. 56.64). Goal achievement rates for PRS-Bolt range from 49.8% (Spaceship Repair, limited by short horizon) to 99.6% (Lane Merger), while RCompliant achieves only 41.3% on Graph Rock Sample and 49.8% on Store Visit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Nelder-Mead, PRS-Bolt achieves lower expected cost on all four problems (e.g., 4.39 vs. 4.89 on Lane Merger, 18.19 vs. 19.56 on GRS), with the gap being most pronounced on Store Visit (19.81 vs. 22.39). Against Particle Swarm, PRS-Bolt wins on Lane Merger (4.39 vs. 4.91), GRS (18.19 vs. 21.12), and Spaceship Repair (8.52 vs. 8.69), but Particle Swarm achieves a marginally lower cost on Store Visit (18.95 vs. 19.81). On Spaceship Repair, both Nelder-Mead and Particle Swarm report slightly higher goal achievement rates (52.7% and 52.0% vs. 49.8%), which the authors attribute to a negative correlation between expected cost and goal rate under the short horizon; PRS optimizes the stated objective (expected cost) more faithfully. Against the random-sampling baseline RCompliant, PRS dominates decisively: RCompliant incurs 3–5× higher expected cost and achieves roughly half the goal rate on GRS and Store Visit. Unconstrained POMDP solvers (DESPOT, SARSOP) fail to produce user-aligned policies: DESPOT's policy aligned with user requirements only 7.3% of the time on Spaceship Repair and 0% on Store Visit, while SARSOP failed to converge entirely due to long horizons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the belief-state query (BSQ) concept introduced by Srivastava et al. (2012) for first-order open-universe POMDPs, where BSQs were used with uniform parameter sampling but no formal analysis or optimization algorithm existed. It departs from LTL-based constraint satisfaction approaches (Voloshin et al., Toro Icarte et al., Vaezipoor et al.) that operate on grounded states and do not extend to partially observable settings, and from Mazzi et al.'s belief-state shielding work that relies on execution traces rather than computing policies from scratch. The key conceptual departure is framing user preferences as parameterized queries on the belief state with a provably finite optimization landscape, rather than encoding preferences into reward functions (which risks wireheading) or LTL specifications over the full state.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The piecewise-constant structure of the expected cost function over the continuous parameter space is a clean theoretical result that reduces what appears to be a continuous non-convex optimization problem to a finite discrete search, enabling a probabilistically complete algorithm (PRS) with convergence guarantees. This provides a principled alternative to reward engineering for user alignment in partially observable settings, which is a persistent practical challenge. Limitations include the requirement for finite horizons, the need for users to specify the BSQ policy structure (only parameters are optimized), and the current scope to relatively small problems; future work on finite memory controllers, belief-state approximation, and more expressive BSQs could broaden applicability.
