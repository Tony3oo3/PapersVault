## Problem

Traditional antitrust tools struggle to quantify the economic power of digital platforms, as illustrated by the Google Shopping case where methodological disputes over position bias undermined the Commission's evidence. While the concept of performative power was recently formalized to measure a platform's causal influence over users through algorithmic actions, no practical methodology existed to operationalize it in a real-world setting. This paper provides the first proof of concept for measuring performative power of search engines through in-the-wild experimentation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the live HTML of Google and Bing search results pages, which the browser extension intercepts and modifies in real time (swapping result positions, hiding ads or shopping boxes) before the user sees them. User queries are observed organically as participants perform their everyday searches, and click events are recorded as anonymized JSON objects (clicked element index, element type, page index, treatment group) sent to a local server. The output is a set of causal effect estimates: performativity gaps (absolute change in click probability for a given element under a counterfactual arrangement), algorithmic distortion factors (relative fraction of clicks lost), and a formal lower bound on performative power via Theorem 1.

## Method

The core contribution is Powermeter, a Chrome browser extension that emulates search algorithm updates by performing randomized controlled trials at the display level without requiring access to the platform's ranking pipeline. Upon detecting a search query, the extension hides the page content, parses the HTML to identify result elements by class names and IDs, randomly assigns the user to one of seven treatment groups (control, swap 1-2, swap 1-3, swap 2-3, hide Ads/Box, hide+swap, hide Box), applies the corresponding DOM modifications, and reveals the page—all within ~40 ms to remain imperceptible. A hash of user ID and query stored in browser cache prevents reassignment on page reloads, and the first four days of each participant's data are discarded to mitigate participation-awareness confounding. Theoretically, the paper defines the performativity gap as the expected change in click probability for a content element under a counterfactual arrangement versus the default, and proves (Theorem 1) that this gap lower-bounds performative power, connecting the empirical RCT estimates to the formal power definition from Hardt et al. [3].

## Results

On Google Search, down-ranking the first organic result by one position reduces its click-through rate from 43% to 24% (performativity gap δ¹(a₁) = −0.19, algorithmic distortion β = 0.44), while a two-position drop yields a loss of over 50% of traffic (δ¹(a₂) = −0.27). The combined conduct of adding Ads/Shopping boxes and down-ranking the first element produces a 66% distortion (β = 0.66) for the first result, substantially larger than either modification alone. On Bing, the position effect is even more pronounced, though with wider confidence intervals due to the smaller sample (~600 queries). The authors note that a similar effect size for a two-position shift was independently reported in the UK market case decision, supporting cross-population robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it contrasts multiple counterfactual arrangements against the default (control) arrangement a₀. The key comparison is between individual modifications and their combination: adding a Shopping box alone distorts c₁ clicks by 23%, adding Ads/Box by 44%, and down-ranking by one position by 44%. However, combining the swap with Ads/Box yields a 66% distortion, exceeding the sum of the individual effects, demonstrating a super-additive interaction. The authors also note that the UK market case decision independently reported a similar effect size for a two-position shift, providing external validation. For Bing, only the swap 1-2 counterfactual was implemented (to preserve statistical power), and the position effect appears larger than on Google, likely due to greater spatial separation of results caused by more Ads and specialized elements on the page.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the performative power framework introduced by Hardt et al. (NeurIPS 2022), which formalizes a platform's causal influence over users through algorithmic actions, by designing the first browser-extension-based randomized controlled trial (Powermeter) to measure this quantity on live search engines. It departs from prior position-bias and display-effect studies (e.g., Craswell et al., Joachims et al., Ursu) that relied on observational click logs, regression discontinuity, or platform-internal randomization, by conducting experiments entirely from the user side without platform cooperation. The browser-extension auditing tradition (Robertson et al., Gleason et al.) is extended from passive data collection to active causal intervention, and the results are explicitly connected to the Google Shopping antitrust case as a concrete application.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first independent, experimentally grounded quantitative evidence of how much search engines can redirect web traffic through arrangement changes, with a 66% combined distortion when adding visually prominent elements and down-ranking a result. This offers a practical blueprint for integrating causal experimental methods into antitrust and competition-policy investigations, potentially complementing or replacing contested market-definition arguments. Key limitations include a convenience sample of 85 participants, a very small Bing subsample (~600 queries), and the open question of how to fit performative-power estimates within existing legal frameworks. Future work should address external validity (e.g., propensity-score reweighting to broader populations) and extend the methodology to other platform types and algorithmic levers.
