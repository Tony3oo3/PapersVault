## Problem

Traditional reinforcement learning requires extensive real-world interaction data to learn a policy, which is costly or infeasible in domains like robotics. While LLMs have demonstrated the ability to process textual knowledge, no prior work has shown how to distill a deployable policy network directly from tutorial books without any real environment interaction. This paper introduces Policy Learning from Tutorial Books (PLfB), which tackles the fundamental modality gap between natural-language knowledge and the parameter space of a policy network.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input (1) tutorial books in natural language (e.g., 90 football-related books from RedPajama-1T or GPT-generated Tic-Tac-Toe strategy texts), (2) a brief simulator description |M| specifying the MDP structure (state/action spaces, task, initial state distribution), and (3) a small set of pre-collected real states (7,500 for football) to bootstrap imaginary trajectory generation. The output is a compact neural policy network that maps states to actions in ~0.009 seconds per step, as opposed to the 2.84–4.12 seconds required by LLM-based agents. The policy is trained entirely offline on an imaginary dataset of 75,000 LLM-generated transitions and is deployed directly in the target simulator.

## Method

URI is a three-stage pipeline. (1) Understanding: an LLM-based knowledge extractor converts each book paragraph into Python-style pseudocode representing policy, dynamics, or reward logic; a knowledge aggregator then iteratively consolidates similar pseudocode segments via embedding-similarity matching until convergence, producing a compact code-based knowledge database partitioned into K_π, K_R, and K_T. (2) Rehearsing: a closed-loop LLM rollout generates imaginary (s, a, r, s') trajectories, where a novel state-based knowledge scope retrieval module first maps each knowledge piece to its applicable state subspace (bridging the modality gap between state queries and code), then a knowledge instantiation step rewrites retrieved pseudocode into task-specific code conditioned on the current state; three LLMs (M_π, M_R, M_T) iteratively produce actions, rewards, and next states. (3) Introspecting: the imaginary dataset is treated as an offline RL dataset, and a new algorithm CIQL (Conservative Imaginary Q-Learning) is applied—extending CQL's Bellman backup with MOPO-style ensemble uncertainty penalties on both the LLM-estimated reward and transition, preventing the distilled policy from exploiting hallucinated or inconsistent dynamics.

## Results

On Tic-Tac-Toe, URI achieves a +66% net win rate against LLM-as-agent, +44% against LLM-RAG, and +26% against Minimax-noise (optimal strategy with 30% randomness), without any real game interaction. On the more complex Google Research Football (GRF) 11v11 benchmark, URI achieves a 40.3%±6.2% average win rate and 0.38±0.05 Goal Difference per Match (GDM) across Easy/Medium/Hard difficulty levels, substantially outperforming LLM-as-agent (6.7% win, −0.30 GDM) and LLM-RAG (16.7% win, −0.13 GDM). Inference cost is 0.009 s per action for URI versus 2.84 s for LLM-as-agent and 4.12 s for LLM-RAG, a >300× speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

URI outperforms all LLM-based baselines by a wide margin on both benchmarks. In GRF, URI's 40.3% average win rate and +0.38 GDM surpass LLM-as-agent (6.7% win, −0.30 GDM) by ~34 percentage points in win rate and LLM-RAG (16.7% win, −0.13 GDM) by ~24 points, with the gap widening at higher difficulty (URI wins 32% of Hard matches while both LLM baselines win 0%). Against the hand-crafted Rule-based-AI (Kaggle 35th-place solution), URI achieves a comparable 40.3% vs 56% average win rate and a positive GDM of 0.38 vs 0.56, and notably exceeds the Rule-based-AI's win rate on the Hard level (32% vs 30%). In Tic-Tac-Toe, URI achieves +66% net win rate against LLM-as-agent and +44% against LLM-RAG, and is the only non-optimal method to maintain a positive net win rate (+26%) against Minimax-noise, while all other baselines lose. The ablation further shows URI slightly outperforms CQL trained on equivalent-scale real data, suggesting the LLM-generated imaginary data captures strategies partially superior to the rule-based behavior policy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces Policy Learning from Tutorial Books (PLfB) as a new problem formulation that departs from both standard RL (requiring environment interaction) and offline RL (requiring pre-collected transition data). It builds on Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) as the base policy distillation algorithm and borrows the ensemble-based uncertainty estimation from MOPO, but repurposes both to handle the unique challenge of learning from LLM-generated imaginary data where transitions and rewards are also inaccurate. The LLM's role here is distinct from prior work: rather than acting as a direct decision-maker or generating auxiliary signals (rewards, plans, transitions), the LLM serves as a knowledge interpreter that converts textual books into structured pseudocode, rehearses imaginary trajectories, and whose outputs are then distilled into a compact policy network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a competitive football policy (37% win rate against built-in AI) can be learned with zero real game interactions, relying solely on tutorial books and a small set of bootstrapping states — a capability that could dramatically reduce data collection costs in embodied AI and robotics. The 300× inference speedup over LLM-based agents makes the distilled policy practical for real-time deployment. Key limitations include the implicit dependence on book quality (must cover dynamics, policy, and reward descriptions) and the lack of metrics for evaluating whether a given text corpus is sufficient for PLfB. Open questions include scalability to domains with sparse or contradictory textual knowledge, and whether the approach generalizes beyond games to continuous control tasks.
