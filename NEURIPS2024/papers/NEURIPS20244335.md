## Problem

Increasing Transformer depth yields diminishing returns in language modeling performance, yet deeper models remain the standard approach to improve perplexity. Standard Transformers only pass information forward via single skip connections between adjacent blocks, forcing early-layer features to be propagated through many intermediate layers without direct access. This paper asks whether a DenseNet-style dense connectivity pattern—where each block can directly access all prior block outputs—can improve information flow in Transformers without increasing model size.

## Input / Output

The model takes a sequence of token indices (using the GPT-2 tokenizer, vocab size 50,304) and produces next-token logits via a linear head over the final DWA output. The architecture uses 12 attention heads of 64 dimensions each (hidden size 768) with rotary positional encoding. The only additional parameters beyond a standard Transformer are the DWA weight vectors: at depth i, the DWA module has i+1 scalar weights, totaling at most ~10³ parameters for typical model depths under 100 blocks, making the parameter overhead negligible.

## Method

DenseFormer inserts a Depth-Weighted-Average (DWA) module after every Transformer block. At depth i, the DWA computes Y_i = Σ_{j=0}^{i} α_{i,j} · X_j, a learned weighted average of the current block output and all prior block outputs plus the embedded input, where the α weights are the sole new parameters. Two sparsification hyperparameters—dilation factor k (each DWA only accesses every k-th prior block) and DWA period p (DWA modules are inserted only every p blocks)—reduce computational overhead by a factor of 1/(kp) with minimal perplexity degradation; a 4×5-DenseFormer achieves near-full performance at ~95% sparsity. The DWA weights are initialized as an identity (α_{i,i}=1, others 0), so training starts from the standard Transformer and gradually learns the dense connections. Analysis of learned weights reveals a stable pattern across depths and seeds: high weights on the diagonal and immediate prior blocks, positive weights on the embedding in early layers flipping to negative in later layers (removing current-token bias for next-token prediction), and an aggregation triangle near the final layers.

## Results

On OpenWebText2, a 48-block DenseFormer (1×1) achieves a perplexity of 17.84, outperforming the same-depth Transformer (18.61) by 0.77 PPL. The 48-block 4×5-DenseFormer reaches PPL 17.87 while running at 5.72 batches/sec, matching the perplexity of a 72-block Transformer (17.82) that runs at only 4.08 batches/sec — a 1.4× inference speedup. On PG-19, a 48-block DenseFormer achieves PPL 18.43 versus 18.94 for the same-depth Transformer. The additional DWA parameters are negligible (at most ~10³ for typical depths), and the 4×5 variant reduces DWA computational overhead to 1/20 of the full DenseFormer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard Transformer [31], DenseFormer consistently wins: at 48 blocks, DenseFormer (1×1) achieves PPL 17.84 vs. 18.61 for the Transformer, and the 4×5 variant (PPL 17.87) matches the 72-block Transformer (PPL 17.82) while being 1.4× faster at inference (5.72 vs. 4.08 BPS) and using 45% fewer parameters. A 'Skips with Gains' ablation (learned scaling on standard skip connections) only reaches PPL 18.45, confirming that the benefit comes from direct inter-block access rather than merely rescaling residual paths. Against ELC-BERT [2] (Charpentier & Samuel), DenseFormer outperforms in the standard training regime; ELC-BERT is slightly better only in a very low-data setting but DenseFormer catches up after approximately 1.2k iterations and then maintains a consistent advantage. On PG-19, the same pattern holds: 48-block DenseFormer (PPL 18.43) beats 48-block Transformer (18.94) by 0.51 PPL, and 24-block DenseFormer (19.60) beats 24-block Transformer (20.13) by 0.53 PPL.

## Relation to Prior Work

DenseFormer translates the DenseNet principle of dense inter-layer connectivity from convolutional networks to the Transformer architecture, adding a learned weighted-average (DWA) module after each block that mixes the current block output with all prior block outputs and the input embedding. It is positioned as a static-weight, per-token approximation of CotFormer's interleaved attention over past representations, and contrasts with Depth-wise Attention by using learned scalar weights rather than dynamic dot-product attention. The paper also distinguishes itself from the concurrent ELC-BERT work by allowing negative DWA weights (rather than softmax-constrained positive weights) and by applying DWA at every block rather than only at the final layer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a 48-block DenseFormer matches the perplexity of a 72-block Transformer while being ~45% smaller and 1.4× faster at inference, with negligible parameter and memory overhead (~10³ extra parameters). The stable, interpretable DWA weight patterns—particularly the negative weights on input embeddings in later layers—offer a mechanistic explanation for why dense connections help, suggesting the model actively strips away current-token identity to focus on next-token prediction. Open questions include whether the gains persist at billion-parameter scale, how to efficiently shard DWA across nodes for distributed training, and whether the observed weight patterns can guide even sparser (and faster) connectivity patterns.
