## Problem

Model editing in GNNs is underexplored compared to CV and NLP, and the inherent message-passing mechanism creates a unique challenge: correcting one node's prediction can propagate unintended changes across the entire graph. The paper identifies that the gradient of the cross-entropy loss on the target node is significantly inconsistent with the gradient on training nodes, meaning naive fine-tuning on the target node degrades overall model performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241974/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires three inputs: a well-trained GNN model (GCN, GraphSAGE, or MLP), a single misclassified target node with its correct label, and a pre-stored anchor gradient computed from the training loss before any editing begins. The output is an updated GNN model that correctly classifies the target node while preserving (or even slightly improving) test accuracy on unrelated nodes. The anchor gradient must be stored in memory (O(L) for GRE, O(KL) for GRE+), which is negligible given the small parameter count of typical GNNs.

## Method

GRE formulates model editing as a constrained optimization: minimize the target loss subject to the training loss not increasing and average prediction differences staying bounded. Via Taylor expansion around the pre-edit parameters, this reduces to a quadratic program whose solution has a closed form: the rewired gradient is the target gradient with its projection onto the training gradient removed, then scaled by (1+λ)⁻¹. GRE+ extends this by partitioning the training set into K disjoint subsets and enforcing non-increase constraints on each subset's loss, solved as a small K-variable QP via a standard solver. The key mechanism is that at each gradient-descent step during editing, the raw target gradient is replaced by the rewired gradient before the parameter update, explicitly removing the component that would harm training-node predictions.

## Results

On small-scale datasets (Table 1), GRE+ achieves the lowest drawdown across most architectures: e.g., 0.56% DD on Cora (GCN) vs. 5.03% for GD, and 43.87% DD on A-photo (GCN) vs. 65.08% for GD. On large-scale datasets (Table 2), GRE+ reduces drawdown from 46.50% to 15.39% on ogbn-arxiv (GCN) and from 36.68% to 37.71% on ogbn-arxiv (GraphSAGE). Notably, GRE+ with EGNN-SAGE achieves negative drawdown (i.e., test accuracy increases after editing) on 7 out of 8 datasets. Success rates remain high (96–100%) for all methods, confirming that GNNs are more easily editable than transformers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ENN [10] (Editable Neural Networks), GRE/GRE+ achieve dramatically lower drawdown: on Cora with GCN, ENN incurs 52.24% DD while GRE+ achieves only 0.56% DD; on A-photo with GCN, ENN's DD is 77.07% versus GRE+'s 43.87%. ENN consistently underperforms even the simple GD baseline on GNNs, suggesting its preparatory training phase is counterproductive in the graph domain. Against EGNN [14] (Editable GNN with stitched peer MLP), GRE and GRE+ are compatible and further improve performance: on A-computers with EGNN-GCN, GD achieves 0.73% DD, GRE degrades to 4.62%, but GRE+ recovers to 0.51% DD. Most strikingly, GRE+ with EGNN-SAGE yields negative drawdown (accuracy gain) on 7 of 8 datasets, e.g., −0.07% on Coauthor-CS, indicating that the gradient rewiring not only preserves but slightly enhances test accuracy. Overall, GRE+ dominates GD on drawdown across nearly all architecture–dataset combinations while maintaining comparable or higher success rates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model editing paradigm established in NLP and computer vision (ENN, MEMIT, ROME) to graph neural networks, building directly on EGNN [14], which was the first to address editable GNN training via a stitched peer MLP. The key departure is a gradient-centric perspective: rather than modifying the model architecture (as EGNN does) or applying standard gradient descent, the authors observe a systematic gradient inconsistency between training-loss and target-loss gradients in GNNs and derive a closed-form gradient rewiring solution via constrained quadratic programming. The technique draws conceptual inspiration from gradient projection methods in continual learning and gradient modulation in meta-learning, but applies them in a novel closed-form setting specific to single-node model editing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Model editing for GNNs is practically important given high-stakes deployments (credit risk, fake news detection) where retraining is infeasible, yet the message-passing mechanism makes edits propagate unintentionally across the graph. The gradient inconsistency diagnostic provides a clear mechanistic explanation for why naive fine-tuning fails on GNNs, and the closed-form rewiring solution is lightweight (O(L) memory, no architectural changes) and composable with existing editors like EGNN. Open questions remain: sequential editing still shows substantial drawdown (up to 65%), the pre-stored anchor gradient may become stale over many edits, and the optimal training-subset selection strategy for GRE+ is left for future work.
