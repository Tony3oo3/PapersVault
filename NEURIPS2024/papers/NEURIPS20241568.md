## Problem

Existing search space transfer methods for Bayesian optimization lack adaptive mechanisms to identify which source tasks are truly similar to the target task during optimization, and they cannot dynamically restructure the search space as new target-task observations accumulate. When source tasks are dissimilar, these methods either devolve into full-space search or produce overly loose geometric subspaces that fail to contain the target optimum.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241568/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline datasets from K source tasks (each a set of input-output pairs collected by various optimizers), a black-box objective function f_T over a continuous search space Ω, and a total evaluation budget N. It produces an optimized solution, a dynamically maintained binary tree where each node represents a subspace with a left-better-than-right potential invariant, and per-source-task weights that are re-ranked after every new evaluation. The source task similarity is measured by the Euclidean distance between the means of the best-N sampled points of each source task and the target task, making the weighting fully data-driven and updated at every iteration.

## Method

MCTS-transfer operates in two stages. In the pre-learning stage, it recursively partitions the full search space by clustering source-task samples with k-means into 'good' and 'bad' groups and fitting a binary classifier to define the decision boundary, building a tree whose leftmost leaf encodes the most promising region. During optimization, it follows the standard MCTS loop (UCB-based selection, expansion, simulation, back-propagation) but augments node potential with a weighted combination of source-task and target-task average objective values, where source-task weights are dynamically re-ranked by similarity to the target task and decayed by a factor γ^t. A key mechanism is the Treeify procedure: after each evaluation, if any node's right child exceeds its left child in potential (violating the construction invariant), the violating subtree is deleted and reconstructed from scratch using the updated sample set, allowing the partition to adapt as the target task's landscape becomes clearer. This makes the search space both inheritable from source tasks and continuously refined by target-task evidence.

## Results

MCTS-transfer is evaluated on BBOB (5 synthetic functions), three real-world problems (LunarLander, RobotPush, Rover), three Design-Bench problems (Superconductor, Ant Morphology, D'Kitty Morphology), and 39 HPOB problems, using mean best objective value and mean rank as primary metrics. On BBOB, MCTS-transfer-GP reaches or surpasses the SOTA surrogate-model transfer method PFN throughout optimization. On Design-Bench, MCTS-transfer-GP achieves the best mean rank after 40 iterations in both similar and mixed transfer settings. On HPOB, MCTS-transfer exceeds all existing search space transfer baselines in mean rank, though gaps among methods are small due to rapid convergence. The additional computational overhead (backpropagation + tree reconstruction) constitutes only a minor fraction of total runtime, especially on expensive real-world evaluations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LA-MCTS [43] (non-transfer MCTS-based space partition), MCTS-transfer shows substantial improvement on BBOB, confirming the benefit of incorporating source task information into node potential. Against Box-GP and Ellipsoid-GP [23], MCTS-transfer clearly wins in mixed and dissimilar transfer settings: these geometric methods fail when the target optimum lies outside the convex hull of source optima, while MCTS-transfer adapts its partition dynamically. Against Supervised-GP [17], MCTS-transfer outperforms in mixed transfer because Supervised-GP degenerates to full-space search when source tasks are dissimilar, whereas MCTS-transfer's adaptive weighting and tree reconstruction maintain focused subspaces. Against PFN [21] (SOTA surrogate-model transfer), MCTS-transfer is competitive: on BBOB it matches or exceeds PFN, on real-world problems it is initially behind but ultimately finds better solutions in mixed transfer, and on HPOB it achieves a lower (better) mean rank than PFN. Overall, MCTS-transfer wins or ties against all four baselines across the majority of experimental settings, with the most consistent advantage over the three search-space-transfer baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends LA-MCTS (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2020), which uses MCTS to iteratively partition the search space for black-box optimization, by incorporating transfer learning from source tasks. It also builds on the search space transfer line of work (Wistuba et al. 2015; Perrone & Shen 2019; Li et al. 2022), which pre-learns a promising subspace from source tasks but lacks mechanisms to adapt during optimization. The key departure is making the space partition dynamic: MCTS-transfer uses UCB-based node selection, adaptive source-task weighting based on evolving similarity, and tree reconstruction to continuously refine the search space as target-task observations accumulate, rather than committing to a fixed geometric region or a one-shot classification boundary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MCTS-transfer offers a general, algorithm-agnostic framework for search space transfer that can be plugged into any BBO optimizer, addressing the practical difficulty of not knowing which source tasks are relevant before optimization begins. The adaptive weighting and tree reconstruction mechanisms provide a principled way to handle mixed source tasks (similar and dissimilar) without degrading into full-space search. Limitations include the inability to handle heterogeneous search spaces (different dimensions or domains) and the absence of theoretical guarantees; the authors note that exploring heterogeneous transfer and providing theoretical analysis are important future directions. The framework's reliance on k-means clustering and binary classifiers for space partition also constrains its expressiveness for highly non-convex or multi-modal landscapes.
