## Problem

Classical ML pricing frameworks assume a single model for a single task with a fixed performance metric, but generative AI models are priced per-prompt while simultaneously serving many distinct tasks with variable multi-round user interactions. No existing framework captures how a generative AI developer should set a single price that balances demand across heterogeneous tasks while anticipating a competitor's sequential entry and per-task user switching.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242410/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes per-task performance scores (V_t, W_t ∈ (0,1)) for both firms, per-task demand functions D_t(p) (assumed differentiable and non-increasing), and the number of tasks T. The output is a piecewise-structured optimal price: the firm selects a contiguous prefix of tasks (ranked by competitive ratio κ_t = V_t/W_t) on which to be price-performance competitive, forgoing revenue on the remainder. Under exponential demand, the optimal price admits a closed-form expression involving the Lambert W function, and the first-to-market firm's feasible price set is bounded above by a threshold that depends on the demand ratio and relative performance gap.

## Method

The paper formulates pricing as a Stackelberg game: firm A (first-to-market) sets price q without knowing firm B's response, then firm B sets price p observing q, and users allocate to the model with the lower price-performance ratio p/V_t per task. The key structural insight is that user preference reduces to comparing p/V_t versus q/W_t, which makes the revenue function piecewise in price with breakpoints at κ_t·q, reducing the pricing problem to T single-variable sub-problems (Theorem 1). For the first mover, this becomes a bi-level optimization where firm A must choose q to maximize its own revenue while constraining firm B's best response to forgo at least one task (Theorem 2). Under exponential demand D_t(p) = a_t·exp(−bp), the paper derives closed-form solutions via the Lambert W function and identifies a critical infeasibility condition: if κ_2/κ_1 > a_1/(a_1+a_2), no price q allows firm A to retain any revenue, meaning the first-to-market model must be sufficiently differentiated on at least one task to survive competition.

## Results

The paper derives closed-form pricing solutions for a two-firm Stackelberg game under exponential demand. Under Assumption 2 (D_t(p) = a_t exp(-bp)), firm B's optimal price is bounded by 1/b ≥ p* ≥ κ_σ(1)q (Corollary 1), and the pricing problem reduces to evaluating at most T candidate prices (Theorem 3). For the critical T=2 case, Theorem 4 shows firm A's feasible price set is governed by the Lambert W function threshold: if κ₂/κ₁ ≤ -W(-a₁e⁻¹/(a₁+a₂)), firm A can set higher prices (problem 10); otherwise, a tighter upper bound applies (problem 11). Proposition 1 establishes the key negative result: if κ₂/κ₁ > a₁/(a₁+a₂), firm A earns zero revenue for any price, meaning the latecomer always undercuts on all tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; the 'comparison' is the structural game-theoretic analysis between the first-to-market firm (A) and the latecomer (B). Firm B, who observes firm A's price, always secures non-zero revenue by pricing competitively on at least the most favorable task (Theorem 1, Theorem 3). Firm A, acting without knowledge of B's price, can only earn revenue if it strategically sets q to incentivize B to forgo at least one task. The decisive condition is the ratio κ₂/κ₁ (relative performance gap across tasks) versus a₁/(a₁+a₂) (demand concentration): when tasks are sufficiently similar in relative performance (κ₂/κ₁ close to 1) and demand is concentrated on the first task, firm A is completely priced out (Proposition 1). Conversely, when κ₂ is sufficiently small relative to the demand ratio (Proposition 2), firm A can achieve its unconstrained optimum q* = 1/b, effectively neutralizing the competitive threat.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Stackelberg (sequential pricing) game framework from Hamilton and Slutsky (1990) and the AI pricing literature of Gurkan and de Véricourt (2022) to the fundamentally different setting of generative AI, where a single model serves multiple tasks and users interact over multiple prompting rounds. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a price-performance ratio (price divided by per-task success probability) as the competitive metric, which naturally yields a piecewise optimization structure absent in single-task pricing models. The geometric distribution of prompting rounds, motivated by empirical observations from Chatbot Arena and GitHub Copilot, replaces the one-shot Bernoulli interaction assumed in classical ML pricing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical framework for how generative AI companies should set per-prompt prices in a competitive multi-task market, yielding closed-form solutions via the Lambert W function under exponential demand. The most practically relevant finding is that first-to-market firms can earn zero revenue if their model's relative performance across tasks is too similar, implying that product differentiation on at least one use-case is essential before deployment. Key limitations include the static (single-round) pricing assumption, exclusion of the AI flywheel effect where early deployment generates training data, and the assumption of known competitor performance and demand functions. Open questions include dynamic pricing with model improvement, multi-firm competition, and the endogenous decision of whether and how large to build a model.
