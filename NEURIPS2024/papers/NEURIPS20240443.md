## Problem

Discrete hierarchical clustering is intractable and non-differentiable, while existing continuous relaxation methods (e.g., FPH's Soft-Das) optimize soft scores whose global optima can diverge from the true discrete optimum. This paper identifies that expected discrete scores under a probabilistic hierarchy model provably share the same global optimum as their discrete counterparts, closing the gap between differentiable optimization and discrete quality.

## Input / Output

The method accepts either a weighted/weighted graph (nodes, edges, edge weights) or a vector dataset (feature vectors converted to a dense cosine-similarity matrix with self-loops removed). For Cifar-100, 2048-dim ResNet-101 activations serve as features. The output is a probabilistic hierarchy defined by two row-stochastic matrices A (n×n′) and B (n′×n′), from which a discrete dendrogram is extracted by selecting the highest-probability outgoing edge per node. The number of internal nodes n′ is a hyperparameter (default 512), and the hierarchy is not restricted to binary branching.

## Method

EPH parameterizes a probabilistic hierarchy via row-stochastic matrices A and B and optimizes the expected Dasgupta cost (Exp-Das) or expected Tree-Sampling Divergence (Exp-TSD) by Monte Carlo averaging over N discrete hierarchies sampled via a straight-through Gumbel-Softmax estimator applied to each row of A and B. The key theoretical result (Prop. 4.1) guarantees that the global minimum of Exp-Das equals the optimal discrete Dasgupta cost, unlike Soft-Das which can be strictly lower (Prop. 4.2). For scalability on large vector datasets, an unbiased subgraph sampling procedure replaces the O(n²) pairwise evaluation with M sampled edges drawn from the edge distribution, reducing complexity to O(N·M·n′² + n²). Training uses projected Adamax with simplex projection after each step, initialized from average linkage, and the loss is computed on discrete hierarchies so that the training score directly reflects discrete evaluation quality.

## Results

EPH achieves the best discrete Dasgupta cost on 13/16 graph dataset settings and 7/8 vector datasets, and the best TSD score on 7/8 graph datasets. On graph datasets, EPH obtains Dasgupta costs of 235.50 (PolBlogs), 400.20 (Brain), 74.01 (Citeseer), 176.57 (Genes), 238.28 (Cora-ML), 312.31 (OpenFlight), 456.26 (WikiPhysics), and 30,600 (DBLP), consistently outperforming FPH (e.g., 238.65, 425.70, 76.03, 182.91, 257.42, 355.61, 482.40, 31,687). On vector datasets, EPH achieves the lowest Dasgupta cost on all 8 datasets, e.g., 55.77 (Zoo), 69.10 (Iris), 120.94 (Glass), 1117.58 (Digits), 1230.60 (Segmentation), 2916.17 (Spambase), 11894 (Letter), and 32913 (Cifar-100). On the HSBM synthetic graphs, EPH recovers the ground-truth hierarchy levels with NMI of 1.0 at levels 1–2 and 0.77–0.99 at level 3, and even achieves better Dasgupta/TSD scores than the ground truth itself.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EPH consistently outperforms FPH (the closest competitor, also using the probabilistic hierarchy framework) on all 8 graph datasets for Dasgupta cost (e.g., 235.50 vs. 238.65 on PolBlogs, 400.20 vs. 425.70 on Brain) and on 7/8 for TSD, confirming the theoretical advantage of optimizing expected scores over soft scores. Against discrete baselines, EPH dominates: Ward linkage (338.52), average linkage (355.61), Louvain (344.47), and RSC (307.70) all yield substantially higher Dasgupta costs on PolBlogs compared to EPH's 235.50. Among continuous methods, HypHC (272.81) and UF (331.79) are competitive on some datasets but are outperformed by EPH across the board; gHHC (349.71) performs worst among continuous methods on graphs, as it was designed for vector data. On vector datasets, EPH achieves the best Dasgupta cost on all 8 datasets, with the closest competitor being RSC (e.g., 1117.58 vs. 1119.43 on Digits, 2916.17 vs. 2917.07 on Spambase), while HypHC is competitive on vectors (its original design domain) but still loses to EPH on 7/8 datasets. Notably, WL, UF, and HypHC fail to scale to DBLP (OOM), while EPH completes in ~224k seconds with a Dasgupta cost of 30,600, outperforming FPH (31,687) and AL (36,463).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Flexible Probabilistic Hierarchy (FPH) framework of Zügner et al. (ICLR 2022), which introduced probabilistic hierarchies parametrized by row-stochastic matrices A and B and optimized relaxed soft scores (Soft-Das, Soft-TSD) via projected gradient descent. EPH departs from FPH by optimizing the expected discrete scores (Exp-Das, Exp-TSD) instead, using differentiable hierarchy sampling with straight-through Gumbel-Softmax estimators to approximate the expectation via Monte Carlo. The differentiable sampling machinery draws from the broader literature on Gumbel-Softmax and Gumbel-Sinkhorn for discrete combinatorial optimization, while the subgraph sampling for scalability is a novel contribution specific to the Dasgupta cost structure. The paper positions itself against both discrete heuristics (linkage algorithms, RSC, Louvain) and continuous relaxations (UF, HypHC, gHHC), arguing that expected scores provide the best of both worlds: differentiability for gradient-based optimization and consistency with the discrete objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key theoretical result—that the global optima of Exp-Das and Exp-TSD equal those of their discrete counterparts, while Soft-Das does not—provides a principled justification for the expected-score formulation and explains a concrete failure mode of FPH (demonstrated on the K4 graph). This matters for the broader community of differentiable hierarchical clustering because it identifies a subtle but important distinction between two seemingly similar relaxations. Limitations include the biased gradients from the straight-through estimator, the non-convexity of Exp-Das (no global optimality guarantee), and the fact that the Dasgupta cost's preference for binary branches may not align with ground-truth hierarchies in all settings. Open questions include whether the expected-score framework extends to other hierarchical metrics (e.g., modularity), whether the non-convexity can be mitigated, and whether the approach scales further to very large graph datasets beyond DBLP.
