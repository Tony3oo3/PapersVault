## Problem

No publicly available large-scale dataset exists for training and evaluating ML models that predict early childhood reading performance, leaving the field reliant on small-scale studies that cannot support high-capacity predictive modeling. The standard 'wait-to-fail' approach to identifying at-risk readers delays intervention until after significant reading deficits have already developed, and the inherent diversity of students with learning disabilities makes this a particularly challenging prediction task.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 16 tabular features per student: eight initial reading assessment scores (from Woodcock Reading Mastery, Nonsense Word Fluency, Oral Reading Fluency, and Stanford Achievement Test) plus student demographics, classroom-level variables, and teacher knowledge scores. Missing entries—prevalent at 30.48% across the dataset—are encoded as -1 rather than imputed. The output is a binary label indicating whether a student's end-of-year improvement in word identification or word attack exceeds the control-group average, effectively flagging students who would benefit from additional instructional support.

## Method

MaskMLP is a two-stage training procedure built on a three-layer MLP (hidden size 64). In the self-supervised pre-training phase, missing features are marked as -1, then 25% of the remaining observed features are randomly masked to produce a second input vector; both the original and masked vectors pass through the same MLP to yield embeddings, and a cosine embedding loss enforces their alignment. This design lets the model infer missing information in embedding space without explicit imputation, preserving the integrity of categorical and numerical features alike. In the fine-tuning phase, a classification head is attached and the network is trained with binary cross-entropy on the labeled subset. The key finding is that this simple masking-and-alignment strategy consistently outperforms more elaborate self-supervised baselines (VIME, SCARF, TabNet with pre-training), whose reconstruction or contrastive auxiliary losses in input space hinder rather than help when data is partially observed.

## Results

On the ECRI dataset (6,916 students, 44 schools), MaskMLP achieves the best performance across both word identification and word attack prediction tasks under the stricter school-split generalization setting: 67.26% accuracy / 66.93% AUC (word identification) and 66.97% accuracy / 65.45% AUC (word attack) over all students. On the intervention subgroup, gains are amplified, with MaskMLP reaching 77.04% accuracy / 76.33% AUC for word identification and 77.04% accuracy / 68.69% AUC for word attack. Under the more lenient student-split setting, MaskMLP further improves to 69.19% accuracy / 68.99% AUC (word identification) and 67.41% accuracy / 64.85% AUC (word attack). Statistical tests confirm significant improvement over the MLP baseline (p=0.009) and a trend toward significance over VIME (p=0.052) and SCARF (p=0.052).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MaskMLP consistently outperforms all seven baselines on both tasks under the school-split setting. Against the strongest tabular deep-learning baselines, MaskMLP beats VIME by 0.75% accuracy and 1.08% AUC on word identification (0.6726 vs 0.6651 / 0.6693 vs 0.6585), and SCARF by 0.70% accuracy and 1.07% AUC (0.6726 vs 0.6656 / 0.6693 vs 0.6586). The gap widens substantially on the intervention subgroup: MaskMLP achieves 77.04% accuracy on word identification versus 73.25% for SCARF and 71.25% for VIME, a 3.8–5.8 point advantage. Tree-based methods (XGBoost, LightGBM) and logistic regression trail by 2–6 accuracy points, while GRAPE (graph-based missing-data imputation) underperforms all deep baselines. Paired t-tests across five folds confirm the improvement over the plain MLP is statistically significant (t=3.876, p=0.009), while gains over VIME (p=0.052) and SCARF (p=0.052) show a trend toward significance. The authors attribute the advantage of MaskMLP's simple cosine-embedding pre-training over VIME's reconstruction loss and SCARF's contrastive loss to avoiding noisy input-space objectives when data is partially missing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper simplifies the self-supervised pre-training frameworks of VIME (reconstruction-based) and SCARF (contrastive + auxiliary losses) into a single masked-input cosine-embedding objective (MaskMLP), arguing that the additional optimization complexity does not transfer to noisy, partially-observed educational tabular data. It departs from the dominant missing-data paradigm of imputation (zero-filling, mean-filling, indicator variables) by learning invariance to missingness in embedding space rather than reconstructing or filling values. The paper positions itself as the first to benchmark ML models for predicting pre-to-post-intervention reading gains at scale in early childhood, filling a gap where prior student-performance-prediction work has focused almost exclusively on higher education.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ECRI dataset (6,916 students, 44 schools, 30% missing) is the first large-scale, publicly available longitudinal benchmark for early childhood reading performance prediction, enabling rigorous study of a problem that has been inaccessible to the ML community. The finding that a simple masked-pre-training MLP matches or exceeds purpose-built tabular architectures (VIME, SCARF, TabNet) suggests that domain-specific auxiliary losses may be counterproductive when data is noisy and partially observed, a lesson potentially transferable to other tabular domains. Open questions include the model's notably weaker performance on at-risk and lower-SES students, the need for uncertainty quantification in deployment, and whether the binary progress label adequately captures the continuum of intervention response.
