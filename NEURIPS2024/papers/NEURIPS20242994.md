## Problem

Machine learning models frequently underperform on underrepresented subgroups, and existing data-based debiasing methods like dataset balancing require training group annotations and remove large fractions of the dataset. This paper asks whether one can identify and remove only the small subset of training examples that disproportionately drive worst-group error, without needing training group labels or extensive hyperparameter tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242994/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training dataset (images or text) and a validation set, and optionally validation group labels. It produces a pruned training subset by removing examples with the most negative group alignment scores, then retrains a standard classifier on the remainder. A key practical detail is that the removal heuristic (drop all examples with negative alignment) typically removes only ~20% of the training set versus ~75% for naive balancing, and the per-example alignment scores are interpretable as attributing each training point's contribution to worst-group degradation.

## Method

D3M leverages the datamodeling framework (via TRAK) to express a model's prediction on any validation example as a linear function of which training examples are included, yielding per-example attribution vectors τ(z). For each validation group, the method averages these vectors to obtain a group-level attribution vector, then combines groups using a soft-maximum weighting (exp(β·ℓ_g)) so that poorly performing groups dominate the score. Each training example receives a scalar group alignment score, and examples with the most negative scores are removed before retraining. In the no-annotation setting (AUTO-D3M), hidden subgroups are discovered by taking the top principal component of the stacked TRAK attribution matrix and thresholding projections to form pseudo-group labels, enabling fully automatic end-to-end debiasing.

## Results

On four group-robustness benchmarks, D3M (using only validation group labels) achieves worst-group accuracies of 75.6% (CelebA-Age), 90.0% (CelebA-Blond), 87.2% (Waterbirds), and 76.0% (MultiNLI), matching or exceeding methods that require full training-group annotations. AUTO-D3M, which requires no group labels at all, improves worst-group accuracy over ERM by 19.3 points on CelebA-Age (76.0% vs. 56.7%), 37.9 points on CelebA-Blond (83.8% vs. 45.9%), 23.1 points on Waterbirds (81.0% vs. 57.9%), and 7.8 points on MultiNLI (75.0% vs. 67.2%). On CelebA-Age, D3M debiases the model by removing only ~10K examples versus 30K required by naive data balancing, a 2.4× reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among methods using the same information (validation group labels only, X/√), D3M outperforms JTT by 14.6 points on CelebA-Age (75.6% vs. 61.0%), 8.4 points on CelebA-Blond (90.0% vs. 81.6%), and 3.4 points on MultiNLI (76.0% vs. 72.6%), though it trails DFR by 1.8 points on Waterbirds (87.2% vs. 89.0%). D3M also beats DFR on CelebA-Age (75.6% vs. 70.4%), CelebA-Blond (90.0% vs. 88.4%), and MultiNLI (76.0% vs. 74.7%). Against full-information baselines (√/√), D3M matches RWG on CelebA-Age (75.6% vs. 75.6%), exceeds it on CelebA-Blond (90.0% vs. 88.4%), Waterbirds (87.2% vs. 81.2%), and MultiNLI (76.0% vs. 68.4%), and beats GroupDRO on CelebA-Age (75.6% vs. 74.8%) and Waterbirds (87.2% vs. 72.5%) while slightly trailing on CelebA-Blond (90.0% vs. 90.6%) and MultiNLI (76.0% vs. 77.7%). AUTO-D3M, the only non-ERM method requiring no group labels, achieves worst-group accuracy within 1–3 points of D3M on CelebA-Age and MultiNLI, and within 6 points on CelebA-Blond, demonstrating that bias discovery via TRAK PCA is effective even without annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the datamodeling framework (Ilyas et al., 2022) and its scalable implementation TRAK (Park et al., 2023) to the group robustness problem, a setting where prior data-selection work (Engstrom et al., Xia et al.) targeted overall accuracy rather than worst-group performance. Within the group robustness literature, it positions itself as a data intervention that departs from coarse-grained approaches like data balancing (Idrissi et al., 2022) by surgically removing only the specific training examples that drive worst-group error, rather than subsampling entire groups. The AUTO-D3M variant extends this to the no-group-label setting by using PCA of the TRAK attribution matrix to discover hidden subpopulations, connecting to the slice-discovery literature (Eyuboglu et al., 2022) but grounding the discovery in data attribution rather than embedding geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that D3M achieves worst-group accuracy competitive with methods requiring full training group annotations while removing only ~25% of the data (vs. ~75% for balancing), and AUTO-D3M operates without any group labels at all—making data-centric debiasing feasible in settings where annotation is prohibitively expensive. The ImageNet case study demonstrates that the approach can surface previously undocumented biases (color, co-occurrence) that align with known hard subpopulations, suggesting a path toward automated bias auditing at scale. Open questions include the computational cost of TRAK (T=100 trials with full backward passes), whether the linear datamodel approximation holds for larger or more heterogeneous models, and how the method behaves when the 'bias' is not a single coherent subpopulation but a diffuse distribution of small effects.
