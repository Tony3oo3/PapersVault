## Problem

Stochastic training algorithms (e.g., SGD) cause DNNs trained on identical data to converge to different minima, forming a distribution over the functional space. Despite this stochasticity, the distributional properties of optimized models—how they relate to each other as a family of functions—had received little systematic attention. This paper asks: what is the structure of this distribution, and how does it change as model capacity increases?

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-trained DNN models (CNNs, ResNets, ViTs) of varying widths k ∈ {8,…,448} and test images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet), computing per-sample input gradient vectors ∇_x f(x) for the target class logit. From these, it produces normalized gradient directions on the unit hypersphere S^{d-1}, pairwise cosine similarity scores ρ(k₁,k₂), estimated population mean directions μ̃(k;x) via averaging over M=100 independently trained models, marginalized Saw-distribution shape functions ψ_k(t), and black-box FGSM attack rates α(k₁,k₂) at ε=0.05.

## Method

The paper adopts a pointwise spherical-statistics framework: for each input x, the set of normalized gradient directions {∇_x f(x)/‖∇_x f(x)‖} across all models f ∈ F(k) is treated as a distribution on S^{d-1}. Two hypotheses are proposed and verified: (H1) the intra-family spherical variance decreases monotonically with width k, so larger models concentrate more tightly around their mean direction; and (H2) the population mean directions μ(k₁;x) and μ(k₂;x) are nearly identical across all capacities within the same architecture, making the mean an intrinsic property of the architecture rather than any single model. The gradient direction distributions are modeled semi-parametrically via the Saw distribution p(u;μ)=ψ_k(u^T μ)/Z, where the shape function ψ_k is empirically recovered by marginalizing over the (d−2)-sphere intersection, yielding p_k(t) = ψ_k(t)(1−t²)^{(d−3)/2}/Z′. Empirical verification uses 100 independently trained models per capacity across CNNs, ResNets, and ViTs on three datasets, confirming that cosine similarities approach 1.0 in high dimensions (d=3072) and that the mean-direction attack outperforms single-model black-box attacks. The findings explain why deep ensembles (which approximate the population mean) achieve superior performance and transferability faster than scaling single models.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that the cosine similarity between population mean gradient directions of different model families (varying width k ∈ {10, 20, 40, 80, 160}) reaches values extremely close to 1.0 within the same architecture (e.g., CNN or ResNet), even in high-dimensional spaces (d=3072 for CIFAR-10). The Wasserstein distance between individual-model similarity distributions S(f) across 100 independently trained models stays below 0.035 for all 60 (dataset, family) pairs, confirming that a single model's self-similarity ρ_ind(f,f) faithfully approximates the family-level ρ(k,k). For black-box attacks (ε=0.05), the mean-direction attack using μ̃(160,x) achieves substantially lower target-model performance (i.e., more successful attacks) than both the largest single model (k=448) and the same-capacity single model, across all four model families on CIFAR-10/100 and Tiny-ImageNet.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against existing baselines; instead, it verifies two structural hypotheses (H1: decreasing spherical variance with capacity; H2: aligned population mean directions) across multiple comparison axes. Across architectures, ResNet families show tighter mean-direction alignment between different depths (RS vs. RL) than CNN families (CS vs. CL), suggesting skip connections reduce depth-induced dispersion. Across capacities, the intra-family similarity ρ(k,k) increases monotonically with k, and the cross-family similarity ρ(k₁,k₂) exceeds ρ(k₁,k₁) for k₂>k₁, confirming the counter-intuitive 'cross-family paradox.' In the black-box attack setting, the estimated mean-direction attack (blue) consistently outperforms both the largest single-model attack (red, k=448) and the same-capacity single-model attack (green) across all four families on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Tiny-ImageNet. Ablations across batch sizes {64,128,256,512}, learning rates {1e-1, 1e-2, default}, solvers {SGD, Adam, AdamW, SGD+momentum}, and initializations all preserve the convergence trend, confirming it is an intrinsic property of the optimization landscape rather than an artifact of a specific training recipe. Vision Transformers (ViT) on CIFAR-10 also exhibit the same convergence trend, though with higher dispersion than CNN-based models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Saw distribution from spherical statistics (Fisher et al., 1993) to model the distribution of input gradient directions across stochastically trained DNNs, a framing the authors explicitly identify as a novel topic. It draws on the XAI literature on input saliency maps (Simonyan et al., 2013) and the benign overfitting paradigm (Nakkiran et al., 2021) for experimental setup, while connecting to deep ensemble theory (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) and adversarial attack transferability (Szegedy et al., 2013) as downstream applications. Unlike prior work that studies convergence of a single model during training or the loss landscape, this paper studies the cross-model distribution of optimized models at a fixed training endpoint, using a pointwise spherical-statistics approach.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified geometric explanation for several previously disconnected phenomena: why larger models are more adversarially robust, why deep ensembles approximate a shared 'limiting' model, and why black-box attack transferability scales with capacity. The cross-family paradox (models of different capacities being more similar than two models of the same capacity) challenges the intuitive assumption that stochastic training produces increasingly diverse solutions. Key limitations include experiments restricted to CIFAR-10/100 and TinyImageNet with relatively small models, leaving open whether the convergence trend and mean-direction alignment persist at ImageNet scale or with modern architectures like large ViTs. The aligned population mean direction as an architecture-level invariant also opens questions about its relationship to the data distribution itself.
