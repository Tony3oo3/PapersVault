## Problem

Translating natural-language experimental protocols into machine-executable instructions for self-driving laboratories currently requires labor-intensive collaboration between domain experts and IT specialists, with hand-crafted DSLs and constraints needed per domain. The paper identifies three fundamental gaps between human and machine protocol comprehension: the need for structured syntax over natural language, explicit semantics over tacit knowledge, and linked execution over implicit causal reasoning, and asks whether a human-free, domain-general translator can close these gaps.

## Input / Output

The method takes a free-form NL-based experimental protocol (e.g., from Nature Protocols, Bio-protocol) as input and produces a three-level output: a structured DSL program with explicit operation-condition mappings and control flows, a completed protocol with all latent parameters made explicit (e.g., 'room temperature' → 20–25 °C), and a linked protocol with spatial-temporal execution constraints. All outputs are serialized into a standardized JSON-style key-value format for evaluation, and the full dependency structure is captured in a Protocol Dependence Graph (PDG) that encodes both operation (control-flow) and reagent (data-flow) dependencies as dual, complementary graph representations.

## Method

The framework incrementally constructs a Protocol Dependence Graph through three stages. At the syntax level, it synthesizes DSL programs via a joint EM-style optimization over operation pattern selection and noisy entity pseudo-labels, handling one-to-many operation mappings (e.g., 'add' with 2 or 3 input slots) by sampling from the legal DSL space and greedily refining. At the semantics level, it performs reagent flow analysis using a Pushdown Automaton with random-access memory that implements the reaching-definitions schema, tracking define/kill events for each reagent along the execution order to reconstruct complete lifecycles of intermediate products. At the execution level, it extends the DSL program with a constraint set and simulates partial execution traces to check spatial constraints (resource capacities) and temporal constraints (operation safety) via counterfactual reasoning over the topological order. LLMs (gpt-3.5-turbo) serve as auxiliary modules for named-entity recognition and reagent extraction via in-context learning, while the structural reasoning is handled by the automaton and constraint machinery, making the approach domain-general without requiring hand-crafted per-domain rules.

## Results

On a test set of 75 experiments (1,166 steps) spanning five domains (Chemical Synthesis, Genetics, Medical, Bioengineering, Ecology), the proposed translator achieves average performance exceeding 85% across all ROUGE and BLEU indicators relative to human expert translations. It significantly outperforms the Best-Baseline (combined strongest alternatives) with t(148) = −17.71, p < .0005. At the syntax level, Ours-SY outperforms ConDec-SY (t(148) = −17.07, p < .0005) and DSL-LLM-SY (t(148) = −15.47, p < .0005); at the semantics level, Ours-SE outperforms NL-RAG-LLM-SE (t(148) = −2.52, p < .05) and NL-LLM-SE (t(148) = −3.07, p < .005).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two syntax-level baselines and two semantics-level baselines, plus a combined Best-Baseline. On the syntax level, Ours-SY (EM-based joint optimization of DSL program patterns and entity pseudo-labels) significantly outperforms both ConDec-SY (Wang et al., 2023a; grammar-constrained LLM generation) and DSL-LLM-SY (Shi et al., 2024a; minimal AutoDSL realization), with large effect sizes (t ≈ −15 to −17). On the semantics level, Ours-SE (PDA-based reagent flow analysis with reaching definitions) outperforms NL-RAG-LLM-SE (vector-database retrieval over NL protocols) and NL-LLM-SE (pure prompt engineering), though the effect sizes are smaller (t ≈ −2.5 to −3.1), suggesting the semantics gap is narrower. Overall, the combined Best-Baseline (strongest per-stage alternatives) is still significantly outperformed (t = −17.71, p < .0005), confirming that the three-stage PDG construction provides compounding benefits. The translator approaches but does not fully match human experts, with the remaining gap concentrated in protocols with non-linear or globally-structured descriptions where humans leverage holistic experimental context.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes compiler-theory machinery—specifically the reaching definitions schema (Alfred et al., 2007) and constraint-based workflow modeling (Pesic et al., 2007)—for the domain of experimental protocol translation, adapting them into a Pushdown Automaton for reagent lifecycle tracking and a constraint-based execution model for spatial-temporal verification. It departs from the Chemputer/XDL paradigm (Mehr et al., 2020), where DSLs and their constraints are hand-crafted by domain and IT experts, by automating the entire translation pipeline through a three-stage framework (EM-based program synthesis, PDA-based reagent flow analysis, and partial execution trace simulation) that incrementally constructs Protocol Dependence Graphs. The approach also builds on the authors' prior AutoDSL work (Shi et al., 2024a) for DSL generation and on grammar-prompting methods (Wang et al., 2023a) as baselines, positioning itself as a general-purpose, domain-agnostic translator rather than a single-domain system.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By achieving >85% of human-expert performance at a computational cost of roughly $10 for 75 protocols, this work removes the primary bottleneck in deploying self-driving laboratories: the labor-intensive, expert-dependent protocol translation step. This has the potential to democratize automated experimental science across domains (chemistry, genetics, medicine, ecology, bioengineering) and close the loop between AI-driven hypothesis generation and empirical validation. Open questions include generalization to protocols with highly non-linear or implicit descriptions (where both the translator and human experts diverge), scaling to extremely long protocols, and whether the framework extends to domains with fundamentally different dependency structures. The paper also acknowledges that runtime safety verification remains long-tail and hard to guarantee exhaustively.
