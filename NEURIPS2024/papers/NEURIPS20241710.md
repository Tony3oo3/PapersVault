## Problem

Existing interpretability research on Vision Transformers focuses on token importance, module significance, and feature evolution, but leaves the internal error mechanisms largely unexplored. This paper asks whether Transformers exhibit conjunction errors analogous to those in biological vision—where correctly extracted features are incorrectly integrated at higher stages—and whether such errors can be diagnosed and rectified to improve performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241710/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on an already-trained Vision Transformer (ViT, DeiT, CaiT, TNT, PVT, Eva, or BeiT) and requires RGB images plus foreground mask annotations for the dynamic integration constraint (manually annotated for ImageNet-10, sourced from ImageNet-S for larger splits; omitted for CIFAR due to small image size). The output is a retrained model with identical architecture and no additional parameters, producing standard class labels at inference with zero extra computational overhead. The static integration constraint additionally requires S high-confidence samples per class to establish per-class integration rules.

## Method

Transformer Doctor is built on an Information Integration Hypothesis: early Transformer blocks mix and process adjacent/low-level information, while deeper blocks selectively integrate specific information—dynamically among tokens in MHSA (where the value matrix V varies per sample) and statically within tokens in FFN (where W^(2) is fixed and integration weights z are class-consistent). Conjunction errors occur when these deeper-stage integrations incorporate incorrect information (e.g., background tokens in MHSA or wrong category dimensions in FFN). The treatment introduces two gradient-based auxiliary losses: L_IDI penalizes the sum of gradient-weighted multi-head attention weights on background tokens using foreground masks, and L_ISI penalizes deviation of a sample's gradient-weighted FFN integration weights from binary rules derived from high-confidence same-class samples. The combined loss L_total = L_ori + α·L_IDI + β·L_ISI is applied to the last block during continued training, with no architectural modification and no inference-time cost.

## Results

Transformer Doctor consistently improves top-1 accuracy across all seven Vision Transformer architectures on all five datasets, with gains ranging from +0.72% to +4.09%. The largest improvement is observed on ImageNet-1K, where ViT-Tiny gains +4.09% (64.77→68.86%), DeiT-Tiny gains +3.92% (66.83→70.75%), and CaiT-XXS gains +3.97% (66.28→70.25%). On smaller datasets like CIFAR-10, improvements are more modest (+0.72% to +1.55%), which the authors attribute to feature extraction errors dominating over conjunction errors on limited data. Importantly, the method introduces no additional parameters or inference-time computational overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates Transformer Doctor against seven mainstream Vision Transformer baselines (ViT, DeiT, CaiT, TNT, PVT, Eva, BeiT), each compared to its own pre-treatment accuracy. Transformer Doctor outperforms every baseline on every dataset, with no architecture showing a regression. The gains are most pronounced on ImageNet-1K, where all seven models improve by 2.72–4.09%, and on CIFAR-100, where CaiT-XXS sees the largest relative jump (+3.90%). A blank control group (same training duration without the proposed constraints) yields negligible changes (±0.1%), confirming that the improvements stem from the integration constraints rather than additional training. The authors also ablate the gradient-based multi-head weight computation (Eq. 5) and gradient-based FFN weights (Eq. 7), showing that simpler alternatives (min/max/mean across heads, or raw weights without gradients) underperform by 0.2–2.0%, validating the specific formulation choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of Transformer interpretability work (attention-based, gradient-based, propagation-based, perturbation-based, and projection-based methods) that focuses on understanding token importance, module significance, or feature evolution, by instead drawing on biological vision research on conjunction errors (Treisman & Gelade, Prinzmetal) to propose an Information Integration Hypothesis for Vision Transformers. It maps MHSA to dynamic inter-token information integration and FFN to static intra-token integration, identifying conjunction errors as a source of misprediction. Unlike prior model-improvement approaches that modify architecture or introduce learnable parameters, Transformer Doctor adds gradient-based and rule-based loss constraints during fine-tuning without altering the model's inference-time computation, and unlike model-debugging work in traditional ML or NLP fact-editing, it targets the specific error mechanism of incorrect information integration in already-trained vision Transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a biologically-grounded interpretive lens for understanding why Vision Transformers make errors, and provides a parameter-free-at-inference treatment that yields consistent 1–4% accuracy gains across seven architectures and five datasets. However, the practical impact is tempered by several limitations: the dynamic integration constraint (IDI) requires foreground segmentation annotations, the experiments are restricted to image classification with small model variants (Tiny/XXS/Small), and the improvements, while consistent, are modest relative to the compute cost of 300-epoch fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the information integration hypothesis generalizes to NLP, multimodal, or dense-prediction tasks, and whether more automated diagnosis (beyond manual foreground annotation) can make the framework broadly deployable.
