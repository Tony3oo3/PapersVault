## Problem

Existing neural network encoding methods for property prediction (e.g., STATNN, Neural Functionals, DWSNet) are restricted to a single fixed architecture because they rely on weight tying to enforce permutation equivariance, requiring the network structure to be known in advance. This makes cross-architecture evaluation impossible and limits practical applicability when model zoos contain heterogeneous architectures. The paper asks whether a single encoder can handle arbitrary architectures while still respecting the symmetries inherent in MLP weight spaces.

## Input / Output

The method takes the full parameter tensors of a trained neural network (convolutional kernels, linear weights, and biases) of any architecture and any parameter count as input. A pad-chunk-encode pipeline first flattens, zero-pads, and splits each layer's weights into fixed-size chunks (default chunksize 32) so that arbitrarily large layers can be processed in parallel within a fixed memory budget. The output is a single fixed-dimension encoding vector (32-d for INRs, 64-d for CNNs) that is fed to a downstream predictor to estimate generalization performance (binary cross-entropy) or INR frequency (MSE).

## Method

SNE reformulates network encoding as a hierarchical set-encoding problem using Set Transformer components: stacked Set Attention Blocks (SAB) serve as set-to-set functions modeling pairwise and higher-order interactions among weight values, while Pooling Multi-Head Attention (PMA) with a single seed vector compresses sets into fixed-size vectors. The pipeline encodes each layer independently via a pad-chunk-encode stage, injecting layer-type and layer-level positional encodings at multiple stages to preserve the network's computational hierarchy without requiring a fixed architecture. To handle the permutation symmetries of MLP hidden layers without weight tying, SNE applies a Logit Invariance Regularizer that penalizes encoding differences under functionally equivalent weight permutations, theoretically guaranteeing convergence to the correct minimal equivariance subgroup. All encoder parameters are shared across layers and architectures, and the entire pipeline (encoder + predictor) is trained end-to-end with a standard supervised loss on model zoos.

## Results

On the INR frequency prediction task (Table 1), SNE achieves an MSE of 0.098±0.002, nearly halving the error of the next-best baseline DWSNet (0.209±0.026) and outperforming NFT (0.401±0.109) by 4x. On the novel cross-architecture task (Table 2), SNE achieves Kendall's τ ranging from 0.490 to 0.660 across nine transfer directions, consistently outperforming DeepSets (0.459–0.553) and NeuralGraph (0.352–0.573). On the cross-dataset task (Table 5), SNE achieves 0.730–0.807 Kendall's τ, surpassing the best baseline NFN_NP (0.705–0.780). On the Transformer transfer task (Table 4), SNE reaches 0.4625 Kendall's τ versus only 0.1975 for DeepSets, demonstrating strong generalization to an architecture with ~5x more parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SNE outperforms all seven baselines across every evaluated task. On the INR task, SNE (MSE 0.098) beats DWSNet (0.209) by 53%, NFT (0.401) by 76%, and NFN_NP (0.911) by 89%, while using only 358K parameters versus 1.5M–6M for the equivariance-based methods. On the cross-architecture task, SNE (Kendall's τ up to 0.660) surpasses DeepSets (up to 0.553) and NeuralGraph (up to 0.573), with the gap widening on harder transfers like CIFAR10→CIFAR10 (0.660 vs. 0.507 and 0.463). On the cross-dataset task, SNE (0.730–0.807) edges out NFN_NP (0.705–0.780) and STATNN (0.696–0.788), with the largest margin on MNIST (0.807 vs. 0.780). On the Transformer transfer, SNE (0.4625) more than doubles DeepSets (0.1975), while NeuralGraph cannot be applied at all due to its fixed filter-size constraint. HyperRep, which is trained fully on the target model zoo, is also outperformed by SNE (0.61–0.68 vs. 0.10–0.45) on the Schurholt et al. model zoo, where SNE is evaluated zero-shot.

## Relation to Prior Work

SNE builds on the Set Transformer framework (Lee et al., 2019) by repurposing its SAB and PMA blocks as the set-to-set and set-to-vector functions for encoding neural network weights, and on Logit Invariance Regularization (Moskalev et al., 2023) as a replacement for the weight-tying strategies used by Neural Functionals (Zhou et al., 2023a,b) and DWSNet (Navon et al., 2023). The key conceptual departure is that weight tying bakes architectural assumptions into the encoder, restricting it to a single fixed architecture, whereas SNE's regularization-based approach learns equivariance from data and processes layers independently via a pad-chunk-encode pipeline, enabling a single model to handle arbitrary architectures and parameter sizes. The hierarchical positional encoding (layer type and level) distinguishes SNE from both the flat-vector approaches of Unterthiner et al. (2020) and the architecture-specific equivariant encoders of Zhou et al. and Navon et al.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By introducing the cross-architecture prediction task and demonstrating that a single encoder trained on one architecture family transfers to unseen architectures—including a much larger model zoo where it outperforms a method trained directly on the target—SNE opens a path toward universal neural network encoders that could support weight-space applications like model retrieval, generation, and latent-space optimization without per-architecture retraining. However, the experiments are limited to small CNNs (3 conv + 2 linear layers) and a single small transformer test, with no evaluation on large-scale or branched architectures (e.g., ResNets, ViTs at scale), and the absolute prediction quality (Kendall's τ ≈ 0.5–0.8) leaves substantial room for improvement. Open questions include whether the pad-chunk-encode pipeline scales to billion-parameter networks, how SNE handles architectures with skip connections or multi-branch topologies, and whether the learned embeddings are useful for downstream generative or optimization tasks beyond property prediction.
