## Problem

Channel simulation requires Alice to send a compressed bit string to Bob so that Bob can generate a sample from a target conditional distribution P_{Y|X}, and the optimal rate per use approaches the mutual information I(X;Y) as the block length n grows. However, all existing practical schemes have complexity that grows exponentially in n, preventing them from exploiting the amortization gains available at large block lengths. This paper asks whether techniques from error-correcting codes can yield channel simulation schemes with subexponential (specifically O(n log n)) complexity that still approach the information-theoretic lower bound.

## Input / Output

The encoder receives a source string x^n over an arbitrary alphabet X, a shared random string z^n drawn i.i.d. from Unif[0,1], and a precomputed table of subchannel parameters p̄_i = h_B^{-1}(H(U_i | U^{i-1}, X^n)) that characterizes the polarization quality of each subchannel. It outputs a prefix-free compressed bit string b whose expected length per symbol approaches I(X;Y). The decoder, given b and the same common randomness, reconstructs y^n ∈ {0,1}^n exactly distributed as P_{Y|X}^{×n}(y^n|x^n). The scheme requires Y to be binary-uniform and the reverse channel P_{X|Y} to satisfy a symmetry condition (W(x|0) = W(π(x)|1)), and the block length n must be a power of two (or partitioned into powers of two).

## Method

PolarSim exploits the duality between channel coding and channel simulation by applying the polar transform U^n = Y^n G_n^{-1} to convert the original i.i.d. channel simulation problem into n sequential subchannel simulations whose mutual informations polarize to 0 or 1 as n grows. For each subchannel, a 'toy scheme' is used: common randomness generates a uniform bit v_i, the encoder computes u_i by sampling from Pr(U_i=0 | U^{i-1}, X^n) (obtained via a soft polar decoder in O(n log n) time), forms the correction bit Δ_i = u_i ⊕ v_i, and the sequence Δ^n is losslessly compressed with arithmetic coding at a per-bit cost determined by the subchannel parameter p̄_i. The decoder decompresses Δ^n, recovers u^n, and applies the polar encoder y^n = u^n G_n to produce the output. Theorem 1 proves that the simulation is exact (the output distribution matches P_{Y|X}^{×n} precisely) and that the average rate converges to I(X;Y) as n→∞, making PolarSim the first channel simulation scheme with both subexponential complexity and an asymptotic optimality guarantee.

## Results

PolarSim achieves rates within a small redundancy of the mutual information lower bound I(X;Y) even at block length n=2^12 across BSC, binary erasure, and binary Gaussian channels, with the redundancy shrinking as n grows (e.g., from ~0.05 bits/symbol at n=2^12 to near-zero at n=2^15 for a BSC with p=0.05). In execution time, PolarSim at n=2^12 runs in a median of ~0.009 seconds per block, compared to ~192–246 seconds for GPRS at n=8 (scaled up), a speedup of over four orders of magnitude (ratio λ ≈ 21,000–27,000). The scheme is asymptotically rate-optimal: lim_{n→∞} (1/n)E[ℓ(b)] → I(X;Y), making it the first channel simulation scheme with subexponential (O(n log n)) complexity and a matching optimality guarantee.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Greedy Poisson Rejection Sampling (GPRS, Flamich 2024), PolarSim wins decisively on both rate and runtime: at n=2^12, PolarSim's median rate is within ~0.01 bits/symbol of the I(X;Y) lower bound for all tested crossover probabilities, while GPRS (limited to n=8) requires 192–246 seconds per block versus PolarSim's 0.009 seconds—a speedup of 21,000–27,000×. The rate gap is also substantial: GPRS's average rate is far above the mutual information bound, whereas PolarSim's redundancy vanishes as n increases. Against the Poisson Functional Representation Lemma (PFRL, Li & El Gamal 2018), which provides a theoretical redundancy bound rather than a practical scheme, PolarSim's empirical redundancy is slightly higher at large n, consistent with known finite-length gaps in polar code performance; however, PFRL-based schemes have exponential complexity in n and cannot exploit the amortization gain that PolarSim harnesses. No other practical channel simulation scheme with subexponential complexity in n exists in the literature, making PolarSim the first to combine O(n log n) scaling with an asymptotic optimality guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes polar codes (Arikan 2009) — originally designed for capacity-achieving channel coding — to solve the channel simulation problem, exploiting the known duality between channel coding and vector quantization (Pradhan et al. 2003). Prior channel simulation schemes (Harsha et al. 2007, Li and El Gamal 2018, Flamich et al. 2022/2024) all suffer from exponential complexity in the block length n, preventing them from harnessing the amortization gain that makes the rate approach the mutual information lower bound. PolarSim is the first scheme to achieve both subexponential (n log n) complexity and asymptotic rate optimality for a nontrivial class of channels, by leveraging the polarization phenomenon to reduce the simulation problem to a collection of near-deterministic or near-uniform subchannels that a simple 'toy scheme' handles efficiently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in learned compression of images, model weights, and gradients, where the channel simulation task arises naturally and high-dimensional i.i.d. structure is common. The 4+ orders of magnitude speedup over GPRS and the near-tight rate performance even at moderate block lengths (n=2^12) make this immediately applicable where prior methods were computationally infeasible. Key limitations include the restriction to symmetric binary-output channels in the basic form, and the approximate (not exact) nature of the trellis-coded quantization extension to continuous channels. Open questions include whether non-binary polar codes or other code families (LDPC, turbo codes) can extend the approach to arbitrary channels, and whether the dithered-quantization variant for BSC simulation generalizes cleanly to other linear codes.
