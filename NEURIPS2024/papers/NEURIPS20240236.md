## Problem

Prior work on multi-armed bandits has studied local differential privacy (LDP) and robustness to Huber corruption or heavy-tailed rewards in isolation, leaving the interplay between the two unexplored. In particular, the order in which corruption and privacy are applied—LDP-then-Corruption (LTC) versus Corruption-then-LDP (CTL)—was not characterized, and no tight minimax bounds existed for either setting under high-probability guarantees. This paper reveals a fundamental separation: LTC is strictly harder than CTL, with the gap widening as the privacy budget ε shrinks.

## Input / Output

For mean estimation, the analyzer receives n samples that have passed through an ε-LDP channel and an α-Huber corruption channel (in either order), and must output a mean estimate with high-probability error guarantees. For online MABs, the learner sequentially selects arms and receives private, corrupted scalar reward feedback at each round, producing an arm-selection policy. For offline MABs, a pre-collected batch dataset with private and corrupted views is given, and the algorithm outputs a single best-arm recommendation. The heavy-tailedness is parameterized by a k-th moment bound (k > 1), and the truncation threshold M in the LDP mechanism depends on the specific setting (LTC, CTL, or C-LDP-C).

## Method

The core technical contribution is a unified mean estimation algorithm (Algorithm 1) that combines truncation at a threshold M, random rounding to binary data, and a random-response LDP mechanism at each user's device, followed by outlier filtering and sample-mean aggregation at the central analyzer. This single algorithm achieves tight minimax rates for all three settings (LTC, CTL, and C-LDP-C) with the only difference being the choice of M. The key insight is that under LTC, the LDP channel acts as a total-variation contraction, allowing an adversary to amplify corruption by a factor of 1/ε, which explains the separation from CTL where corruption and privacy effects are separable. For online MABs, a UCB-based algorithm (Algorithm 2) plugs in the private robust mean estimator with confidence radii derived from the high-probability concentration bounds, plus a burn-in exploration phase. For offline MABs, an LCB-based pessimistic algorithm (Algorithm 3) applies the same estimator with a penalty term, achieving near-tight sub-optimality bounds under a finite concentrability coefficient.

## Results

The paper establishes tight (or near-tight) minimax rates for three problems under two privacy-corruption orderings. For mean estimation, the high-probability error is Θ((α/ε)^(1−1/k) + (1/ε·√(log(1/δ)/n))^(1−1/k)) under LTC versus Θ(α^(1−1/k) + (1/ε·√(log(1/δ)/n))^(1−1/k)) under CTL, revealing an extra 1/ε factor in LTC. For online MABs, the minimax clean regret is Õ(T·(α/ε)^(1−1/k) + T^((k+1)/2k)·(K/ε²)^((k−1)/2k)) under LTC versus Õ(T·α^(1−1/k) + T^((k+1)/2k)·(K/ε²)^((k−1)/2k)) under CTL. The key separation: LTC is strictly harder, and the gap widens as privacy budget ε decreases (stronger privacy). Simulations with k=2, K=10 arms, α∈{0, 0.02, 0.05}, ε∈{0.3, 0.5, 1} confirm the theoretical separation and show sublinear regret under non-adversary corruption.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Tao et al. (2022) (LDPRSE), the paper identifies a fundamental flaw: their claimed regret upper bound of O((K/ε²)^(1−1/k)·T^(1/k)) is actually below the true minimax lower bound for large T (e.g., T^(3/4) lower bound vs. √T upper bound for k=2), rendering their algorithm's guarantee ungrounded. In direct simulation comparisons (Fig. 5), the proposed algorithm outperforms LDPRSE under weak Huber corruption across all tested α and ε values. Against Bubeck et al. (2013), a classic robust MAB algorithm for heavy-tailed rewards, the proposed method achieves sublinear regret under non-adversary Pareto corruption (Fig. 4), whereas Bubeck et al. does not account for privacy or corruption. Against Charisopoulos et al. (2023), who study linear bandits under LTC, the paper's MAB regret bound is tighter: their linear-in-T term scales as √α while the paper achieves α (for bounded rewards), and the paper's algorithm is anytime while theirs is not. Against Wu et al. (2023), who work under the strictly weaker central DP model, the paper's LDP model is stronger and enables the LTC/CTL separation analysis that is impossible under central DP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads—locally private MABs (Ren et al., 2020; Tao et al., 2022) and robust MABs under Huber corruption (Kapoor et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2022)—by studying their interplay through the lens of ordering: LDP-then-Corruption (LTC) versus Corruption-then-LDP (CTL). The algorithmic backbone is a standard UCB/LCB structure (Auer et al., 2002; Rashidinejad et al., 2021) whose confidence intervals are replaced by a novel private-and-robust mean estimator built on truncation and the random response mechanism (Kairouz et al., 2015). The only prior works combining both concerns are Wu et al. (2023) under the weaker central DP model and Charisopoulos et al. (2023) for linear bandits under LTC only, both of which yield suboptimal bounds when specialized to MABs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean separation result—that corruption after LDP is fundamentally harder than corruption before LDP, with a 1/ε penalty that grows as privacy tightens—reframes how practitioners should think about where in the data pipeline to apply privacy mechanisms when adversarial corruption is a concern. The tight C-LDP-C bounds (corruption both before and after LDP) address the most realistic deployment scenario. A notable byproduct is the identification of a fundamental flaw in the regret bound of Tao et al. (2022) for locally private heavy-tailed MABs, correcting the state of the art. Open questions include extending to linear bandits, shuffle DP, central-moment characterizations of heavy tails, and locally private robust RL.
