## Problem

GNNs often fail to preserve relational structures among graphs, causing similarity rankings to become inconsistent across layers, while graph kernel methods like WLOA naturally maintain such consistency. The paper identifies this gap by formalizing a 'consistency property' (monotonic decrease and order consistency) for iterative graph kernels, proving that WLOA satisfies it while WL-subtree does not, and then bridging this insight to GNN training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244332/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard graphs with node features and edge structure, processed through any message-passing GNN backbone (GCN, GIN, GraphSAGE, GTransformer, GMT). The consistency loss is computed internally at each layer using pairwise cosine distances between graph representations within a training batch, requiring no additional input beyond the standard graph data. The final output remains a graph class label, but the intermediate representations are regularized to maintain stable similarity orderings across layers.

## Method

The paper defines iterative graph kernels (IGK) and formalizes two key properties—monotonic decrease and order consistency—proving via PAC-learning that kernels satisfying both yield monotonically decreasing test error bounds. It then shows WLOA asymptotically satisfies both properties while WL-subtree violates them, explaining WLOA's empirical superiority. For GNNs, the authors propose a model-agnostic consistency loss: for each reference graph x_k in a batch, it computes predicted probabilities (via sigmoid of pairwise cosine distance differences) that x_k is closer to x_n than x_m at layer h, and minimizes cross-entropy against the known ordering from layer h−1. This loss is added to the standard classification objective with a tunable weight λ, requiring no architectural changes and only O(batch_size²) extra memory per layer.

## Results

The proposed consistency loss (L_consistency) is a model-agnostic add-on that improves graph classification across all five GNN backbones (GCN, GIN, GraphSAGE, GTransformer, GMT) on 10 datasets. GIN shows the largest gains, with improvements of up to 4.51% on D&D, 4.32% on COLLAB, and 3.70% on IMDB-B. The method also scales to large-scale and multi-class settings, yielding gains on ogbg-molhiv (41K graphs, ROC-AUC) and COIL-RAG (100 classes). Spearman rank correlation between consecutive GNN layers increases substantially (e.g., GIN on NCI1: 0.666→0.877; GCN on PROTEINS: 0.584→0.946), confirming that the loss enforces the intended consistency property.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares L_consistency added to five GNN backbones: GCN (Kipf & Welling, ICLR 2017), GIN (Xu et al., ICLR 2019), GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., NeurIPS 2017), GTransformer (Shi et al., IJCAI 2021), and GMT (Baek et al., ICLR 2021). GIN benefits the most, with the consistency loss closing or reversing gaps on most datasets (e.g., NCI1: 74.40→78.26, COLLAB: 78.92→82.12, COIL-RAG: 89.56→92.31). GCN shows mixed results: strong gains on NCI1 (+4.17) and COIL-RAG (+1.61) but slight drops on D&D and IMDB-B, suggesting GCN's spectral message-passing already partially preserves some relational structure. GraphSAGE and GTransformer (which share identical numbers in the reported table) show consistent moderate improvements, particularly on COIL-RAG (83.74→91.67) and REDDIT-T. GMT, which uses multiset pooling, shows the smallest gains on most datasets but still improves on COLLAB (80.36→82.80) and REDDIT-T (72.06→77.19). The paper notes that where base models already exhibit high inter-layer rank correlation (GCN on NCI109, GMT on IMDB-B), the consistency loss provides minimal additional benefit, confirming that the loss targets a specific representational deficiency rather than acting as a universal regularizer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a formal bridge between Weisfeiler-Lehman graph kernels and message-passing GNNs by defining a class of 'iterative graph kernels' and proving that the WLOA kernel (Kriege et al., 2016) satisfies monotonic decrease and asymptotic order consistency while the WL-subtree kernel (Shervashidze et al., 2011) does not. Rather than incorporating kernels directly into GNN architectures (as in Nikolentzos et al., 2020 or Lee et al., 2024), the authors extract a *training principle*—preserving similarity rankings across layers—and implement it as a probabilistic pairwise-ranking loss inspired by Burges et al. (2005). The analogy between GNN message-passing and WL relabeling (Xu et al., 2019) provides the conceptual justification for transferring kernel properties to neural network training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The consistency loss offers a simple, model-agnostic regularization that practitioners can add to any message-passing GNN with minimal architectural changes, and the theoretical analysis explains *why* it helps (monotonically decreasing error bound under order consistency). However, the method is validated only on graph classification; whether the principle transfers to node-level or link-level tasks, or to non-iterative architectures like attention-based GNNs, remains open. The O(batch_size²) cost of the loss also raises scalability questions for very large-batch training, and the theoretical guarantees are established only for binary classification with uniform-norm representations.
