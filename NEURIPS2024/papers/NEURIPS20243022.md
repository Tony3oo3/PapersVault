## Problem

Graph prompt learning has emerged as a promising alternative to the 'Pre-train & Fine-tune' paradigm for graph neural networks, but the field lacks a unified taxonomy for diverse prompt methods, a standardized benchmark for fair comparison, and an easy-to-use toolkit for practical deployment. Existing works use inconsistent experimental setups, varying tasks, metrics, and data-splitting techniques, obstructing a comprehensive understanding of when and why graph prompts succeed or fail.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a graph (adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N} and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×d}), a frozen pre-trained GNN backbone, and k-shot labels (k ≤ 10 per class) as input, and produces node or graph class predictions evaluated by accuracy, macro F1, and AUROC. A key constraint is that the pre-trained backbone remains frozen during prompt tuning—only lightweight prompt parameters (e.g., K prompt tokens with K×d learnable representations) are optimized, making the approach far more parameter-efficient than fine-tuning the full backbone. Evaluation spans 15 datasets covering homophilic, heterophilic, large-scale, and pure-structural graphs across node-level and graph-level classification.

## Method

The paper proposes a unified framework that decomposes any graph prompt into three components—prompt tokens (P ∈ R^{K×d}), token structure (A_inner ∈ {0,1}^{K×K}), and insert patterns (A_cross ∈ {0,1}^{K×N})—and categorizes existing methods into two branches: 'prompt as graph' (e.g., All-in-one, which learns insert patterns connecting prompt subgraphs to original nodes) and 'prompt as token' (e.g., GPPT, Gprompt, GPF, GPF-plus, which add learnable vectors to node features or graph pooling). The benchmark systematically evaluates 6 pre-training methods (DGI, GraphMAE, EdgePreGPPT, EdgePreGprompt, GCL, SimGRACE) crossed with 5 prompt methods across 15 datasets under 1/3/5-shot settings, measuring effectiveness, flexibility (via error-bound analysis of graph manipulations), and efficiency (training time and tunable parameter count). The ProG open-source library implements all methods in a unified PyTorch/PyG pipeline with standardized data splits, hyperparameter search, and evaluation metrics to enable objective comparison.

## Results

On 1-shot node classification across 7 datasets, graph prompt methods consistently outperform both supervised GCN and Pre-train & Fine-tune baselines. GPF-plus achieves the highest accuracy on Wisconsin (91.03%), Texas (95.83%), and Actor (29.32%), while Gprompt leads on ogbn-arxiv (75.72%) and GPF on ogbn-arxiv (78.37%). On 1-shot graph classification across 8 datasets, All-in-one achieves the best results on 7 out of 8 datasets, including MUTAG (79.87%), BZR (79.20%), and PROTEINS (66.49%). In flexibility analysis, All-in-one achieves a 95.06% reduction in error bound compared to the original manipulation error, demonstrating the strongest adaptability to graph transformations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All five graph prompt methods (GPPT, All-in-one, Gprompt, GPF, GPF-plus) consistently outperform the supervised GCN baseline across nearly all 15 datasets, confirming positive transfer from pre-training. Against the best Pre-train & Fine-tune variant, graph prompts win on most datasets: GPF-plus surpasses fine-tuning by ~50 points on Wisconsin (91.03 vs. 40.69) and Texas (95.83 vs. 46.88), while All-in-one leads on graph tasks like MUTAG (79.87 vs. 65.47) and BZR (79.20 vs. 34.69). Among prompt methods, GPF-plus is the strongest for node classification (best on 4/7 datasets), while All-in-one dominates graph classification (best on 7/8 datasets). GPPT underperforms on heterophilic graphs (Wisconsin: 30.40, Texas: 31.81) and large-scale ogbn-arxiv (14.65), likely due to instability in its k-means clustering. A key finding is that GPF-plus eliminates negative transfer entirely (reducing the rate from 43% to 0% in node tasks), and All-in-one does the same for graph tasks (38% to 0%), whereas classical fine-tuning still suffers negative transfer on Wisconsin and several graph datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes the fragmented landscape of graph prompt learning, which has emerged as an alternative to the classical 'Pre-train & Fine-tune' paradigm for GNNs. Rather than proposing a new prompt method, it introduces a unified taxonomy that decomposes all existing graph prompts into three components (prompt tokens, token structure, insert patterns) and categorizes them into 'prompt as graph' (e.g., All-in-one) and 'prompt as token' (e.g., GPPT, Gprompt, GPF, GPF-plus). The benchmark draws on the NLP prompt learning lineage (prefix-tuning, discrete prompts) and applies it systematically to the graph domain, while the pre-training baselines span node-level (DGI, GraphMAE), edge-level (EdgePreGPPT, EdgePreGprompt), and graph-level (GCL, SimGRACE) strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first comprehensive benchmark for graph prompt learning, ProG fills a critical gap by providing standardized evaluation across 15 datasets, 6 pre-training methods, and 5 prompt techniques, enabling fair comparisons that were previously impossible due to inconsistent experimental setups. The finding that graph prompts can fully reverse negative transfer (reducing rates from 43%/38% to 0%) has practical implications for deployment in few-shot settings. Open questions remain around scalability to very large graphs, handling pure structural datasets without node features, and whether the unified taxonomy generalizes to emerging prompt variants. The authors also note that prompt performance diminishes as shot count increases, suggesting the approach is inherently suited to data-scarce regimes.
