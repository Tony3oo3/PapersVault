## Problem

Surveys have become a popular tool for measuring which human demographic subgroups LLMs best represent, but it was unclear whether prompting models with survey questions yields responses that meaningfully reflect human populations. This paper systematically examines whether the de-facto standard survey-prompting methodology produces valid, interpretable results, or whether systematic biases in how models process multiple-choice formats confound the conclusions drawn from prior alignment studies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241457/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 25 multiple-choice questions from the 2019 American Community Survey (and later ATP, GAS/WVS, and ANES questionnaires) and prompts 43 language models (110M–175B parameters) using a standard 'Question: ... A. ... B. ... Answer:' format. The model's next-token probabilities over the answer-choice labels (A, B, C, etc.) are extracted and renormalized to form a categorical distribution over answers. This distribution is then characterized by normalized entropy, KL divergence to census reference populations, and A-bias (deviation from uniform choice-label selection). Under randomized choice ordering, the expected distribution over answers (the 'adjusted response') is computed by averaging over all permutations of answer-to-label assignments.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic diagnostic of survey-prompting methodology rather than a new model or training procedure. First, it identifies that all surveyed models exhibit strong A-bias and other ordering/labeling biases that make response distributions depend on superficial formatting rather than question content, with entropy increasing log-linearly with model size independent of the question asked. Second, it applies randomized choice ordering (averaging over all answer permutations) to obtain 'adjusted responses,' revealing that base models produce near-uniform distributions regardless of size or question. Third, it compares adjusted responses to the U.S. Census via average KL divergence, showing models are consistently closer to a uniform baseline than to any human population or state. Finally, it demonstrates that survey-derived alignment metrics correlate linearly with the entropy of the reference subgroup's responses, explaining prior findings (e.g., models appearing aligned with 'moderate' or 'lower-income' groups) as artifacts of those subgroups having near-uniform answer distributions rather than genuine representational properties of the model.

## Results

Across 43 language models (110M–175B parameters) prompted with 25 American Community Survey questions, base models' normalized response entropy increases log-linearly with model size under naive prompting, but after randomized choice-ordering adjustment, entropy collapses to ≈1 (uniform) regardless of model size or question. Models' adjusted responses are consistently closer to a uniform baseline than to the U.S. census or any individual state; even GPT-4, the most similar model to human data, is closer to the census than to uniform on only 6 of 25 questions (24%). A binary XGBoost discriminator separates model-generated synthetic ACS responses from real census data with >90% accuracy (TV distance >0.9), and >99% accuracy on the ANES survey, confirming that LLM survey responses do not qualitatively resemble human population data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between LLM survey responses and three reference baselines: the overall U.S. census population, 50 individual U.S. state populations, and a uniform distribution over answer choices. All 43 models, regardless of size or fine-tuning, are substantially closer to the uniform baseline than to any human reference population in terms of average KL divergence across questions. Instruction tuning does not consistently reduce divergence to the census; only models above ~70B parameters (e.g., Llama 2 70B Chat, Llama 3 Instruct) show a clear decrease in divergence after instruction tuning, yet even these remain closer to uniform than to the census. The paper further demonstrates that survey-derived alignment metrics (as used by Santurkar et al. 2023 and Durmus et al. 2023) are driven by the entropy of the reference subgroup rather than by model-specific properties: subgroups with more balanced (higher-entropy) responses always appear more 'aligned' with any given model. This pattern holds across the ACS, American Trends Panel, Global Attitudes Surveys, and World Values Surveys, and persists both before and after adjustment for ordering biases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper critically examines the survey-based LLM evaluation methodology popularized by Santurkar et al. (2023) and extended by Durmus et al. (2023) and Argyle et al. (2023), which prompt models with multiple-choice survey questions and compare their answer distributions to human reference populations. It adopts the randomized choice ordering adjustment from Robinson and Wingate (2023) to disentangle genuine model preferences from systematic labeling and position biases. Rather than proposing a new evaluation framework, the paper's contribution is a systematic diagnostic: it shows that the methodology itself introduces confounds (A-bias, ordering bias) that dominate model responses and render the resulting 'alignment' metrics largely uninformative about which populations models actually represent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that, after adjusting for ordering biases, all 43 models produce near-uniform responses regardless of size, pre-training data, or instruction tuning fundamentally undermines the interpretability of survey-derived alignment metrics that have been widely cited in the literature. This means that prior conclusions about which demographic subgroups LLMs 'best represent' may be artifacts of subgroup entropy rather than genuine model properties. The paper leaves open the important question of how to design evaluation methodologies that can actually elicit meaningful, non-uniform signal from LLMs about their encoded biases and represented populations, and whether the multiple-choice prompting paradigm is fundamentally unsuitable for this purpose.
