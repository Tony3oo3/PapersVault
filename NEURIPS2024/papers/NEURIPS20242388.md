## Problem

Event-based cameras produce asynchronous event streams that must be sliced into groups before conversion to neural-network-compatible representations. Existing slicing methods rely on fixed time intervals or fixed event counts, which cause information loss in low-speed motion and redundancy in high-speed motion, and require task-specific hyperparameter tuning. No fully learnable, content-adaptive slicing mechanism existed that could dynamically determine optimal cut points based on the event stream itself.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a raw asynchronous event stream (position, timestamp, polarity) that is first discretized into a sequence of event cells—each a single small time-interval (δt) voxel grid—fed sequentially into the SNN. The SNN outputs a binary spike signal whose timing determines slice boundaries, producing a variable-length sequence of sub-event groups. These groups can then be converted into any standard event representation (frame, voxel grid, time surface, event spike tensor, point cloud, or graph) for downstream ANNs, making the slicer plug-and-play across representation choices.

## Method

SpikeSlicer uses a lightweight SNN (three conv + two linear layers with IF neurons) as a sequential event trigger: event cells are fed one-by-one in a loop, and a spike from the final LIF neuron marks a slice boundary. Training is guided by the Spiking Position-aware Loss (SPA-Loss), which combines a Membrane Potential-driven Loss (supervising the no-reset membrane potential U[n*] to a target bounded between V_th and max(βV_th + γI[n*], V_th) to guarantee spiking exactly at n*) and a Linear-assuming Loss (enforcing monotonic linear increase of membrane potential to counteract the 'hill effect' where earlier potentials fire prematurely). A Feedback-Update strategy closes the loop: a neighborhood search around the SNN's spike time generates candidate event representations, the downstream ANN's loss on each candidate selects the optimal slice index n*, and this label drives SPA-Loss backpropagation; the process iterates in two stages (train SNN with ANN feedback, then fine-tune ANN on newly sliced events). A dynamic hyperparameter tuning rule updates α in the same direction as the desired spike index to avoid premature or delayed spiking.

## Results

On the FE108 tracking benchmark (ALL scenario), TransT+SpikeSlicer-S achieves the best overall RSR of 63.6 and OP.50 of 82.3, improving over the original TransT (RSR 61.3, OP.50 78.3) and substantially over the fixed-event baseline (RSR 51.0, OP.50 59.0). On recognition tasks, ResNet-34 with SpikeSlicer reaches 96.18% on DVS-Gesture, 82.54% on N-Caltech101, 82.23% on DVS-CIFAR10, and 89.93% on SL-Animals, outperforming both fixed-slice and random-slice baselines by 2.8–6.5 percentage points. The method adds only 0.85 mJ energy and 0.73G OPs (SpikeSlicer-Base) while delivering up to 11.9% RSR gain in tracking and 19.2% accuracy gain in recognition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates SpikeSlicer as a plug-and-play add-on to seven existing trackers (SiamFC++, KYS, CLNet, DiMP, PrDiMP, TaMOs, TransT) on FE108. Against the original (fixed-slice) versions of each tracker, SpikeSlicer-S consistently improves RSR: DiMP gains +7.5 (52.1→59.6), PrDiMP gains +6.4 (54.5→60.9), and TransT gains +2.3 (61.3→63.6). The most dramatic gains appear when comparing against the matched fixed-event baseline (same number of event frames): TransT jumps from RSR 51.0 to 63.6 (+12.6), and PrDiMP from 48.0 to 60.9 (+12.9), confirming that adaptive slicing is the primary source of improvement rather than simply having more/fewer frames. On recognition, SpikeSlicer outperforms both fixed-slice and random-slice baselines across all four datasets (e.g., ResNet-34 on N-Caltech101: 82.54% vs 76.08% fixed vs 78.77% random). The one exception is TaMOs, where SpikeSlicer-S slightly degrades OP.50 (41.2→36.3) while improving RPR (78.8→82.9), suggesting the method benefits trackers that rely heavily on temporal structure more than those with weaker temporal modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing spiking neural networks — typically used as the primary processing backbone for event data — as a lightweight, low-energy slicing trigger that sits upstream of a conventional ANN. It departs from fixed-interval and fixed-count slicing (the dominant paradigm in event-to-representation pipelines) and from recent adaptive sampling methods [13,14] that still rely on manually tuned hyperparameters. The SPA-Loss (combining Mem-Loss and LA-Loss) is a novel loss designed specifically to control *when* an SNN fires, rather than *what* it computes, and the Feedback-Update strategy introduces a closed-loop SNN-ANN cooperation paradigm not previously explored in event-based vision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of SpikeSlicer means it can be inserted into any existing event-based pipeline without modifying the downstream model, addressing a fundamental but overlooked bottleneck in event processing. The SNN-ANN cooperation paradigm — where a tiny 0.42M-parameter SNN guides a much larger ANN — opens a new design space for hybrid neuromorphic systems. Key limitations include the multi-stage training procedure, the reliance on GPU simulation rather than actual neuromorphic hardware, and the use of single-frame representations for recognition tasks. Open questions include real-time asynchronous deployment on brain-inspired chips, extending to multi-frame slicing, and whether the feedback-update loop can be made more sample-efficient.
