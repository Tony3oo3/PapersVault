## Problem

Scalable oversight protocols such as debate and consultancy aim to let weaker judges accurately supervise stronger AI agents, but prior empirical evaluations were confined to a single extractive QA task with artificial information asymmetry. This paper asks whether debate's advantages generalize across diverse capability gaps (mathematics, logic, coding, multimodal reasoning) and whether, when used as a training signal, the protocol risks amplifying the agent's existing mistakes rather than correcting them.

## Input / Output

Each task presents a binary-choice question with two answer options; extractive tasks additionally provide an article visible only to debaters/consultants (not the judge), and multimodal tasks include an image alongside the question. The judge LLM receives a structured transcript of the debate or consultancy interaction—including verified/unverified passage tags for extractive tasks—and outputs a single binary answer choice (1 or 2). The critical constraint is that the judge model is deliberately weaker than the agent models, creating a controlled capability gap that mimics the human-vs-superhuman-AI oversight scenario.

## Method

The paper conducts a large-scale inference-only evaluation (~5M model calls) across 9 tasks, 4 judge models (Gemma 7B, GPT-3.5, Gemini Pro 1.0, Gemini Pro 1.5), and six protocols: debate, consultancy, open debate, open consultancy, and two direct QA baselines. In assigned-role protocols, debaters/consultants are forced to argue for a randomly assigned answer; in open-role protocols, the agent chooses its own answer, enabling direct measurement of whether the weak judge amplifies or corrects the agent's mistakes. For extractive tasks, a passage verification tool marks debater-quoted text as verified or unverified via substring matching against the article. The paper computes Elo ratings from cross-play debate win-rates to quantify debater persuasiveness and correlate it with judge accuracy, and runs systematic ablations on number of turns, best-of-N sampling, few-shot prompting, chain-of-thought, turn style, and positional bias.

## Results

Across 9 tasks (~5M model calls), debate consistently outperforms consultancy for all judge models and task types (extractive, closed, multimodal). On extractive QA with information asymmetry, debate significantly outperforms QA without article for all judges except Gemma7B, but does not match QA with article. On closed tasks, debate beats QA without article for GPT-3.5 (p=0.037) but not for Pro 1.0 (p=0.1) or Pro 1.5 (where debate is actually worse). In open debate, when the protagonist chooses incorrectly, judge accuracy is substantially higher than in open consultancy, indicating debate provides a less misleading training signal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the consultancy protocol (Michael et al. [30]), debate wins across all task types and all judge models, confirming and extending the single-task finding of Khan et al. [25] to closed QA, multimodal, and additional extractive tasks. Against the direct QA without article baseline (the 'naive' baseline from Khan et al. [25]), debate shows a clear advantage on extractive tasks (where information asymmetry exists) but only a marginal or non-significant advantage on closed tasks (p=0.037 for GPT-3.5, p=0.1 for Pro 1.0), and is actually worse when the judge equals the debater (Pro 1.5). Against the QA with article baseline (the 'expert' baseline), debate does not match performance on extractive tasks, indicating that direct access to information still outperforms filtered oversight. Against open consultancy (Roger [40]), open debate produces a more reliable training signal: when the protagonist/consultant chooses the incorrect answer, the weak judge's accuracy in open debate is substantially higher than in open consultancy, meaning debate is less likely to amplify the agent's existing mistakes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scalable oversight evaluation framework established by Irving et al. (2018) and most directly by Khan et al. (2024), which studied LLM debate on a single extractive QA task (QuALITY) with information asymmetry. It generalizes the evaluation to nine diverse tasks spanning mathematics, logic, coding, and multimodal reasoning, introduces the open debate protocol (where a protagonist debater selects its own answer, extending Roger's open consultancy concept), and varies judge strength across four models. The paper also departs from Khan et al. in concluding that debate does not clearly outperform direct QA baselines on non-extractive tasks, and that several setup choices (best-of-N, chain-of-thought, number of turns) have little effect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The mixed results—debate consistently beats consultancy but does not clearly surpass direct QA on non-extractive tasks—provide important calibration for the scalable oversight research program, suggesting that debate's theoretical advantages may not materialize in inference-only settings without targeted judge fine-tuning. The finding that open debate provides a less misleading training signal than open consultancy (judges detect incorrect answers much better in debate) is practically significant for future RLHF-style training. Key open questions include whether training debaters via self-play with judge signals would close the gap to QA baselines, whether human judges would show different patterns, and whether the results generalize to truly superhuman agents where capability gaps are far larger.
