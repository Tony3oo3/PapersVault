## Problem

Despite the rapid proliferation of graph condensation (GC) methods, no comprehensive benchmark exists to systematically evaluate them under unified settings across diverse datasets, tasks, and evaluation dimensions. Prior studies either overlook graph-specific structural properties such as heterogeneity and heterophily, or lack the breadth of methods and condensation ratios needed for fair comparison, leaving the field's actual progress and limitations unclear.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241197/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 12 graph datasets—7 node-level (5 homogeneous, 2 heterogeneous) and 5 graph-level (molecular/chemical)—each specified by node feature matrices and adjacency matrices or collections of graphs. The 12 evaluated GC methods produce condensed graphs at multiple condensation ratios, which are then used to train downstream models. Outputs span node/graph classification accuracy, ROC-AUC, link prediction accuracy, clustering metrics (NMI, ARI, F1), anomaly detection ROC-AUC, plus condensation wall-clock time and peak CPU/GPU memory.

## Method

GC-Bench unifies 12 representative GC methods across 6 categories (traditional core-set, gradient matching, trajectory matching, distribution matching, kernel ridge regression, and computation tree compression) under a single experimental protocol on 12 datasets. It investigates six research questions: effectiveness across a broader range of condensation ratios than prior work, structural impact (homogeneity vs. heterogeneity, homophily vs. heterophily), cross-task transferability (node classification → link prediction, node clustering, anomaly detection), cross-backbone transferability (SGC, GCN, GraphSAGE, APPNP, ChebyNet, Graph Transformer, MLP), initialization sensitivity (5 strategies), and time/space efficiency. A key design choice is evaluating heterogeneous and heterophilic datasets in addition to standard homogeneous ones, and measuring both CPU and GPU memory alongside wall-clock time to expose scalability bottlenecks. The benchmark is released as an open-source library that supports easy extension to new methods and datasets.

## Results

On node-level tasks, gradient matching methods (GCond, DosCond, SGDD) and trajectory matching methods (SFGC, GEOM) achieve near-lossless condensation, with SGDD reaching 82.1% on Cora at 3.90% ratio (vs. 80.8% whole dataset) and SFGC/GEOM matching the whole dataset at 91.7% on Reddit at 0.50% ratio. At very small ratios (0.26% on Cora), gradient methods (DosCond 78.7%, GCond 79.8%) dramatically outperform core-set baselines (Random 26.0%, Herding 31.6%). On graph-level tasks, KiDD with GIN backbone wins 18 out of 25 cases but still trails the whole dataset substantially (e.g., 65.4% vs. 80.0% on NCI1 at 2.43% ratio), indicating a significant gap in graph-level condensation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gradient matching methods (GCond, DosCond, SGDD) and trajectory matching methods (SFGC, GEOM) consistently dominate node-level condensation, achieving or exceeding whole-dataset accuracy in 5 of 7 datasets, while distribution matching methods (GCDM, DM) underperform them in 6 of 7 datasets. Traditional core-set baselines (Random, Herding, K-Center) are weakest at small condensation ratios (e.g., Random at 26.0% vs. GCond at 79.8% on Cora at 0.26%) but close the gap as the ratio increases, with K-Center reaching 77.4% at 3.90% on Cora. On graph-level tasks, KiDD (KRR) with GIN backbone wins 18/25 cases, while DosCond (gradient matching) dominates with GCN backbone (12/25 cases), showing strong backbone dependence. Mirage (CTC) is competitive on DD and ogbg-molhiv but is limited by recursive depth failures at larger GPC values. Structure-free methods (SFGC, GEOM) perform well in node classification but show significant degradation when transferred to anomaly detection, whereas gradient matching methods (GCond, DosCond, SGDD) demonstrate better cross-task transferability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first comprehensive benchmark unifying both node-level and graph-level graph condensation, extending the image dataset condensation benchmarking tradition (DC-Bench, DD-RobustBench) to the graph domain. It departs from prior surveys [39, 10] that 'either overlook graph-specific properties or lack systematic experimentation' and from the concurrent GCondenser [23], which covered only node-level methods on homogeneous graphs with limited evaluation dimensions. By integrating 12 methods across 6 algorithmic families (core-set, gradient matching, trajectory matching, distribution matching, KRR, CTC) under a unified protocol, GC-Bench carves out a systematic empirical space that no prior work had occupied.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that graph-level GC remains far from lossless compression, that larger condensation ratios can degrade performance (saturation/decline), and that condensed datasets exhibit poor cross-task transferability—findings that redirect the field toward task-agnostic and structure-preserving methods. The discovery that Graph Transformers, despite their strength in general graph learning, are incompatible with current GC paradigms as backbones challenges a natural assumption and opens a concrete research gap. Open questions include whether a Pareto-optimal condensation theory exists, how to handle complex graph types (heterogeneous, dynamic, hypergraphs), and how to decouple condensation from full-dataset training to achieve the efficiency gains GC was originally designed for.
