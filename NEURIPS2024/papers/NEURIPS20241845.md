## Problem

Existing diffusion-based trajectory optimization methods are model-free: they learn the score function from demonstration data, which limits generalization to new robots or dynamics and requires high-quality, dynamically feasible demonstrations. This paper asks whether the known dynamics model, cost function, and constraints in a trajectory optimization problem can be used directly to compute the score function, enabling data-free trajectory optimization that still handles non-smooth dynamics and non-convex objectives.

## Input / Output

MBD takes a dynamics model f_t, stage costs l_t, constraint functions g_t, and an initial state x_0 as required inputs, and optionally accepts demonstration data (including partial-state or dynamically infeasible trajectories) as a soft steering signal. The decision variable is the full state-control sequence Y = [x_{1:T}; u_{1:T}], and the output is an optimized trajectory that minimizes the cumulative cost while satisfying dynamics and constraints. A key practical detail is that the shooting method (recursively rolling out controls through the dynamics) converts infeasible Monte Carlo samples into dynamically feasible ones, making the approach applicable to contact-rich and high-dimensional systems where direct sampling from the feasible set is intractable.

## Method

MBD reframes trajectory optimization as sampling from a known target distribution p_0(Y) ∝ p_d(Y)·p_J(Y)·p_g(Y) (dynamics feasibility × Boltzmann optimality × constraint satisfaction) and uses a diffusion process to iteratively refine samples from isotropic Gaussians toward this distribution. The core technical contribution is estimating the score function ∇_{Y^(i)} log p_i(Y^(i)) via Monte Carlo: by connecting the intermediate density p_i to p_0 through Bayes' rule, the score reduces to a weighted average of samples drawn from a Gaussian proposal φ_i, weighted by the model-evaluated likelihood p_0. Instead of the stochastic reverse SDE used in model-free diffusion, MBD performs deterministic Monte Carlo score ascent with step size (1−ᾱ_i), which is provably optimal for the 1/(1−ᾱ_i)-smooth log-density. The multi-stage diffusion schedule provides a coarse-to-fine search: early stages with large sampling covariance explore globally, while later stages with small covariance perform local refinement. For the constrained TO setting, the shooting method enforces dynamics feasibility, and an optional demonstration-augmentation step uses a max-weighting scheme w(Y) = max{w_model(Y), w_demo(Y)} to adaptively trust the model when samples are good and fall back to demonstrations when they are not.

## Results

On seven contact-rich locomotion and manipulation tasks (Hopper, Half Cheetah, Ant, Walker2D, Humanoid Standup, Humanoid Running, PushT), MBD achieves the highest average step reward across all tasks, outperforming the best RL baseline (PPO/SAC) by 59% on average while using only ~10% of the computational time. For example, MBD scores 2.92 on Humanoid Running vs. 1.80 for PPO, 2.63 on Walker2D vs. 1.09 for PPO, and 0.67 on PushT vs. −0.63 for PPO. In high-dimensional black-box optimization (up to 800D Ackley/Rastrigin), MBD improves sampling efficiency by an average of 23% over leading Bayesian optimization baselines. MBD also optimizes a 27,562-parameter MLP for MNIST in 2s without gradients, reaching 85.5% accuracy comparable to SGD+momentum (92.7%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MBD dominates all three zeroth-order trajectory optimization baselines (CMA-ES, CEM, MPPI) across every task, with the largest gaps on high-dimensional tasks like Humanoid Running (2.92 vs. 0.60/0.41/0.59) and Walker2D (2.63 vs. 0.83/1.06/0.90). Against the RL baselines (PPO and SAC), MBD wins on 6 of 7 tasks, with the most dramatic improvement on PushT where PPO fails entirely (−0.63) while MBD achieves 0.67; the paper reports a 59% average reward improvement over PPO. In the black-box optimization appendix, MBD outperforms all five Bayesian optimization methods (TuRBO, LA-MCTS, HesBO, Shiwa, BAxUS) on Ackley and Rastrigin benchmarks up to 800 dimensions, achieving over 20× computational time savings compared to GP-based BO approaches. Notably, MBD matches the computational time of CEM and MPPI (~16–35s per task) while substantially exceeding their reward, and requires only ~10% of the wall-clock time of RL training (which takes 3–67 minutes).

## Relation to Prior Work

MBD repurposes the forward/backward diffusion process structure from denoising diffusion models (Ho et al., 2020) and the score-function framework from score-based generative modeling (Song et al., 2021), but inverts the typical paradigm: rather than learning the score from data, it computes the score analytically from the known dynamics model and cost function via Monte Carlo estimation. This positions MBD as a bridge between diffusion-based generative modeling and sampling-based trajectory optimization (CEM, MPPI, CMA-ES), with the paper explicitly showing that MBD reduces to CEM when the diffusion schedule is collapsed to a single step (N=1). The use of Monte Carlo score ascent instead of the standard reverse SDE is a deliberate departure from model-free diffusion planners, motivated by the fact that the target distribution is known (up to normalization) in TO, making the stochastic SDE unnecessary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MBD offers a practical, data-free trajectory optimizer that leverages readily available dynamics models, addressing a key limitation of model-free diffusion planners that struggle to generalize across robots or tasks with different dynamics. The ability to integrate imperfect or partial demonstrations (infeasible RRT trajectories, lower-body-only mocap data) as a soft steering signal rather than a hard constraint is a genuinely useful capability for real-world robotics where high-quality demonstrations are scarce. Open questions include the lack of convergence guarantees for the multi-stage Monte Carlo score ascent on non-smooth, non-convex objectives, the computational cost of forward dynamics rollouts limiting online control frequency to ~4–10 Hz, and whether the approach scales to very long horizons where the shooting-based feasibility check becomes combinatorially expensive.
