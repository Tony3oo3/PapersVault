## Problem

Existing bilevel RL methods for model design, RLHF, and reward shaping assume a single follower, require deterministic lower-level solving (e.g., soft value iteration), and compute exact hypergradients that demand full access to the follower's internal computations. No algorithm handles the more general setting where a leader configures a contextual MDP faced by multiple followers with heterogeneous preferences or side information, and where the leader can only observe follower trajectories without controlling their training procedure.

## Input / Output

The leader receives the CMDP parameterization (reward r_{x,ξ}, transition kernel P_{x,ξ}, initial state distribution μ_{x,ξ}), the context distribution P_ξ, and trajectory samples generated by the follower's current policy π_{x,ξ}^o. It outputs an updated model configuration x and a stochastic hypergradient estimate constructed from finite-length trajectory rollouts (length T ~ Geo(1−γ)) rather than from the follower's value or Q-functions. The leader is agnostic to the follower's algorithm: it only requires that the oracle policy satisfies E‖π*−π^o‖²∞ ≤ δ², which holds for soft value iteration, Q-learning, and natural policy gradient.

## Method

HPGD derives a closed-form hypergradient (Theorem 1) by exploiting the softmax structure of entropy-regularized optimal policies, replacing the expensive Hessian inversion of the follower's value function with a tractable expression involving ∂_x A_{λ,x,ξ}^{π*}(s,a). The hypergradient is then estimated stochastically via a GradientEstimator (Algorithm 2) that rolls out the follower policy for geometrically-distributed trajectory lengths and uses importance sampling to compute ∂_x Q and ∂_x V from samples, yielding an unbiased estimate without access to the follower's internals. The paper proves non-asymptotic convergence to a stationary point at rate O(1/√T) under mild smoothness and oracle-accuracy assumptions. When the leader can direct the follower's training, an accelerated variant RT-Q applies randomly truncated soft Q-learning combined with multilevel Monte Carlo and importance sampling, reducing lower-level iterations from Õ(δ^{-2}) to O(log(δ^{-1})) while maintaining the same bias and only O(K) variance.

## Results

On the Four-Rooms reward shaping task, HPGD achieves an upper-level objective of 0.91±0.088 at (λ=0.001, β=1), substantially outperforming AMD (0.58±0.000) and Zero-Order (0.59±0.059), primarily by escaping the local optimum of zero penalty. Across all nine hyperparameter combinations, HPGD wins or ties in 6 out of 9 settings. On the Tax Design macroeconomic model, HPGD increases social welfare by approximately 25% and converges faster than the Zero-Order baseline, while also producing qualitatively better tax rate differentiation across goods based on household preferences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares HPGD against two baselines: AMD (Adaptive Model Design, Chen et al. 2022) and a novel Zero-Order gradient approximation algorithm. On the Four-Rooms problem, HPGD decisively outperforms both at (λ=0.001, β=1) with 0.91 vs. 0.58 (AMD) and 0.59 (Zero-Order), and at (λ=0.003, β=3) with 0.73 vs. 0.39/0.40, because HPGD's stochastic updates escape the local optimum of zero penalty while both baselines converge to it. However, AMD wins at three settings (λ=0.003,β=1; λ=0.005,β=1; λ=0.005,β=3) due to its deterministic gradient computation yielding lower variance, and the three methods are essentially tied at the remaining three settings. On the Tax Design problem, HPGD converges faster than Zero-Order and produces qualitatively superior tax rate differentiation (setting VAT rates according to household preferences), while Zero-Order fails to distinguish between goods. The paper notes that AMD requires full access to the MDP to compute exact hypergradients, whereas HPGD only needs trajectory samples, making it more scalable and applicable when the leader cannot control the follower's training procedure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends contextual bilevel optimization [37] from static lower-level problems to sequential decision-making via contextual MDPs, and generalizes model design for MDPs [15] and bilevel RL [13, 58, 75] from a single deterministic follower to multiple followers with side information. The key methodological departure is replacing exact hypergradient computation (which requires inverting Hessians of the follower's value function) with stochastic estimates built from trajectory samples, making the leader agnostic to the follower's specific learning algorithm. The accelerated RT-Q algorithm further draws on multilevel Monte Carlo [29] and importance sampling to reduce lower-level soft Q-learning iterations from O(K2^K) to O(K²), adapting techniques previously applied to static contextual bilevel optimization to the sequential RL setting where trajectory samples depend on the lower-level decision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CB-RL provides a unifying framework for a wide range of incentive-alignment problems (RLHF, tax design, reward shaping, mechanism design, contract theory) that were previously treated in isolation with problem-specific algorithms. The stochastic hypergradient estimation is practically crucial: it allows followers to use any model-free or model-based RL algorithm while the leader only needs trajectory access, which matches real-world scenarios where a policy-maker cannot control how agents learn. Open questions include extending to settings where the lower level is a game (multiple interacting followers), developing single-loop algorithms, and validating the framework on larger-scale real-world applications beyond the small grid-world and 3-good tax model tested here.
