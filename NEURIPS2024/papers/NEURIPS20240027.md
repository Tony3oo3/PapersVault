## Problem

The mechanism behind in-context learning (ICL) in Transformers remains an open question. Previous works connecting ICL to gradient descent were restricted to linear attention settings or required specific constructions of model weights and input token formats. This paper asks whether ICL can be related to gradient descent under the standard softmax attention without such restrictive assumptions, and what the specific training data and loss function are in the corresponding gradient descent process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240027/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained softmax attention layer with fixed parameters {W_Q, W_K, W_V} and an input sequence of N demonstration tokens followed by T query tokens. It constructs a dual model f(x) = Wφ(x) whose training data is derived from the demonstration tokens (inputs z_std = W_K x_i, labels y_std = W_V x_i) and whose test input is z_test = W_Q x_{T+1}', producing a prediction strictly equivalent to the attention layer's ICL output. The paper also derives a generalization error bound that scales with the number of demonstration tokens and proposes three modified attention mechanisms (regularized, augmented, negative-sample) inspired by contrastive learning.

## Method

The core technique uses kernel methods to express softmax attention scores as inner products under a feature mapping φ satisfying K_sm(x,y) = φ(x)^T φ(y), enabling a dual model formulation for softmax attention. Theorem 3.1 proves that the ICL inference of one softmax attention layer is strictly equivalent to one step of gradient descent on the dual model f(x) = Wφ(x) with a self-supervised loss L = -(1/ηD) Σ (W_V x_i)^T W φ(W_K x_i), where key and value projections of each demonstration token serve as the training input and label respectively. From a representation learning perspective, this process is analogous to contrastive learning without negative samples: W_K and W_V act as two augmentations, φ projects into a higher-dimensional space, and W is trained to align the mapped key representation with the value representation. The result extends to full Transformer layers (where the FFN introduces a potential low-rank transformation W_F = W_2 I_M W_1 on the labels) and to multiple stacked attention layers (sequential gradient descent on a sequence of dual models with layer-dependent initializations and datasets). Inspired by contrastive learning, the paper proposes three attention modifications—L2 regularization on the loss, nonlinear MLP augmentations on key/value embeddings, and negative-sample subtraction from attention scores—validated on synthetic linear, trigonometric, and exponential regression tasks.

## Results

The primary experimental validation confirms Theorem 3.1: after N=15 epochs of gradient descent on the dual model, the test prediction ŷ_test is exactly equivalent to the ICL inference output h'_{T+1} of one softmax attention layer (‖ŷ_test − h'_{T+1}‖₂ → 0). For the proposed attention modifications on linear regression tasks, the regularized model with α < 0 converges faster than the standard attention (α = 0) while achieving comparable final MSE; the augmented model using a two-layer MLP for key augmentation (g₂⁺) initially converges slower but ultimately reaches a better solution; and the negative model with k=3 negative samples and β=0.1 achieves slightly faster convergence. The generalization bound (Theorem 3.2) shows the error scales as O(wρd_o·√Tr(K_S)/N + √(log(1/δ)/N)), confirming that more demonstration tokens reduce generalization error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily theoretical, so it does not benchmark against external methods on standard NLP tasks. Instead, the experiments compare the standard softmax attention mechanism against three proposed modifications (regularized, augmented, and negative) on synthetic linear regression tasks. The standard attention (α=0, no augmentation, no negatives) serves as the implicit baseline. The regularized modification with positive α (the originally intended direction) actually degrades performance, while negative α accelerates convergence — a finding the authors attribute to preserving full-rank attention matrices. The augmented model with key-only augmentation (g₂) shows slightly faster convergence than the standard baseline, and the two-layer MLP augmentation (g₂⁺) eventually surpasses it in final solution quality. The negative model with k=3, β=0.1 provides marginal convergence speedup. Overall, the modifications show promise but improvements are modest in the synthetic setting, and the authors note that task-specific design of augmentations and negative samples would likely yield stronger gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL-as-gradient-descent line initiated by Dai et al. (2022) and the dual-form connection from Irie et al. (2022), but makes two key departures: it works under softmax attention (via kernel methods and the Performer-style feature map) rather than linear attention, and it defines a separate 'dual model' whose single SGD step reproduces the attention output, rather than fine-tuning the original model's parameters. The loss function is explicitly identified as a cosine-similarity-like objective, and the authors reframe the entire process through the lens of contrastive representation learning, drawing analogies to BYOL/SimSiam and kernel contrastive learning. Extensions to full Transformer layers (with FFN) and stacked attention layers (under PrefixLM) are provided, and modifications inspired by contrastive learning (regularization, data augmentation, negative samples) are proposed and tested.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean equivalence between softmax-attention ICL and one gradient step on a dual model with a contrastive-learning loss provides a unifying theoretical bridge that was missing between the gradient-descent interpretation and the representation-learning literature, potentially guiding future architectural modifications. The generalization bound (Theorem 3.2) formally connects ICL performance to the number of demonstration tokens, consistent with empirical scaling observations. However, the analysis relies on a simplified Transformer (no layer norm, residual connections), the experiments are primarily on synthetic regression tasks with Performer approximation, and the proposed contrastive-inspired modifications show only modest gains—so the practical impact on real LLMs remains an open question.
