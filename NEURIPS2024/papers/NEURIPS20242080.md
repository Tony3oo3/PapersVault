## Problem

While LLMs are evaluated on increasingly complex graph reasoning tasks (node classification, link prediction, cycle detection), no prior work has examined whether they can even accurately recall a graph described in earlier text — a prerequisite for all such tasks. Cognitive science has documented for decades that humans exhibit systematic biased microstructural patterns (triadic closure, degree biases) when recalling social networks, but whether LLMs display analogous biases, and how their recall accuracy compares to humans, remained entirely unexplored.

## Input / Output

The pipeline feeds an LLM a narrative vignette encoding 5–30 node graphs from five domains (DBLP co-authorship, Facebook social network, CA road, Reactome protein interactions, Erdős-Rényi random graphs), followed by a word-span memory clearance sequence, then a zero-shot recall prompt. For GPT models, edge probabilities are extracted directly from token log-probabilities (normalized P('1') vs P('0')); for Gemini-Pro, 100 Monte Carlo samples per edge yield an empirical existence fraction. The recalled probability graph is then fit with an ERGM to produce per-motif coefficient gaps (recalled minus ground-truth) alongside accuracy and F1 scores.

## Method

The paper adapts Brashears et al.'s human cognition protocol into a six-stage LLM pipeline: task introduction, graph vignette presentation, word-span memory clearance, zero-shot recall prompting, edge-probability retrieval, and ERGM-based microstructure analysis. The key analytical tool is the Exponential Random Graph Model, which estimates coefficients for five microstructural patterns (edge, triangle, star, alt-triangle, alt-2-path) on both the recalled and ground-truth graphs, with the coefficient gap revealing statistically significant recall biases. Beyond the core recall test, the paper cross-evaluates five narrative styles against five graph domains to test domain-narrative alignment effects, varies memory clearance strength (0–7 sentence sets) to probe noise sensitivity, and correlates recall microstructures with link-prediction microstructures to trace how recall biases propagate to downstream tasks. Results are benchmarked against 301 human subjects from the original Brashears experiment on two 15-node social networks.

## Results

GPT-4 achieves near-perfect graph recall on Facebook (99.80% accuracy), DBLP (98.70%), and CA Road (98.11%), but drops to 77.04% on Reactome and 60.34% on Erdős-Rényi graphs. GPT-3.5 ranges from 53.68% (Reactome) to 95.52% (CA Road), while Gemini-Pro performs worst overall (44.69%–51.13% accuracy). Across all models, ERGM analysis reveals a consistent bias toward encouraging triangles and alternating 2-paths while suppressing edges and alternating triangles, indicating selective forgetting rather than hallucination. GPT-4 shows a striking domain-narrative matching effect, performing best when the narrative style aligns with the graph's original domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against human graph recall performance from Brashears et al. (2015), who tested 301 humans on two 15-node social networks. On the 'irreducible' social network, GPT-4 achieves 95.71% accuracy versus 29.72% for humans, while GPT-3.5 (31.51%) roughly matches humans and Gemini-Pro (24.99%) underperforms. On the 'reducible' network, GPT-4 again dominates at 95.74% versus 17.80% for humans, with GPT-3.5 (51.11%) and Gemini-Pro (38.82%) both exceeding human performance. In terms of microstructural bias patterns, both LLMs and humans share the same qualitative direction of bias (forgetting edges and alt-triangles, hallucinating triangles and stars), but LLMs exhibit weaker forgetting and stronger hallucination magnitudes than humans. Notably, GPT-4's microstructural coefficients on Facebook are near zero (e.g., Triangle: 0.05±0.78), indicating near-perfect recall, while humans show strong triangle hallucination (9.52±1.23).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the experimental protocol from Brashears & Quintane (2015) on human social-network recall—word span memory clearance, ERGM-based microstructural analysis, and staged vignette presentation—into the LLM setting for the first time. It positions graph recall as a prerequisite task that underlies all prior LLM graph-reasoning work (node degree, cycle checking, link prediction, node classification), arguing that those tasks assume correct graph encoding that has never been systematically validated. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from the 'edge existence' task in Fatemi et al. (2023) by evaluating full-graph recall with probabilistic edge outputs and comparing microstructural bias patterns against human baselines, rather than binary per-edge queries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT-4 achieves only ~60% accuracy on Erdős-Rényi graph recall calibrates expectations for all downstream graph-reasoning benchmarks that assume faithful graph encoding. The narrative-style × domain interaction (GPT-4 performs best when the text style matches the graph's origin domain) suggests LLMs have internalized domain-specific structural distributions, pointing toward domain-adaptive graph-to-text encoding as a practical improvement. Open questions include whether findings generalize to noisier, less templated real-world text, whether higher-order microstructures reveal additional biases, and how memory-augmentation techniques (analogous to 'needle-in-a-haystack' fixes) might close the recall gap.
