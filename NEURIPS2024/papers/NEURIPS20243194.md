## Problem

Existing LVLM benchmarks evaluate perception, reasoning, and creation in isolation via single-turn, closed-ended multiple-choice questions, making it impossible to assess multi-turn conversational follow-up ability or attribute errors to the correct capability level (e.g., whether a reasoning failure stems from a perception error). ConvBench addresses this by introducing the first open-ended, multi-turn visual conversation benchmark with hierarchical capability ablation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243194/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark sample consists of one RGB image paired with three sequential text instructions (perception, reasoning, creation) that are designed to reference and build upon the previous turn's content. The evaluation pipeline consumes the LVLM's multi-turn responses alongside human-verified reference answers, instruction-conditioned captions (detailed image descriptions that embed answer-relevant information, averaging ~1064 words), and annotated focus points for creation tasks. Output is structured as per-turn win rates (S1, S2, S3), an overall conversation score (S_O), and hierarchical ablation scores (Ŝ2, Ŝ3, Ŝ_O, S̃3, S̃_O) that quantify how much downstream performance recovers when earlier turns are replaced with ground-truth answers.

## Method

ConvBench extends VisIT-Bench images into 577 three-turn conversations spanning 215 tasks (71 perception, 65 reasoning, 79 creation), where each subsequent turn is conditioned on the prior turn's content to test context memory and anaphora resolution. The key evaluation mechanism is hierarchical ablation: by substituting human-annotated perception answers (M̂1=R1) or both perception and reasoning answers (M̃i=Ri for i=1,2) into the conversation history, the framework isolates how much downstream reasoning/creation degradation is attributable to upstream perception or reasoning errors versus genuine capability gaps. ConvBenchEval uses ChatGPT as a pairwise judge that compares LVLM responses against human-verified references in random order, with the instruction-conditioned caption providing the judge sufficient visual context to make accurate judgments without direct image access. The evaluation achieves 81.83% agreement with human raters, and a full model assessment costs approximately $21–26 in API tokens.

## Results

On ConvBench (577 multi-turn conversations, 215 tasks), the best-performing model GPT4V achieves only 39.51% overall win rate (R1) against human-verified references, with per-turn scores of 38.47% (perception), 39.34% (reasoning), and 37.61% (creation). The hierarchical ablation reveals that under perfect perception, the average reasoning score increases by 11.38 points and creation by 5.65 points across 20 LVLMs, while under perfect perception and reasoning, creation improves by an additional 5.32 points. Performance declines from the first turn (mean 15.44%) to the third turn (mean 12.55%), indicating comprehension drift in multi-turn settings. The evaluation uses a pairwise grading scheme where ChatGPT judges LVLM responses against human references, achieving 81.83% agreement with human evaluators.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ConvBench is a benchmark, so the 'method' is the evaluation framework itself, and the 20 LVLMs are the subjects being compared. GPT4V leads with 39.51% overall win rate, followed by Claude (36.60%) and Reka Flash (25.60%), establishing a clear closed-source over open-source gap. Among open-source models, InternVL-Chat-V1-2 (21.17%) and ShareGPT4V-13B (17.56%) rank highest, while BLIP2-FLAN-T5-XL (0.06%) and BLIP2-FLAN-T5-XXL (0.32%) are near the bottom. The hierarchical ablation shows that perception errors are the dominant bottleneck: under perfect perception, GPT4V's reasoning jumps from 39.34% to 47.31% (+16.97), while open-source models like LLaVA-V1.5-13B gain +14.74 points in reasoning. Under perfect perception and reasoning, creation scores improve further (e.g., InternVL-Chat-V1-5 gains +8.84 points), confirming that reasoning weaknesses cascade into creative failures. The paper also notes that performance distribution across tasks is similar between open-source and closed-source models, suggesting synchronous development, and that larger language model backbones (13B vs. 7B) consistently yield 1–2 point improvements in overall score.

## Relation to Prior Work

ConvBench extends VisIT-Bench's single-turn, real-world VQA paradigm into a three-turn hierarchical structure (perception → reasoning → creation), while adopting the LLM-as-judge pairwise evaluation framework from MT-Bench. It departs from existing LVLM benchmarks (MMBench, MM-Vet, SEED, MMBench, etc.) that evaluate capabilities in isolation via single-turn, often closed-ended questions, by introducing multi-turn follow-up instructions that are explicitly conditioned on prior responses. The hierarchical ablation mechanism—substituting human-annotated answers at earlier turns to isolate downstream capability errors—is a methodological novelty not present in prior visual or text-only multi-turn benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first multi-turn visual conversation benchmark, ConvBench fills a clear gap in LVLM evaluation and provides a practical tool for error attribution that the field previously lacked. The finding that weak perception (particularly OCR, fine-grained, and spatial tasks) systematically suppresses reasoning and creation performance (average +11.38 and +5.65 gains under perfect perception) offers actionable guidance for model developers. Key limitations include the modest scale (577 conversations), restriction to image-text modality, and an acknowledged bias of the ChatGPT-based judge toward GPT-family models, which could inflate scores for GPT-distilled architectures. Open questions include whether the hierarchical structure generalizes to video/audio, how to scale annotation cost-effectively, and how to eliminate the judge-model bias.
