## Problem

Neural networks trained with SGD implicitly assume stationary data, but in continual learning, reinforcement learning, and distribution-shift settings, the data distribution changes over time, causing a loss of plasticity where the network can no longer adapt. Existing remedies like hard resets are too aggressive (discarding useful learned knowledge) while fixed heuristics for reset timing or magnitude cannot adapt to the actual level of non-stationarity. The paper asks: can we learn *when* and *how much* to reset each parameter directly from the data stream?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242646/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of data batches B_t drawn from a time-varying distribution p_t(x,y), along with the NN initialization distribution p_init(θ) (a per-parameter Gaussian). At each step it produces an updated parameter vector θ_{t+1} and a learned drift parameter γ_t (either per-parameter or shared per layer) that quantifies the degree of soft reset applied. The drift parameter is estimated online via K gradient steps on the predictive log-likelihood of the incoming batch, requiring only a single Monte-Carlo sample in practice. The method is agnostic to the specific loss (cross-entropy, TD error, etc.) and works with both supervised and RL objectives.

## Method

The core mechanism is a discretized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck drift model p(θ|θ_t, γ_t) that, for each parameter dimension, interpolates between retaining the current value (γ_t^j=1) and drawing from the initialization prior (γ_t^j=0), with variance that ensures the stationary distribution of the process is the prior. The drift parameter γ_t is learned online by maximizing the approximate predictive log-likelihood of the next batch under the drifted prior, using the reparameterization trick for gradient-based optimization. This learned γ_t then modifies the SGD update in two coupled ways: it shifts the starting point from θ_t toward the initialization mean μ_0 (the soft reset), and it inflates the per-parameter learning rate by a factor (γ_t^j)² + (1−(γ_t^j)²)/s², so that parameters undergoing a larger reset also receive a proportionally larger gradient step. Two algorithmic variants are provided: a fast MAP/proximal version that tracks only point estimates, and a full Bayesian version that propagates per-parameter Gaussian posteriors (mean and variance) via online variational inference, which achieves the best empirical performance at higher computational cost.

## Results

On plasticity benchmarks (permuted MNIST, random-label MNIST, random-label CIFAR-10), Soft Reset consistently outperforms Online SGD, Hard Reset, L2-Init, and Shrink & Perturb in per-task average online accuracy, with the largest gains in the data-efficient regime where SGD from scratch achieves only ~50% accuracy. In off-policy RL (SAC on Hopper-v5 and Humanoid-v4), Soft Reset surpasses SAC with hard-coded Hard Reset as the off-policy ratio increases, with the learned drift parameter γ_t changing primarily in the value function rather than the policy. The Bayesian Soft Reset variant (which tracks per-parameter uncertainty) achieves the best overall performance across all benchmarks but at higher computational cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Soft Reset outperforms Shrink & Perturb [2] on all three plasticity benchmarks (permuted MNIST, random-label MNIST, random-label CIFAR-10), because it adaptively learns the drift parameter γ_t from data rather than using fixed shrinkage and noise hyperparameters. Against L2-Init [34], Soft Reset wins in the data-efficient regime where task similarity matters, while L2-Init is competitive only in the memorization regime (where it effectively acts as a fixed γ_t=0 hard reset). In the RL setting, Soft Reset outperforms the SAC + Hard Reset baseline from [43] (which resets all parameters 5 times at fixed intervals), with the advantage growing as the off-policy ratio M increases, confirming that the adaptive drift is more effective than periodic hard resets. Online SGD (without any reset) is the weakest baseline across all settings, suffering from plasticity loss as training progresses. The Bayesian Soft Reset variant further improves over the MAP-based Soft Reset by leveraging per-parameter uncertainty estimates in the drift estimation, though at roughly 2× the computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of non-stationary online convex optimization (Herbster & Warmuth 1998; Hall & Willett 2013; Khodak et al. 2019), which introduced drift models and adaptive learning rates for tracking changing optima, and the recent line of work on plasticity loss in neural networks (Lyle et al. 2023, 2024; Dohare et al. 2022, 2024; Nikishin et al. 2023), which identified parameter norm growth and preactivation shifts as root causes. The key departure from prior reset-based methods (Shrink & Perturb, L2-Init, Continual Backprop, ReDO) is that the reset magnitude is learned online from data via an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck drift process rather than being fixed or triggered by heuristics. The Bayesian variant builds on variational continual learning (Nguyen et al. 2018) and OU-process-based Bayesian forgetting (Kurle et al. 2020; Titsias et al. 2023), but uniquely learns the drift parameter per-parameter and connects it to a proximal SGD interpretation with an adaptive learning rate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a principled, data-driven alternative to hard resets that preserves useful parameter information while restoring plasticity, and the finding that the Bayesian variant (with per-parameter uncertainty) substantially outperforms the MAP variant highlights the importance of uncertainty estimation for drift model accuracy. The RL experiments reveal that non-stationarity is concentrated in the value function rather than the policy, which has practical implications for where to apply adaptive resets in off-policy RL. Limitations include the computational overhead of the Bayesian variant, the instability of the closed-form γ_t linearization, and evaluation on relatively small MLP/CNN architectures; open questions include scaling to larger networks, theoretical guarantees on dynamic regret, and connections to continual learning theory.
