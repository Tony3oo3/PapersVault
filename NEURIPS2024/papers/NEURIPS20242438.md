## Problem

Intracortical BCI decoders degrade over time due to neural nonstationarities, forcing users into burdensome recalibration cycles. While diverse approaches have been proposed to reduce or eliminate recalibration (few-shot alignment, zero-shot deep networks, test-time adaptation), they have been evaluated on different datasets with different metrics and preprocessing, making rigorous cross-method comparison impossible. FALCON addresses this gap by providing a standardized multi-dataset benchmark with unified evaluation protocols for iBCI decoding robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242438/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

All decoders consume multi-unit threshold crossings (spike events) from intracortical microelectrode arrays as input, with the amount of labeled calibration data varying by evaluation class (full held-in sessions, a small few-shot subset from held-out sessions, or no held-out data for zero-shot). Outputs are task-specific: 16-channel EMG for M1, 2D finger positions for M2, 7D robotic arm kinematics for H1, discrete English characters for H2, and continuous vocal spectrograms for B1. Evaluation is causal and open-loop, requiring timestep-by-timestep predictions for movement tasks but coarser per-sentence (H2) or per-song-motif (B1) predictions for communication tasks, mimicking real iBCI deployment.

## Method

FALCON is a benchmark suite rather than a single algorithm: it curates five multi-session iBCI datasets (monkey reach/grasp, monkey finger movement, human robotic arm, human handwriting, songbird vocalization), each split into held-in sessions (full data released) and held-out sessions (only a small calibration fraction released, with evaluation data withheld). A flexible EvalAI-based platform accepts user-submitted executables that must produce causal, open-loop predictions on streaming neural data, enabling comparison across zero-shot, few-shot supervised, few-shot unsupervised, and test-time adaptive methods under identical conditions. The paper seeds the benchmark with representative baselines spanning the method space: linear Wiener Filters, single-session RNNs, the multi-session Neural Data Transformer (NDT2 Multi), unsupervised manifold alignment (NoMAD, CycleGAN), the EnSongdec birdsong decoder, and the CORP test-time adaptive recalibration method for handwriting. Metrics are task-appropriate (variance-weighted R² for movement, word error rate for handwriting, MSE for spectrograms), computed per session and aggregated across held-in and held-out splits separately.

## Results

On the FALCON movement datasets, the best few-shot supervised method (NDT2 Multi) achieves 0.59±0.07 R² on M1-A, 0.43±0.08 R² on M2, and 0.52±0.04 R² on H1 (held-out), compared to oracle upper bounds of 0.78, 0.58, and 0.63 R² respectively. Zero-shot static decoders suffer severe degradation: the RNN drops by up to 1.94 R² on M1-A and 0.82 R² on M2, while the Wiener Filter drops by up to 0.28 R². For communication tasks, the test-time adaptive CORP method achieves 0.11±0.01 WER on H2 held-out (vs. 0.02 oracle), and EnSongdec zero-shot achieves 21.8×10⁻⁴ MSE on B1 (vs. 5.61×10⁻⁴ oracle).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among few-shot supervised methods, NDT2 Multi [32] is the strongest baseline, achieving 0.59 R² on M1-A and 0.52 R² on H1 held-out, but still trails its own oracle by 0.19 and 0.11 R² respectively. Between the two few-shot unsupervised alignment methods, NoMAD+WF [22] outperforms CycleGAN+WF [23] on M1-A (0.49 vs. 0.43 R²) and H1 (0.13 vs. 0.12 R²), while CycleGAN edges ahead on M2 (0.22 vs. 0.20 R²); both substantially outperform the zero-shot Wiener Filter (0.34 R² on M1-A) but remain well below the few-shot supervised NDT2. For H2, CORP [29] (test-time adaptive) reduces WER from 0.53 (zero-shot RNN) to 0.11, a 4.8× improvement, yet still lags the oracle RNN+LM by 3.7× (0.11 vs. 0.03 WER). On B1, EnSongdec [67] zero-shot achieves 21.8×10⁻⁴ MSE versus 5.61×10⁻⁴ for the oracle, a 3.9× gap, and no dedicated stabilization method is yet available for this task. Overall, all few-shot approaches show only marginal gains over static zero-shot decoders on movement tasks, indicating that the stability challenge remains largely unsolved.

## Relation to Prior Work

FALCON carves new ground as the first standardized multi-dataset benchmark specifically for few-shot and zero-shot iBCI decoding robustness, filling a gap left by prior BCI benchmarks (BCI Competition, MOABB, Brain2Text) that focused on noninvasive signals or single tasks, and by neural data benchmarks (NLB, BrainScore) that evaluate encoding or latent variable models rather than end-to-end decoding. It repurposes existing stabilization methods—manifold alignment (NoMAD, CycleGAN), multi-session transformers (NDT2), test-time adaptation (CORP), and audio-based synthesis (EnSongdec)—into a common evaluation protocol with held-in/held-out session splits, rather than proposing a new decoding architecture. The benchmark's design draws on the iBCI literature's recognition that neural population manifolds are more stable than individual electrode signals, and that the practical challenge is maintaining performance across nonstationary sessions with minimal recalibration data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FALCON provides the field with a rigorous, reproducible selection criterion for choosing decoder-stabilization methods for in-device iBCI use, replacing the prior ad hoc comparison paradigm. A key open question it surfaces is the open-loop-to-closed-loop gap: FALCON evaluates prediction accuracy, but real iBCI control involves sensory feedback and user compensation that can mask or amplify decoder errors, making it unclear whether FALCON performance translates to consistent real-world control. Additional open challenges include subject generalization (all datasets are single-subject), naturalistic behavior (current datasets use cued, trial-structured tasks), and the B1 birdsong task, for which no stabilization method has yet been developed. The finding that even the strongest few-shot baselines show only marginal gains over static decoders on M1 underscores that the nonstationarity problem remains largely unsolved despite years of methodological progress.
