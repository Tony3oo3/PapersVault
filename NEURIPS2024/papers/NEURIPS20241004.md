## Problem

Variational quantum algorithms require deep parametrized quantum circuits for sufficient expressivity, but greater circuit depth causes detrimental error accumulation on noisy near-term hardware and increases the number of parameters (and thus measurement overhead for gradient evaluation). No prior work had explored whether deep equilibrium models (DEQs), which simulate infinite-depth weight-tied networks via a fixed-point solver, could be applied to quantum machine learning models to achieve high performance with shallow circuits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241004/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pixel images (MNIST/FashionMNIST at 28×28 downsampled to 4×4 for the 4-qubit circuit or 10×10 for the 10-qubit circuit; CIFAR-10 at 32×32) and produces a class label among K classes (4 or 10). Images are encoded into quantum states via amplitude or angle encoding onto 4 or 10 qubits, processed through a single parametrized quantum circuit, and read out via a Pauli-Z measurement ensemble per qubit. The K-dimensional measurement vector is then passed through a small linear classifier head with cross-entropy loss.

## Method

QDEQ replaces explicit stacking of L repeated quantum circuit layers with a single shallow PQC whose fixed point z* is found by solving f_θ(z*; x) − z* = 0 using Broyden's method (a quasi-Newton root finder with finite-difference Jacobian updates). Training proceeds via implicit differentiation through the implicit function theorem, avoiding backpropagation through L layers and instead solving a single adjoint linear system per backward pass. The quantum model function per layer consists of data encoding, a parametrized unitary circuit, Pauli-Z measurements, and an upsampling isometry, with input injection at each Broyden iteration. The paper proves that weight-tied QDEQ with input injection is equivalent to a sequence of independently parametrized layers (Theorem 3) and provides a contractiveness argument showing the quantum model family admits fixed points. A warm-up strategy pre-trains with a 2-layer DIRECT (explicit) solver before switching to the IMPLICIT solver, and the approach achieves performance comparable to or exceeding circuits with 5× more layers while using a single shallow circuit.

## Results

On MNIST-4, the QDEQ IMPLICIT+WARMUP solver achieves 93.4% test accuracy with amplitude encoding, outperforming the Wang et al. baseline (85.3%) and the 10-layer DIRECT solver (91.8%) while using less memory (22.0 MB vs. 33.3 MB). On MNIST-10, the IMPLICIT solver reaches 73.68% accuracy using only 273.1 MB of memory, surpassing the 10-layer DIRECT solver (71.18%) which requires 1233.3 MB — a 4.5× memory reduction. On FashionMNIST-10, the IMPLICIT solver achieves 72.11%, competitive with Dilip et al. (63–75%). On CIFAR-10, IMPLICIT+WARMUP (25.45%) outperforms the 10-layer DIRECT baseline (23.71%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Wang et al. (2022b) circuit baseline on MNIST-4, QDEQ improves accuracy by 8.1 points (93.4% vs. 85.3% amplitude; 86.7% vs. 77.3% angle) while using less memory. On MNIST-10, the IMPLICIT solver (73.68%) beats the 10-layer DIRECT solver (71.18%) by 2.5 points while requiring only 22% of its memory (273 MB vs. 1233 MB), and also edges out the Alam et al. PCA baseline (71.75%); however, the Alam VAE baseline (89.80%) remains substantially higher, suggesting data compression is a key gap. On FashionMNIST-10, QDEQ (72.11%) falls within the range reported by Dilip et al. (63–75%) but trails Shen et al. (77–80%), which uses more trainable parameters. West et al. (2024) report higher absolute accuracies (90–92% on MNIST-4, 79.3% on MNIST-10, 74.5% on FashionMNIST) but employ circuits 5–50× deeper (20–200 layers), making the QDEQ advantage in circuit depth the key differentiator. On CIFAR-10, QDEQ (25.45%) outperforms its own DIRECT-10 baseline (23.71%) by 1.7 points, though all near-term QML models struggle on this dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the Deep Equilibrium Model (DEQ) framework of Bai et al. (2019) into the quantum machine learning setting, where the 'layer' is a parametrized quantum circuit (PQC) with data encoding and measurement. While Ahmed et al. (2022) previously explored implicit differentiation for PQCs, this is the first work to apply the full DEQ paradigm—fixed-point solving via Broyden's method combined with implicit gradient computation—to quantum model families. The quantum architecture itself follows the circuit-centric classifier design of Schuld et al. (2020) and the specific 4-qubit circuit of Wang et al. (2022a), extended to 10 qubits via a staircase repetition. The paper also provides a theoretical bridge by proving that weight-tied quantum models with input injection are equivalent to independently parametrized sequences (Theorem 3), mirroring the classical DEQ universality result.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical motivation is reducing circuit depth on noisy, near-term quantum hardware, where each additional layer compounds error accumulation and measurement overhead. The finding that a single implicit QDEQ layer matches or exceeds a 5-layer DIRECT circuit is directly relevant to NISQ-era constraints, though the results are from classical simulation of small (4–10 qubit) circuits and the accuracy gains are modest (1–3 percentage points). Key open questions include quantifying the measurement overhead of the Broyden solver relative to the parameter-shift rule, understanding how shot noise and decoherence affect fixed-point convergence, and whether the depth-reduction benefit persists as circuit scale grows. The theoretical analysis of fixed-point existence (Observation 2) is suggestive but relies on probabilistic arguments about alignment that may not hold for all encoding-measurement combinations.
