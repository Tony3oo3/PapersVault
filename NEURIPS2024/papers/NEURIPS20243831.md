## Problem

Estimating instance-dependent noise transition matrices requires relating the matrices of a few 'special' instances (e.g., anchor points) to those of all others. Existing methods impose predefined similarity assumptions (same class, same manifold, nearest neighbors) that are unverifiable from noisy data alone and may fail in practice, leading to large estimation errors in both transition matrices and clean labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243831/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a noisy dataset of images with unreliable labels (no clean labels initially available) and produces per-instance noise transition matrices, clean class predictions, and learned latent causal variable values. A warmup phase first selects a subset of clean examples via the small-loss trick, after which the full generative model is trained. The noise transition matrix for each instance is read off the noisy-label decoder's output distribution, making it inherently instance-dependent rather than shared across a class or manifold.

## Method

CSGN models the noisy data-generating process as a structural causal model where latent causal variables Z (with causal dependencies among themselves, forming a DAG) generate both the instance X (via a masked subset M_X ⊙ Z plus style variables S) and the noisy label Ỹ (via a different masked subset M_Ỹ ⊙ Z). The causal relations within Z are linear with label-dependent weights W(Y), means μ_N(Y), and variances σ_N²(Y), while the generation of X and Ỹ is nonlinear. Training proceeds in a VAE framework: an encoder infers (Z, S) from (X, Y), two decoders reconstruct X and Ỹ, and a classification network provides the clean-label estimate; the objective combines the ELBO, an L1 sparsity penalty on the masks, and a MixMatch-style semi-supervised loss. Unlike CausalNL and InstanceGM, which assume X directly causes Ỹ, CSGN routes the noisy label through latent causal variables, so instances sharing similar causal variable values automatically share similar transition matrices without any explicit similarity assumption.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 50% instance-dependent noise, CSGN achieves 95.88% test accuracy, outperforming the next-best method DivideMix (86.30%) by nearly 10 percentage points. On the real-world CIFAR-10N (Worst, ~40% noise), CSGN reaches 94.01%, surpassing DivideMix (93.41%) and SOP (93.24%). On WebVision, CSGN attains 79.84% top-1 accuracy, exceeding DivideMix (77.32%) and ELR+ (77.78%). The method also achieves state-of-the-art noise transition matrix estimation error across MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CSGN consistently outperforms all 14 baselines across every noise level and dataset. At the hardest setting (50% instance-dependent noise on CIFAR-10), CSGN (95.88%) beats the strongest competitor DivideMix (86.30%) by 9.6 points, and SOP (63.43%) by over 32 points. On CIFAR-100 at 50% noise, the gap widens further: CSGN (74.60%) vs. DivideMix (61.54%) and SOP (60.03%). On real-world CIFAR-10N (Worst), CSGN (94.01%) edges out DivideMix (93.41%) and SOP (93.24%), while on CIFAR-100N it leads by 2.5 points over DivideMix (71.99% vs. 69.45%). On WebVision, CSGN achieves 79.84% top-1, surpassing DivideMix (77.32%) and ELR+ (77.78%). The causal-based baselines CausalNL and InstanceGM degrade severely at high noise rates (e.g., CausalNL drops to 57.93% at 50% noise on CIFAR-10), whereas CSGN maintains near-constant accuracy, demonstrating the advantage of learning latent causal structure over predefined similarity assumptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

CSGN extends the line of causal label-noise learning initiated by CausalNL (Yao et al., NeurIPS 2021) and InstanceGM (Garg et al., WACV 2023), which model noisy label generation under a structural causal model. The key departure is that prior methods assume the observed instance X is a direct cause of the noisy label, whereas CSGN introduces latent causal variables Z (with causal dependencies among them) that mediate between X and the noisy label, and uses learnable masks to select different subsets of Z for generating instances versus noisy labels. The identifiability theory builds on Liu et al. (2022) for weight-variant latent causal models, and the training procedure leverages the VAE framework and MixMatch-style semi-supervised learning for the warmup phase.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By learning the latent causal structure, CSGN provides a principled, assumption-free mechanism for relating noise transition matrices across instances, avoiding the ad-hoc similarity assumptions (class-level, manifold-level, k-NN) that prior instance-dependent methods rely on and that are hard to verify with only noisy data. The method achieves consistent SOTA across synthetic and real-world benchmarks, with particularly large gains at high noise rates (e.g., ~10 points over DivideMix at 50% IDN on CIFAR-10). Key limitations include the requirement for a subset of clean examples (via the small-loss trick), the assumption of linear causal relations among latent variables, and the additional computational cost of maintaining generative decoders. Open questions include extending to nonlinear causal relations among Z and reducing the number of clean examples needed for identifiability.
