## Problem

Australia lacks a publicly available large-scale word-level Auslan dataset for isolated sign language recognition, while existing ISLR datasets worldwide are limited in vocabulary size, lack depth information, or are captured from a single viewpoint. This gap is particularly urgent given that over 3.6 million Australians have hearing loss, and the number is projected to reach 7.8 million by 2060.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242226/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each data sample comprises synchronized RGB-D video from one of four cameras (three Kinect-V2 at left-front, front, and right-front positions, plus one centrally placed RealSense), along with camera intrinsic/extrinsic parameters, AlphaPose-estimated 2D/3D pose sequences, gloss identity, corresponding English word/phrase, and signer identity. The recognition task maps these multi-modal inputs to one of 3,215 Auslan gloss classes, evaluated via Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy across four test subsets (studio, in-the-wild, synthetic background, and temporal disturbance) that progressively increase environmental difficulty.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the construction of MM-WLAuslan and a comprehensive benchmark across six ISLR settings. Data is recorded in a green-screen studio with four RGB-D cameras positioned hemispherically around the signer's front half, capturing 282,900 videos of 3,215 glosses from 73 signers (22 repetitions per gloss per camera). Background removal uses AlphaPose keypoints with fixed-size cropping and eye-level alignment, and the test set is split into four subsets simulating real-world challenges (dynamic backgrounds, synthetic backgrounds, and temporal disturbances). Benchmarking evaluates 14+ SOTA models (pixel-based: I3D, S3D, SlowFast, UMDR, KVNet-V; pose-based: TGCN, ST-GCN, SPOTER, DSTA-SLR, STC-SLR, KVNet-K; multi-modal: SAM-SLR, NLA-SLR) under single-view RGB, single-view RGB-D, multi-view RGB, multi-view RGB-D, cross-camera, and cross-view settings, revealing significant performance drops under cross-camera and cross-view conditions.

## Results

On the standard single-view RGB setting (Table 3), NLA-SLR achieves the best average Top-1 accuracy of 81.90% and Top-5 of 95.16% across the four test subsets (STU, ITW, SYN, TED). In the single-view RGB-D setting (Table 4), SAM-SLR leads with 84.11% Top-1 AVG, while in multi-view RGB (Table 5) and multi-view RGB-D (Table 6), NLA-SLR again achieves the highest scores at 89.63% and 89.51% Top-1 AVG respectively. Cross-camera and cross-view evaluations reveal severe generalization failures: KVNet-V drops from 62.82% to 19.21% Top-1 AVG when trained on RealSense and tested on Kinect-V2, and from 62.82% to 14.53% when trained on the front view and tested on left+right views.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NLA-SLR [14] (ensemble of KVNet-V and KVNet-K) consistently outperforms all other baselines across every ISLR setting, achieving 81.90% Top-1 AVG in single-view RGB vs. 76.82% for KVNet-K and 75.55% for DSTA-SLR [50]. In the RGB-D setting, SAM-SLR [15] edges out NLA-SLR (84.11% vs. 83.14% Top-1 AVG), while UMDR [73] leads among pixel+depth-only models at 84.07%. Pose-based methods (DSTA-SLR, STC-SLR, KVNet-K) substantially outperform pixel-based methods in challenging ITW and SYN subsets (e.g., DSTA-SLR 74.96% vs. I3D 14.18% on ITW), confirming that structural pose features are more robust to background variation. Cross-camera experiments expose a critical weakness: KVNet-V drops from 62.82% to 19.21% (K→RS) and UMDR from 84.07% to 48.80% (K→RS), while cross-view drops are even more severe (KVNet-V F→L+R: 62.82%→14.53%). Among action-recognition backbones, SlowFast [9] (58.07%) and S3D [8] (52.94%) outperform I3D [6] (43.82%) and TSN [71] (33.41%) in single-view RGB, but all remain well below dedicated ISLR methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first large-scale word-level Auslan ISLR dataset, filling a gap where no publicly available Australian sign language corpus existed for isolated sign recognition. Conceptually, it extends the ISLR dataset lineage (WLASL, MS-ASL, ASL Citizen, BSL-1K, CSL 500) by simultaneously addressing three dimensions that prior datasets handle in isolation: data volume, vocabulary breadth, and multi-modal camera diversity. The benchmarking framework repurposes existing SOTA ISLR methods (KVNet, NLA-SLR, SAM-SLR, UMDR) and action recognition models (I3D, SlowFast, Timesformer) under new evaluation protocols—cross-camera and cross-view—that no prior ISLR dataset supported, thereby testing hardware and viewpoint generalization in a way that single-view corpora cannot.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset is practically important as the largest ISLR corpus to date (282K+ videos, 3,215 glosses, 73 signers) and the only multi-view, multi-modal Auslan resource, enabling research on view invariance, camera robustness, and multi-modal fusion for sign language. The benchmark reveals that current SOTA methods suffer dramatic accuracy collapses under cross-camera (e.g., UMDR drops from 84% to 6% average Top-1) and cross-view settings, exposing a significant gap in generalization that remains open. Limitations include underrepresentation of African signers, studio-centric recording that lacks multi-person interactions, and the absence of error bars, which temper confidence in the reported comparisons. Future directions highlighted include sign language production, real-world data collection, and developing multi-view fusion methods that avoid the information redundancy penalty observed with NLA-SLR.
