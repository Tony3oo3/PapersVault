## Problem

In multiclass classification over n outcomes, consistent convex surrogate losses require prediction dimension d ≥ n−1, which is computationally infeasible for large n (e.g., information retrieval, structured prediction). Practitioners routinely embed outcomes into d ≪ n dimensions but lack rigorous tools to characterize when such partially-consistent surrogates are reliable. This paper formalizes partial consistency, identifies the worst-case failure mode (hallucinations), and provides a framework for trading off consistency guarantees against surrogate dimensionality.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite label space Y of size n, a d-dimensional polytope P with n vertices, a bijection φ assigning outcomes to vertices, and a strictly convex function G defining a Bregman divergence. The surrogate loss L_φ^G(u, y) = D_G(u, v_y) is minimized over u ∈ R^d, and the MAP link ψ^φ projects the optimal report onto P and selects the nearest vertex as the predicted label. Calibration guarantees are stated over a restricted subset P ⊆ Δ_Y of the simplex (e.g., a low-noise region Θ_α), not the full simplex, reflecting the d < n−1 dimension violation. For the multi-instance construction, the input is m separate cross-polytope embedding problems each in R^d, and the output is an aggregated mode prediction via a comparison-based aggregation function.

## Method

The paper constructs surrogates by embedding n outcomes as vertices of an arbitrary d-dimensional polytope P and defining the loss via a Bregman divergence D_G between the surrogate report u and the vertex v_y, paired with a MAP link that projects onto P and selects the nearest vertex. The key negative result (Theorem 3) shows that whenever d < n−1, a non-empty hallucination region always exists where the surrogate predicts an outcome with zero probability mass, characterized as the union of convex hulls of all-but-one vertices intersected with the corresponding link level set. On the positive side, Theorem 4 guarantees non-zero Lebesgue measure strict calibration regions near each vertex, and Theorem 5 shows that under a Massart low-noise assumption (max_y p_y ≥ 1−α), calibration holds for any polytope embedding with an appropriately scaled link; explicit bounds are given for the unit cube (α < 0.5, independent of d) and the permutahedron (α < 1/d, shrinking exponentially). Finally, Theorem 10 sidesteps the n−1 lower bound entirely by using m ∈ [2d−1, d(2d−1)] parallel cross-polytope instances in R^d, each eliciting pairwise probability comparisons, which are aggregated via a sorting algorithm to recover the full mode over 2d outcomes—trading a single high-dimensional optimization for multiple low-dimensional ones that are embarrassingly parallel.

## Results

The paper establishes several key theoretical results. Theorem 3 proves that for any polytope embedding with d < n−1, the hallucination region H is never empty, meaning there always exist distributions where the surrogate predicts an outcome with zero true probability. Theorem 4 guarantees that non-zero Lebesgue-measurable strict calibration regions always exist for any polytope embedding. Theorem 5 shows that every polytope embedding becomes ℓ₀₋₁-calibrated under a sufficiently strong low-noise assumption (α < 0.5). Corollary 7 establishes that binary encoding into a d-dimensional unit cube is calibrated for all α ∈ [0, 0.5), while Corollary 8 shows permutahedron embedding of d! outcomes into d dimensions is calibrated only for α ∈ [0, 1/d). Finally, Theorem 10 demonstrates that the mode over 2d outcomes is fully elicitable via O(d²) cross-polytope problem instances, each operating in d dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments, datasets, or baseline comparisons. The paper's contributions are framed relative to prior theoretical work: it extends the binary consistency framework of Ramaswamy and Agarwal [2016] (which requires d ≥ n−1) to a partial consistency paradigm, provides the first rigorous characterization of hallucination regions for low-dimensional surrogates, and offers a constructive method (Theorem 10) to recover full consistency via multiple parallel problem instances. Unlike Agarwal and Agarwal [2015], who impose a low-noise assumption with d = log(n) but provide no control over the consistency-dimension trade-off, this paper gives practitioners fine-grained control via the parameter α and the choice of polytope embedding. The permutahedron result (Corollary 8) shows that factorial embedding shrinks the calibration region exponentially in d, suggesting it is impractical without strong distributional priors, whereas the unit cube result (Corollary 7) offers a dimension-independent α bound of 0.5.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the property elicitation and surrogate loss framework of Agarwal and Agarwal (2015) and Finocchiaro et al. (2024) by moving beyond the binary consistent/inconsistent dichotomy to a formal partial consistency paradigm. It builds on Blondel's (2019) projection-based losses and Bregman divergence construction, but departs from prior work by explicitly analyzing what happens when the surrogate dimension d violates the known n−1 lower bound. Unlike Ramaswamy and Agarwal (2016), who establish the lower bound, or Struminsky et al. (2018), who characterize excess risk of inconsistent surrogates, this paper provides a geometric (polytope-based) toolkit for practitioners to reason about the consistency-dimension trade-off and to recover full consistency via parallel problem instances.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The guaranteed existence of hallucination regions (Theorem 3) is a strong negative result: any multiclass classifier using a surrogate with d < n−1 will, for some distribution, confidently predict a class that has zero true probability. This has direct implications for high-stakes applications (medical, legal) where practitioners routinely use low-dimensional encodings. The low-noise calibration results (Theorems 5–8) give actionable guidance on when specific embeddings (unit cube, permutahedron) are safe to use, while the multiple-instance construction (Theorem 10) offers a parallelizable path to full consistency. Open questions include the practical frequency of hallucination reports, efficient identification of strict calibration regions, and extension to cost-sensitive or structured prediction settings.
