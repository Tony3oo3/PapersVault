## Problem

Learning from uncertain data—where features or labels may be missing, erroneous, or biased—requires reasoning over all possible dataset variations (possible worlds) to assess model robustness. Existing approaches either handle only label uncertainty with interval arithmetic (which ignores correlations between weights and labels, yielding overly conservative bounds) or focus on test-time robustness rather than training-time effects. This paper addresses the gap of computing sound, tight over-approximations of all possible optimal linear models and their prediction ranges under arbitrary feature and label uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a zonotope representation of the uncertain training dataset (encoding all possible worlds via shared error symbols), a test data point (which may itself be uncertain), a learning rate η, a regularization coefficient λ, and a transformation matrix A for order reduction. It produces a zonotope of abstract model parameters w*# from which prediction ranges V(x) are computed as [min, max] over the concretization, and robustness certificates indicating whether the prediction interval falls below a threshold. The zonotope representation preserves correlations between data uncertainty and model weights through shared error symbols, enabling joint concretization for fixed-point verification.

## Method

The core technique is abstract gradient descent in the zonotope domain: an exact abstract transformer Φ_exact# symbolically executes one gradient descent step over all possible datasets simultaneously, but generates polynomial zonotopes whose order grows exponentially with iterations. To make this tractable, the paper decomposes the abstract gradient into three components—Φ_R (real part, closed-form), Φ_D# (data-dependent symbolic terms, linear with constant symbol count), and Φ_N# (data-independent nonlinear terms requiring linearization and order reduction). The key insight is a transformation-based interval hull (TIH) order reduction operator applied to Φ_N# that guarantees the existence of a fixed point and yields a non-trivial closed-form solution for ridge regression. Algorithm 1 constructs the fixed point w*# = w_R* + w_D#* + w_N#* sequentially: w_R* via the standard ridge closed-form on the real data, w_D#* by solving a linear system equating coefficients of shared error symbols, and w_N#* by solving a system of k equations enforcing equal interval-hull edge lengths in the projected space.

## Results

On the MPG dataset (392 instances) and Insurance dataset (1,338 instances), ZORRO consistently certifies significantly higher robustness ratios than the MEYER baseline across all uncertainty configurations. At higher uncertainty radius values, MEYER fails to certify robustness for most test instances, while ZORRO still certifies robustness for 100% of instances (robustness threshold: 5% of label range for MPG, 0.8% for Insurance). ZORRO's over-approximation of the worst-case loss range tightly tracks the ground truth computed by exhaustive enumeration of all possible worlds, with the gap widening only as uncertainty radius increases due to higher-order linearization errors. The tightness of the over-approximation is unaffected by data dimensionality, and a small positive regularization coefficient (λ ≈ 0.02–0.025) yields the best worst-case test loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline is MEYER [47] (Meyer et al., FAccT 2023), which uses interval arithmetic to over-approximate prediction ranges for linear regression under training label uncertainty. ZORRO wins decisively on the robustness ratio metric across all tested configurations of uncertain data percentage and uncertainty radius on both MPG and Insurance datasets. The paper attributes MEYER's inferior performance to interval arithmetic's failure to capture correlations between model weight dimensions and between training labels and weights, resulting in overly conservative (wider) prediction intervals. At high uncertainty radius, the gap is stark: MEYER certifies robustness for a small fraction of test points while ZORRO maintains 100% certification. MEYER also cannot handle feature uncertainty at all, whereas ZORRO extends naturally to uncertain features (though with somewhat lower robustness ratios than the label-uncertainty case due to additional higher-order terms). No other baselines are directly compared, as the paper notes there is no existing method that computes prediction ranges for linear regression trained on uncertain data with both feature and label uncertainty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dataset multiplicity framework of Meyer et al. [47], which used possible world semantics and interval arithmetic to handle label-only uncertainty in linear regression, by generalizing to arbitrary feature and label uncertainty and replacing interval arithmetic with zonotope-based abstract interpretation. It draws on the abstract interpretation tradition (Cousot & Cousot) and zonotope reachability analysis (Althoff, Kochdumper) to perform symbolic execution of gradient descent across all possible datasets simultaneously. The key departure from prior robustness certification work (Mirman, Gehr, Vechev) is the focus on training-time robustness of an iterative learning algorithm rather than test-time input perturbation, and the introduction of abstract fixed points with closed-form solutions for ridge regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first sound, computationally tractable method for certifying prediction ranges and parameter robustness under training-data uncertainty for iterative learning algorithms, which is critical in high-stakes settings (medical prediction, causal inference) where data quality issues are systematic rather than random. The closed-form fixed-point solution is a significant theoretical contribution that avoids the exponential blowup of naive symbolic gradient descent. However, the approach is currently limited to linear models (ridge regression); extending to non-linear models like neural networks would require more advanced linearization and order reduction techniques, and the empirical evaluation is restricted to two small regression datasets, leaving open whether the tightness of over-approximations holds at scale.
