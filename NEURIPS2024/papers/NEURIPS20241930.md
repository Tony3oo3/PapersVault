## Problem

Existing gradient coding protocols coarsely classify workers as either fully operational or failed, discarding the potentially valuable partial work performed by slow workers (partial stragglers). Moreover, communication-efficient gradient coding (reducing transmitted vector dimension from d to d/ℓ) relies on polynomial interpolation that is numerically unstable for more than ~20 workers, and there are essentially no schemes for communication-efficient *approximate* gradient coding with general assignment matrices.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241930/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The protocol takes as input the assignment matrix A (N×m), a shared random ℓ×m matrix R with i.i.d. N(0,1) entries, and a length-m state vector ψ broadcast by the PS indicating how many chunks each worker has processed. Each worker independently computes its encoding coefficients via a minimum-ℓ₂-norm least-squares problem on a submatrix of indeterminates, then transmits a single encoded gradient vector of dimension d/ℓ. The PS recovers the full gradient (exactly when ≥ℓ copies of every chunk are processed, or approximately otherwise) and can compute an analytical MSE estimate of the reconstruction error at any point in the computation.

## Method

The PS generates a random ℓ×m matrix R and shares it with all workers; workers periodically report their processed-chunk counts, and the PS broadcasts an encode-and-transmit signal along with the global state vector ψ. Each worker constructs an m×Nℓ indeterminate matrix B (populated only where it has processed the corresponding chunk), extracts its relevant submatrix B̃ᵢ, and solves a minimum-ℓ₂-norm least-squares problem to determine its encoding coefficients — the minimum-norm constraint guarantees all workers independently arrive at identical coefficient values without further coordination. This replaces the numerically unstable polynomial interpolation of prior communication-efficient GC with well-conditioned least-squares systems whose condition number scales as O(log Δ) for random Gaussian R. For the chunk-ordering problem (critical when leveraging partial stragglers), the paper presents a polynomial-time algorithm that decomposes the regular bipartite graph of the assignment matrix into δ perfect matchings via Hall's theorem, yielding an optimal ordering that minimizes the worst-case number of chunks processed before every chunk has at least one completed copy.

## Results

For exact gradient reconstruction (N=m=200, δ=8), the proposed protocol completes in approximately half the time of the original GC protocol across all values of ℓ (Fig. 3b). For approximate gradient reconstruction on Ramanujan graphs (m=200, degree ν=8, α=ν−1 failures), the MSE of the proposed protocol is several orders of magnitude lower than the original GC protocol with ℓ=1, and this gap widens with increasing time T (Fig. 3a). Crucially, even with ℓ≥2 (which reduces per-worker communication to d/ℓ), the proposed protocol achieves lower MSE than the original protocol operating at ℓ=1. The theoretical MSE estimate from Eq. (6) closely tracks the empirically observed error, enabling the PS to decide when to trigger encoding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares exclusively against the original gradient coding protocol of Tandon et al. [5] (cyclic assignment for exact GC; least-squares decoding for approximate GC), evaluated at ℓ=1 since the communication-efficient variants of the original protocol (ℓ≥2) rely on Lagrange interpolation and are numerically unstable for m≥20 workers (Appendix A). For exact GC, the proposed protocol achieves roughly 2× faster completion time across all tested ℓ values and both assignment matrices (cyclic and Ramanujan-graph-based). For approximate GC, the proposed protocol's MSE is several orders of magnitude lower than the original protocol at every time T, and this advantage persists even when the proposed protocol uses ℓ≥2 (reducing communication to d/ℓ per worker) while the baseline operates at ℓ=1. The paper also shows that the optimal chunk ordering from Algorithm 2 consistently outperforms the best-of-100 random orderings in both MSE (approximate GC) and completion time (exact GC), confirming the importance of ordering when leveraging partial stragglers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient coding framework of Tandon et al. (ICML 2017) by introducing an interactive protocol that exploits partial work from slow (but not failed) workers, a direction previously explored only for exact GC via multi-message schemes [10, 24–28]. It departs from the communication-efficient GC protocols of Ye & Abbe (ICML 2018) and Jahani-Nezhad & Maddah-Ali (2021), which rely on Lagrange/rational polynomial interpolation and suffer from severe numerical instability beyond ~20 workers, by replacing interpolation with a per-worker minimum-ℓ₂-norm least-squares solve that is provably well-conditioned. The chunk-ordering algorithm in Section 3 adapts the Q_max worst-case metric from coded matrix computation (Das & Ramamoorthy, IEEE ITW 2018) to the GC setting and solves it optimally via bipartite matching decomposition of regular graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first numerically stable protocol for communication-efficient approximate gradient coding, filling a gap where prior methods (polynomial interpolation) become unusable at realistic cluster sizes. The ~2× reduction in completion time for exact GC and orders-of-magnitude MSE improvement for approximate GC are practically significant for cloud-based distributed training. Key limitations include the chunk-ordering algorithm's restriction to N = m with uniform degree δ, and the absence of measurements on actual cloud platforms for the communication-time savings. Open questions include extending the optimal ordering to non-regular assignment matrices and to ℓ > 1, and validating the protocol's gains in production clusters with heterogeneous and time-varying worker speeds.
