## Problem

Existing AI-generated text detection methods rely solely on next-token prediction difficulty in surrogate LLM output logits, ignoring the equally informative memorization of preceding tokens encoded in those same logits. This feature insufficiency, combined with contextual heterogeneity across text domains, causes most baselines to drop below 80% F1 on the latest commercial LLMs (e.g., Claude-3-Opus), leaving a significant gap in reliable detection.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single subject text (natural language or code, ranging from ~10 to ~41,000 tokens) and produces a binary label indicating whether it is AI-generated or human-written. The input text is first summarized by a surrogate LLM to generate a guidance prompt, then split into a short prefix segment and a longer completion segment; the completion segment is fed through six open-source LLMs in parallel via teacher forcing to extract per-token logits. The final output is a single classification score from a lightweight binary classifier (random forest) trained on concatenated statistical features, with no fine-tuning of the detection LLMs required.

## Method

BISCOPE introduces a bi-directional cross-entropy loss computation that exploits both the prediction (forward CE between output logits and the next ground-truth token) and memorization (backward CE between output logits and the immediately preceding input token) information encoded in a causal LLM's output logits. The key insight is that for human-written text, the surrogate LLM exhibits poor next-token prediction but strong preceding-token memorization, while for AI-generated text the pattern is reversed, providing a complementary discriminative signal. The four-step pipeline is: (1) generate a text summary to guide completion and split the text into prefix and completion segments; (2) compute FCE and BCE at every token position using teacher forcing across six open-source LLMs (Gemma-2B/7B, Llama-2-7B/13B, Mistral-7B, Llama-3-8B) in parallel; (3) partition the text into 10 segments and extract mean, max, min, and standard deviation of both loss types per segment to form a length-invariant feature vector; (4) train a binary classifier on the concatenated multi-model, multi-segment features. This design requires only a single forward pass per LLM (no iterative generation), achieving sub-200ms per-sample inference while reaching >0.95 average F1 across five datasets and five commercial LLMs.

## Results

On the in-distribution normal dataset, BISCOPE (with text summary guidance, BISCOPE*) achieves an average F1 score of 0.95 across five datasets (Arxiv, Yelp, Creative, Essay, Code) generated by five latest commercial LLMs (GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4-Turbo, Claude-3-Sonnet, Claude-3-Opus, Gemini-1.0-Pro), exceeding the best non-commercial baselines by over 0.30 F1 on average. Without summary guidance, BISCOPE still reaches 0.94 average F1 while processing each sample in 0.14s (vs. up to 27s for Raidar). On the paraphrased dataset, BISCOPE maintains or slightly improves its F1 score (0.95 average), while baselines drop by ~0.03 F1. BISCOPE outperforms the commercial tool GPTZero in 72% of the 25 dataset×LLM cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BISCOPE outperforms all nine baselines across all five datasets in the in-distribution setting. The closest competitor is GhostBuster, which reaches 0.976 F1 on Arxiv and 0.997 on Essay, but BISCOPE still leads by 0.008–0.002 F1 on those datasets and by a much larger margin (0.16 F1) on Code, where GhostBuster drops to 0.673. Binoculars is competitive on long-form text (0.984 on Creative, 0.997 on Essay) but collapses on Code (0.662) and Yelp (0.734), while BISCOPE maintains 0.835+ on Code and 0.942 on Yelp. RADAR, the strongest training-based baseline, achieves 0.925 on Creative and 0.953 on Essay but only 0.662 on Code, a 0.17 F1 gap versus BISCOPE. On the paraphrased dataset, BISCOPE actually improves slightly (0.954 avg F1) while baselines degrade by ~0.03 F1, demonstrating superior paraphrase robustness. Against the commercial GPTZero tool (pre-trained on millions of samples), BISCOPE wins in 72% of cases, with a notable 0.19 F1 advantage on Code, though GPTZero leads by 0.04 F1 on Yelp.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the surrogate-model statistical detection family (LogRank, DetectGPT, Binoculars, GhostBuster, etc.) by identifying that output logits encode not only next-token prediction information but also preceding-token memorization information, and formalizing both as a bi-directional cross-entropy loss. It departs from prior work in two specific ways: (1) adding backward cross-entropy (BCE) as a complementary feature to the standard forward cross-entropy (FCE), and (2) introducing a text-summary-guided completion prompt to reduce contextual heterogeneity. The paper also carves new empirical ground by constructing a large-scale dataset from five latest commercial LLMs (GPT-4-Turbo, Claude-3-Opus, Gemini-1.0-Pro, etc.), which prior detection benchmarks had not covered.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that backward cross-entropy (memorization) is more discriminative than forward cross-entropy (prediction) for AI-text detection challenges the field's default assumption that next-token prediction difficulty is the primary signal, potentially redirecting future feature engineering. The >0.30 F1 gap over non-commercial SOTA and outperformance of GPTZero in 72% of cases with only ~4,000 training samples demonstrates practical viability. However, the OOD cross-dataset setting still shows a >0.10 F1 drop, and the method's reliance on multiple open-source surrogate LLMs in parallel raises deployment cost questions. Open questions include whether the memorization signal generalizes to multilingual or multimodal settings, and whether further exploitation of internal model states (beyond output logits) could close the remaining gap.
