## Problem

Existing differentiable structure learning (CSL) methods default to the least squares loss, which is not scale-invariant, is susceptible to varsortability artifacts, and lacks provable guarantees for recovering meaningful DAG structures in nonidentifiable models. The paper asks: what score function, combined with the continuous acyclicity constraint, provably identifies the sparsest model in the Markov equivalence class without requiring an identifiable parameterization?

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. tabular samples of p variables and optimizes over a continuous adjacency matrix B ∈ R^{p×p} subject to a differentiable acyclicity constraint h(B)=0 and positive noise variances Ω > 0. The output is a DAG adjacency matrix and noise covariance, interpreted as the sparsest element of the Markov equivalence class (i.e., a CPDAG), since the linear Gaussian model is inherently nonidentifiable and multiple (B, Ω) pairs induce the same distribution. The quasi-MCP hyperparameters λ and δ must be chosen sufficiently small (independent of n) for the theoretical guarantees to hold.

## Method

The core contribution is a fully differentiable score function combining the negative log-likelihood (NLL) with a quasi-MCP penalty (a reparameterized MCP that is quadratic near zero and constant beyond a threshold δ, approximating ℓ₀ for large coefficients). For the linear Gaussian SEM, the NLL alone has a finite equivalence class of global minimizers (at most p! pairs (B,Ω) inducing the same precision matrix), and the quasi-MCP penalty breaks this degeneracy by selecting the pair with the fewest nonzero edges. The paper proves that for all sufficiently small λ, δ > 0, the global minimizers of the regularized problem converge in probability to the minimal equivalence class E_min(Θ), which under faithfulness equals the Markov equivalence class. The score is also shown to be scale-invariant (rescaling or standardizing data does not change the recovered DAG structure), addressing the varsortability concern. In practice, the optimization is solved with the augmented Lagrangian algorithm from NOTEARS, replacing the LS+ℓ₁ objective with the NLL+quasi-MCP objective, requiring no Gumbel-Softmax or other discrete-relaxation approximations.

## Results

The paper evaluates structure recovery using Structural Hamming Distance (SHD) between the Markov equivalence classes (MECs) of the estimated and ground-truth DAGs, with p ∈ {10, 20, 50, 70, 100} nodes, n = 1000 samples, and ER/SF graphs with k ∈ {1, 2, 4} expected edges. LOGLL-NOTEARS (NLL + quasi-MCP) achieves the best SHD across most graph types and ranks second-best on the sparsest graphs (ER-1, SF-1). Under data standardization, NOTEARS, GOLEM, and DAGMA degrade to SHD values no better than an empty graph, while LOGLL-NOTEARS remains robust; for p = 10, LOGLL-NOTEARS matches the scale invariance of PC and FGES, and for p = 50 the degradation is only mild.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LOGLL-NOTEARS outperforms NOTEARS, GOLEM, and DAGMA on both raw and standardized data, with the gap widening dramatically under standardization where those three methods collapse to empty-graph-level SHD while LOGLL-NOTEARS retains meaningful structure recovery. Against VarSort (sortnregress), LOGLL-NOTEARS is substantially better; VarSort's average SHD on standardized p = 50 data exceeds 300 and is omitted from the comparison figure entirely. Against the constraint-based baselines PC and FGES, LOGLL-NOTEARS is competitive: all three methods exhibit scale invariance at p = 10, and LOGLL-NOTEARS achieves the best or near-best SHD on raw data across most graph types. The only setting where LOGLL-NOTEARS is not strictly best is the sparsest graphs (ER-1, SF-1), where it ranks second. For larger graphs (p = 50), standardization mildly degrades LOGLL-NOTEARS due to increased optimization difficulty, but the degradation is far less severe than for the LS-based baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the continuous structure learning (CSL) framework initiated by NOTEARS (Zheng et al., 2018), which introduced differentiable acyclicity constraints for DAG learning, by replacing the default least squares score with a log-likelihood score regularized by a quasi-MCP penalty (a reparameterization of Zhang's 2010 MCP). It departs from the prevailing CSL practice of using LS loss—which Reisach et al. (2021) and Ng et al. (2024) showed is susceptible to varsortability and scale sensitivity—by providing the first provable statistical guarantees that the global optimum of the acyclicity-constrained program recovers the sparsest DAG in the Markov equivalence class. The theoretical framework also draws on the sparsest Markov representation (SMR) concept from Raskutti and Uhler (2018) and connects CSL to classical Bayesian network theory (faithfulness, CPDAGs, Markov equivalence).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a critical gap in CSL by showing that the choice of score function—not just the acyclicity constraint—determines whether the optimizer recovers a meaningful causal structure, and that a properly regularized log-likelihood is provably scale-invariant and identifies the sparsest Markov equivalent DAG. The practical implication is that standardizing data (a common preprocessing step) no longer destroys structure recovery, addressing a major empirical failure mode of existing CSL methods. Key limitations and open questions include: the theoretical guarantees require global optimization (which becomes intractable for larger p, as evidenced by mild degradation at p=50 in experiments), the finiteness assumption on the equivalence class restricts applicability to parametric models, and extensions to nonparametric models, interventional data, and relaxing the faithfulness assumption remain future work.
