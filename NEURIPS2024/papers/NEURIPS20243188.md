## Problem

Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022) derived influential but contradictory compute-optimal scaling laws for language models, predicting scaling exponents of ~0.88 and ~0.5 respectively for optimal model size as a function of compute budget. Hoffmann et al. hypothesized the discrepancy stemmed from Kaplan et al. not tailoring learning rate decay per token budget, but no prior work had systematically identified the actual causes. This paper resolves the discrepancy by isolating three concrete factors and disproving the learning-rate-decay hypothesis.

## Input / Output

Given a compute budget C (in FLOPs), the method predicts the optimal model size N*(C), optimal token count D*(C) = C/(6N*), and the token-to-parameter ratio ρ*(C) = C/(6[N*]²), all expressed as power laws in C. The FLOP grid spans {1.25e16 · 2^i} for i = 0..11, and model sizes range from 5M to 901M parameters. As a secondary output, the paper derives power-law scaling relations for optimal learning rate and batch size as functions of model size N, which are then applied to select hyperparameters for all model sizes in the grid.

## Method

The paper performs a progressive ablation over 900+ training runs using a Llama-derived decoder-only Transformer (SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, (qk)-LayerNorm) trained via OpenLM on OpenWebText2 and RefinedWeb. Starting from a faithful reproduction of Kaplan et al.'s setup (exponent a ≈ 0.86), it sequentially corrects: (1) the FLOP count to include the final linear head layer (shifting a to ~0.70), (2) the warmup duration from a fixed 1.57B tokens to N tokens per model (shifting a to ~0.60), and (3) per-model-size tuning of learning rate, batch size, and AdamW β₂ (shifting a to ~0.50, matching Hoffmann et al.). Critically, adding cosine learning rate decay matched to each FLOP budget (Hoffmann's hypothesized fix) only shifts a from 0.60 to 0.57, demonstrating it is not the primary cause. The optimal N*(C) at each FLOP grid point is estimated via a noise-and-interpolate procedure using Akima interpolation in log-space with empirically calibrated Gaussian noise, and power-law exponents are fitted by weighted linear regression with log-space Gaussian MLE weights.

## Results

The paper identifies three factors that explain the discrepancy between Kaplan et al.'s scaling exponent (a=0.88) and Hoffmann et al.'s (a=0.5): (1) counting the last-layer FLOPs shifts a from ~0.84 to ~0.70, (2) scaling warmup duration with model size shifts a to ~0.60, and (3) tuning batch size, learning rate, and AdamW β₂ per model size brings a to 0.518 (OpenWebText2) and 0.497 (RefinedWeb), matching Hoffmann et al. within 0.6%. The predicted optimal model size at Chinchilla's compute budget (C=5.88e23) is within 15% of Chinchilla's 70B parameters. Notably, the final configuration uses a constant learning rate schedule, disproving Hoffmann et al.'s hypothesis that careful learning rate decay is essential for their scaling law to hold.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Kaplan et al. [30], the paper first faithfully reproduces their scaling law (a=0.864/0.835 vs. their reported 0.88), then systematically shows that three methodological choices — excluding the model head from FLOP counts, using a fixed 3000-step warmup, and using a fixed batch size of 2^19 — each inflate the exponent. Against Hoffmann et al. [25], the paper achieves near-exact agreement on the scaling exponent (a=0.497–0.518 vs. their 0.5) and predicts the Chinchilla-scale optimal model size within 15%. Critically, the paper disproves Hoffmann et al.'s central hypothesis: adding a per-budget cosine learning rate decay (their conjectured key fix) only shifts a from 0.60 to 0.57, while the remaining gap is closed by tuning batch size, learning rate, and β₂ — and the final matching result uses a constant learning rate with no decay at all. The paper also reproduces Kaplan et al.'s 'adjusted' scaling law (a=0.73) by re-introducing the FLOP and warmup issues while keeping tuned hyperparameters, confirming the decomposition is consistent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly addresses the long-standing discrepancy between the Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022, 'Chinchilla') compute-optimal scaling laws, which have produced substantially different predictions for optimal model size given a compute budget. Methodologically, it adopts the IsoFLOP analysis framework from Hoffmann et al. and the Llama-derived architecture/training stack via OpenLM, while drawing on DeepSeek's approach of fitting power laws for optimal hyperparameters as a function of model size. The paper departs from Hoffmann et al.'s hypothesis by demonstrating that learning rate decay is not the primary cause of the discrepancy, instead attributing it to three more mundane factors: FLOP counting of the model head, warmup duration, and scale-dependent optimizer tuning (batch size, learning rate, and AdamW β₂).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Resolving this discrepancy is important because the two scaling laws led to fundamentally different model design decisions in practice (e.g., GPT-3 followed Kaplan, Chinchilla followed Hoffmann), and understanding the true cause prevents future scaling experiments from being confounded by methodological artifacts. The finding that a constant learning rate schedule suffices for the Chinchilla exponent to emerge is practically significant, as it eliminates the need to commit to a training duration in advance and roughly halves the compute cost of scaling law experiments. Key limitations include the relatively small compute scale (comparable to Kaplan et al., ~3 orders of magnitude below Chinchilla), the focus on pretraining loss rather than downstream capabilities, and the concern that hyperparameters were tuned at a single token-to-parameter ratio. Open questions include whether the Hoffmann exponent holds in vision and other domains, and whether data repetition or dataset quality shifts the optimal ratio.
