## Problem

Estimating treatment effects from natural experiments is challenging because treatments are pre-assigned and often correlated with both outcomes and the treatment effect itself. Applying over 20 established estimators to the same real-world dataset (an early childhood literacy program) produces wildly inconsistent results, yet no comprehensive benchmark exists to determine which estimators are actually accurate under realistic conditions like varying sample sizes, propensity score accuracy, and treatment-outcome correlation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data where each observation (a grade-level class in the RORCO dataset) has 78 covariates, a binary treatment indicator, a single observed outcome (either treatment or control, never both), and estimated propensity scores truncated to [0.01, 0.99]. It outputs a scalar estimate of the average treatment effect τ. A key constraint is that the estimator must work with only one potential outcome per observation, and the propensity scores are never known exactly but must be learned from the data.

## Method

The paper introduces Double-Double, a doubly robust estimator that uses split training (randomly partitioning data into S₁ and S₂ so each observation's prediction is independent of its own outcome) combined with a novel double-weighting loss: treatment outcomes are weighted by (1−pᵢ)/pᵢ² and control outcomes by pᵢ/(1−pᵢ)² when fitting the regression functions. This weighting is derived from an exact closed-form variance expression for any split-training doubly robust estimator, which the paper proves in Theorem 4.1. The estimator combines inverse propensity weighting with regression adjustment, and the double weighting corrects for the bias introduced by the fact that whether an observation appears in the training set depends on its propensity score. The paper also builds a comprehensive benchmark of 20+ estimators (including CATENet architectures, TMLE, Horvitz-Thompson, and others) evaluated across seven datasets under varying sample sizes, distance correlations, and propensity score accuracies, finding that doubly robust estimators outperform more complex methods by orders of magnitude.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic RORCO dataset (the primary benchmark), the standard Doubly Robust estimator achieves a mean squared error of 9.98e-07 over 100 runs, while the proposed Double-Double estimator achieves 1.07e-05. These are 1–2 orders of magnitude better than the next-best methods (TARNet at 1.92e-04, TNet at 8.05e-04, RANet at 7.85e-04). Naive baselines perform dramatically worse: Direct Difference has a mean squared error of 4.48e-01, Horvitz-Thompson 1.06e-02, and TMLE 1.19e-01. The doubly robust estimators also maintain their advantage as sample size, outcome-propensity correlation, and propensity score accuracy vary, though Double-Double is more sensitive to propensity score inaccuracy than the standard Doubly Robust estimator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 22 treatment effect estimators on the RORCO semi-synthetic dataset. The standard Doubly Robust estimator (9.98e-07 MSE) outperforms all other methods by at least 1.5 orders of magnitude, with the proposed Double-Double (1.07e-05) a close second. The next tier consists of CATENet neural network estimators—TARNet (1.92e-04), RANet (7.85e-04), TNet (8.05e-04), XNet (1.00e-03), and FlexTENet (1.15e-03)—which are 1–2 orders of magnitude worse than the doubly robust methods but require 10–25× more compute time. The Off-policy estimator of Mou et al. (3.17e-03), the closest prior work to Double-Double, is roughly 300× worse than Doubly Robust. Simple non-learning baselines (Direct Difference at 4.48e-01, Horvitz-Thompson at 1.06e-02) and complex methods like TMLE (1.19e-01) and PWNet (2.28e-01) perform the worst. On the observational RORCO Real dataset, estimates vary wildly across methods (from −0.607 to +0.459), underscoring the difficulty of the natural experiment setting and the value of the synthetic benchmark for evaluating estimator accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the doubly robust estimation literature (Scharfstein et al. 1999, Kang & Schafer 2007, Vermeulen & Vansteelandt 2015, Kennedy 2023) and is most closely related to the off-policy estimator of Mou et al. (2022), differing in that it learns separate treatment and control outcome functions and uses a theoretically motivated double-weighting loss. It departs from prior theoretical work by deriving an exact finite-variance expression for split-training doubly robust estimators, whereas all prior results provide only upper bounds or asymptotic characterizations. On the empirical side, it extends the CATENet benchmark (Curth & Van der Schaar 2021) and standard treatment effect datasets (Jobs, Twins, IHDP, ACIC) by introducing a domain-expert-guided semi-synthetic dataset (RORCO) specifically designed to capture the correlation structure of natural experiments, where treatments are targeted to those most likely to benefit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple regression-adjusted doubly robust estimator outperforms sophisticated neural network architectures (CATENet family) by 2–3 orders of magnitude across multiple datasets is practically significant for the causal inference community, suggesting that investment in complex architectures may be misdirected when the estimator formula itself is the key lever. The exact variance characterization provides a principled foundation for designing future estimators, and the RORCO dataset fills a gap in natural experiment benchmarks with realistic correlation structures. Key limitations include the assumption of known propensity scores in the theory, the data loss from split training (which is why the non-split DR estimator actually wins empirically), and the fact that synthetic outcomes, while expert-guided, may not capture all real-world complexity. Open questions include a full finite-sample analysis of non-splitting doubly robust estimators and understanding robustness to propensity score estimation error.
