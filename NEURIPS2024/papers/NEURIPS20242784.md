## Problem

Current Newton-based algorithms for training Cox proportional hazards (CPH) models suffer from vanishing second-order derivatives outside the local minimizer region, causing loss explosion or extremely slow convergence—especially in high-dimensional settings with correlated features. This prevents practitioners from obtaining the high-precision solutions needed for correct variable selection and from iterating quickly during feature engineering workflows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242784/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard right-censored survival data: a feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×p}, observation times t_i, and binary event indicators δ_i. It produces the CPH coefficient vector β ∈ R^p, and when coupled with the beam search framework, outputs cardinality-constrained sparse models where only k features are retained. A key practical detail is that the per-coordinate derivative evaluations cost O(n) rather than O(n²), making each coordinate update as cheap as a first-order method while retaining second-order convergence information.

## Method

The paper discovers that the CPH loss's first, second, and third order partial derivatives with respect to each coordinate l can be computed exactly in O(n) time by exploiting a reverse cumulative summation trick, bypassing the O(n²) Hessian in sample space. These derivatives have a probabilistic interpretation as (weighted) central moments under a softmax-like distribution over the risk set R_i, which yields explicit Lipschitz constants via Popoviciu's inequality (for the second derivative) and a variance-skewness bound (for the third derivative). Using these constants, the authors construct quadratic and cubic surrogate upper bounds on the loss whose minimizers admit closed-form solutions, producing coordinate descent algorithms with guaranteed monotonic loss decrease and global convergence—no backtracking required. For variable selection, they embed this coordinate descent into a beam search framework that expands the support by selecting the feature whose optimization yields the largest loss decrease, enabling cardinality-constrained CPH models that are much sparser than LASSO-based baselines while maintaining predictive accuracy.

## Results

On the Flchain dataset, the proposed quadratic and cubic surrogate methods are the only approaches with monotonically decreasing loss curves, while all Newton-type baselines (exact, quasi, proximal) exhibit loss blow-up or initial increases. On synthetic datasets with high correlation (ρ=0.9) and 1200 samples, FastSurvival achieves 100% support recovery (recovering all 15 true features with a model of size 15), while all baselines fall short. On the Dialysis dataset, the method obtains solutions with the smallest number of coefficients without losing test-set predictive performance (CIndex and IBS), whereas other model classes overfit on training sets. In wall-clock time, the proposed methods are significantly faster than quasi Newton and proximal Newton due to O(n) per-coordinate derivative evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three Newton-type optimization baselines (exact Newton [Goeman, 2008], quasi Newton [Simon, 2011], proximal Newton [Moufad, 2023]), FastSurvival wins decisively on both convergence stability and speed: Newton-type methods suffer loss explosion or initial loss increases, while the surrogate-based methods guarantee monotonic decrease and converge faster in wall-clock time. In variable selection on synthetic data (ρ=0.9), FastSurvival achieves 100% F1 at the true support size of 15, substantially outperforming Coxnet (quasi Newton coordinate descent [Simon, 2011]), Abess [Zhu, 2022], and Adaptive Lasso [Zhang, 2007], all of which show degraded F1 scores. On the Employee Attrition dataset, FastSurvival dominates all three Cox-based baselines (Coxnet, Abess, Adaptive Lasso) on both CIndex and IBS across the full sparsity range. On the Dialysis dataset, FastSurvival outperforms non-Cox model classes (trees, random forests, neural networks) by achieving the best accuracy-sparsity tradeoff, with other methods overfitting on training data. The key differentiator is that the surrogate-based coordinate descent is the only method compatible with the beam search framework for cardinality-constrained selection, since Newton-type methods cannot guarantee loss decrease at each step.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the Newton-type optimization lineage for CPH models (Simon et al. 2011, Goeman 2008, Moufad et al. 2023), which all approximate the Hessian in sample space at O(n²) cost. Instead, it borrows the quadratic/cubic surrogate function framework from Nesterov & Polyak (2006) and applies it in feature space, where a key discovery—that first and second-order partial derivatives are computable in O(n) via reverse cumulative summation—makes the surrogate approach practical. For variable selection, it combines the beam search paradigm from Wiseman & Rush (2016) with the support-expansion strategy of generalized orthogonal matching pursuit (Elenberg et al. 2018), but requires the new coordinate-descent solver to function in highly correlated settings where prior beam-search CPH attempts would fail.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(n) partial derivative computation removes the primary computational bottleneck in CPH training, enabling high-precision convergence without the loss explosions that plague Newton-type methods. This directly unlocks cardinality-constrained CPH (sparse, interpretable models) in regimes with highly correlated features—a practically important but previously infeasible setting for healthcare and business applications. Open questions include whether higher-order partial derivatives are Lipschitz-continuous, how to extend the framework to time-varying covariates, stratified models, and feature interactions, and whether the central-moment connection generalizes beyond order three. A key limitation is that the CPH proportional-hazards assumption itself may not hold for all data, which the paper acknowledges but does not address.
