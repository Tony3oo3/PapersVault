## Problem

Existing optimizations of Mamba for visual tasks rely on predefined architectural modifications (bidirectional scanning, cross-scanning, etc.) that require strong priors and extensive trial-and-error, while recent work has questioned whether such improvements are even necessary. There is no systematic, architecture-agnostic method to understand how Mamba's internal computational modules actually drive predictions, identify where they go wrong on difficult samples, and repair those failures post-hoc.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241644/cropped_page14_idx11.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on 224×224 RGB images from ImageNet subsets (50, 300, or 1K classes) and additionally consumes per-class foreground segmentation masks from ImageNet-S (averaging ~10 annotated samples per class) to score and constrain external state correlations. Beyond the standard class label output, the framework produces two diagnostic artifacts: an external state correlation map of shape H×W (spatially aligned to the input image, indicating which image regions each module's states correlate with the prediction) and an internal state correlation vector of shape D (indicating which hidden-state dimensions drive the prediction for a given sample).

## Method

Vision Mamba Mender introduces two gradient-based correlation analysis tools tailored to Mamba's block structure: Grad-ESC computes external state correlation by backpropagating the predicted-class gradient through any module's output states, averaging across the feature dimension, and reshaping to image resolution; Grad-ISC computes internal state correlation by element-wise multiplying the same gradient with the state vector to retain per-dimension importance. Each correlation is scored quantitatively (ECS via perturbation test × foreground IoU; ICS via simplicity × cross-sample XOR consistency), and a drop in scores on difficult versus simple samples flags a flaw. Repair is then applied as an auxiliary loss with second-order gradients: external repair constrains the Conv and SSM outputs (c_n, s_n) in deeper blocks to correlate with foreground masks, while internal repair constrains the linear-mapping output (x_n) in deeper blocks to match class-specific correlation templates built from simple training samples. Both repair losses are added to the standard cross-entropy objective with balancing coefficients, introducing no extra parameters, and can be applied sequentially (external first, then internal) to any Mamba-based vision architecture.

## Results

On ImageNet-50, applying both external and internal flaw repair (+All) yields the largest gains: SiMBA-S improves from 81.32% to 86.52% (+5.20%) and LocalVim-T from 75.08% to 80.56% (+5.48%). On ImageNet-300, ViM-T benefits most, rising from 75.11% to 79.53% (+4.42%), while on ImageNet-1K the improvements are more modest, ranging from +0.49% (EMamba-T) to +3.13% (LocalVim-T). External repair alone and internal repair alone each contribute independently, and the two are orthogonal, with combined repair consistently outperforming either in isolation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is applied additively on top of five SOTA Vision Mamba architectures (ViM-T, VMamba-T, SiMBA-S, EMamba-T, LocalVim-T) and consistently improves all of them across all three dataset scales. The largest absolute gains appear on ImageNet-50, where SiMBA-S jumps by +5.20% and LocalVim-T by +5.48% with combined repair, while the smallest gains are on ImageNet-1K (e.g., EMamba-T only +0.49%). External repair tends to help more for models with stronger spatial scanning (SiMBA-S, LocalVim-T), whereas internal repair is more effective for VMamba-T (+2.40% on ImageNet-50) and EMamba-T (+1.80%). The two repair types are orthogonal: on ImageNet-50, ViM-T gains +2.04% from external and +1.76% from internal repair individually, but +3.24% when both are applied, confirming complementary effects. No baseline is degraded by the method under any configuration tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of improving Vision Mamba models through pre-defined architectural modifications (bidirectional scanning in ViM, cross-scanning in VMamba, continuous 2D scanning in PlainMamba) by proposing a post-hoc analysis-and-repair framework. It builds directly on Mamba's selective SSM structure and draws methodological inspiration from Grad-CAM, extending gradient-based attribution to define external and internal state correlation scores specific to Mamba's computational modules. The work also positions itself against Yu et al.'s [23] argument that Mamba architectural improvements are unnecessary for visual recognition, instead showing that targeted, data-driven repair of identified state-level flaws yields consistent gains across five SOTA Vision Mamba architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces the first systematic post-hoc optimization framework for Mamba models, offering an architecture-agnostic alternative to trial-and-error architectural design that could be applied as Mamba variants continue to proliferate. However, the approach requires foreground segmentation annotations (from ImageNet-S) for the external repair loss, limiting its applicability to settings where such supervision is available. The authors acknowledge that they only analyze module-level interactions without delving into the internal mechanics of individual components (e.g., within the SSM itself), and the modest gains (1–5% depending on dataset scale) suggest the identified 'flaws' may represent only a portion of the model's error budget. Open questions include whether the correlation-based repair transfers to non-visual tasks or non-Mamba architectures, and whether deeper module-level analysis would yield larger improvements.
