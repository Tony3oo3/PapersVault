## Problem

The dominant hypothesis in the ICL literature is that Transformers internally run Gradient Descent to solve regression tasks from in-context examples. This paper challenges that hypothesis by showing Transformers actually learn second-order optimization methods—specifically Iterative Newton's Method (Newton-Schulz)—whose convergence rate is exponentially faster than GD, and which can handle ill-conditioned problems where GD fails.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243131/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a causal sequence of t in-context (x_i, y_i) pairs (x_i ∈ R^d, y_i ∈ R) followed by a query point x_{t+1}, and outputs a scalar regression prediction ŷ_{t+1} via a linear readout on the final hidden state at the query position. A key experimental detail is that the authors probe intermediate layers by re-training the readout head at each layer index ℓ while freezing all other parameters, yielding L distinct predictions per input that can be directly compared to k iterations of an iterative algorithm. Data is drawn from a distribution P_D parameterized by a covariance matrix Σ, with most experiments using Σ = I and a separate set using ill-conditioned Σ with κ(Σ) = 100.

## Method

The paper combines empirical probing with a theoretical construction. Empirically, GPT-2 Transformers (12 layers, 8 heads, d=20, n=40) are trained on in-context linear regression, and two similarity metrics—cosine similarity of residual vectors and cosine similarity of induced weight vectors—are used to match each Transformer layer ℓ to the best-matching number of iterations k of candidate algorithms (GD, Iterative Newton, OGD, BFGS, L-BFGS, CG). The key finding is a linear trend between Transformer layers 3–9 and Newton iterations (each layer ≈ 3 Newton steps) but an exponential trend with GD steps, indicating O(log log(1/ε)) convergence. Theoretically, Theorem 5.1 proves that k iterations of Newton's method (M_{k+1} = 2M_k − M_k S M_k) can be implemented with exactly k+8 Transformer layers and O(d) hidden dimension: 3 layers initialize M_0 = αS, one layer per Newton iteration, and 5 layers read out the prediction x_test^T M_k X^T y. This is more efficient than prior work (Giannou et al.) which required 13 looped layers per Newton iteration.

## Results

Transformers trained for in-context linear regression (d=20, n=40) exhibit superlinear convergence across layers 3–9, matching Iterative Newton's Method at a rate of O(log log(1/ε)), while Gradient Descent converges sublinearly at O(κ log(1/ε)). Each middle Transformer layer computes roughly 3 Newton iterations, and to match a Transformer's prediction at layer k, GD would require exponentially many steps. On ill-conditioned data (κ(Σ)=100), GD requires ~2,000 steps to converge while Transformers and Newton converge within 12 layers. Theoretically, k Newton iterations are implementable with k+8 Transformer layers and O(d) hidden dimension (Theorem 5.1).

|---|---|---|---|
| Transformers | **0.991** | 0.957 | 0.806 |
| LSTM | 0.920 | 0.916 | **0.954** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Iterative Newton's Method, Transformers show a linear correspondence in best-matching steps (each layer ≈ 3 Newton iterations) with error similarity of 0.991, confirming they share the same O(log log(1/ε)) convergence rate. Against Gradient Descent, the best-matching steps follow an exponential trend (GD steps grow exponentially with Transformer layer index), and GD requires ~2,000 steps on ill-conditioned data (κ=100) versus 12 Transformer layers. Against BFGS (a quasi-Newton method), Transformers also show a linear correspondence (layers 6–11), consistent with BFGS's superlinear convergence. Against L-BFGS and Conjugate Gradient, Transformers converge substantially faster, as these methods use only partial second-order information. Against LSTM, Transformers clearly outperform: LSTMs do not improve across layers (a readout on layer 1 matches the full 10-layer model), and LSTMs are more similar to OGD (0.954) than to Newton (0.920), indicating they learn an online rather than batch optimization strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly challenges the dominant hypothesis in the in-context learning literature that Transformers internally implement gradient descent (von Oswald et al. 2022, 2023; Ahn et al. 2023; Dai et al. 2023). It builds on Garg et al. (2022)'s in-context linear regression setup and Akyürek et al. (2022)'s analysis of Transformer-computable operations, but argues the learned algorithm is a second-order method (Iterative Newton / Newton-Schulz) rather than a first-order one. The theoretical construction (one layer per Newton iteration) improves on Giannou et al. (2023), who required 13 looped layers per iteration, and the LSTM comparison extends the analysis beyond the Transformer architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If Transformers genuinely implement second-order optimization in-context, this reframes the mechanistic interpretability of ICL and explains why Transformers outperform LSTMs (which behave more like online GD) on tasks requiring global data access. The ill-conditioned data experiments are particularly compelling: Transformers match Newton's mild logarithmic dependence on condition number while GD degrades polynomially, a gap no first-order method can close. Open questions include whether the specific second-order method is always Newton-Schulz or varies with the task, how this extends to classification and non-linear function classes, and what architectural features (attention vs. recurrence) enable second-order computation.
