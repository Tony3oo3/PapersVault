## Problem

Existing multi-agent decision-making environments use fixed task sets and static interaction topologies, failing to capture how social connections dynamically reshape agents' rewards, information access, and available actions. While single-agent adaptive environments (Crafter, MineDojo) generate new tasks from agent behavior, no prior environment simultaneously expands physical state/action spaces and models adaptive, agent-controlled social structures, leaving the interplay between physical exploration and social relationship management unexplored.

## Input / Output

Each agent observes a partially visible grid window (o cells in four cardinal directions), its own inventory counts, and the full social state of all agents represented as a multi-layer directed graph. Actions are split into physical (movement, resource pick/dump, synthesis on event grids, communication) and social (directed connect/disconnect with agents or group nodes); the action space grows as new resources are synthesized. Rewards are resource-dependent and agent-specific via R_i(ρ) = m_i^ρ · h_i(ρ) · r̄^ρ, and the environment's transition and reward functions themselves evolve as the synthesis tree is unlocked, making the MDP non-stationary within a single episode.

## Method

The paper introduces the Growing-MG formalism: a base Markov Game augmented by a Monotonic-MG-bundle map β that, upon certain transitions, monotonically expands the state and action spaces (S_t ⊆ S_{t+1} ⊆ S_w) and updates the transition/reward functions, yielding a non-stationary multi-agent framework. Social structure is encoded as a multi-layer directed graph where layer 0 holds individual agents, layer 1 holds groups, and higher layers capture hierarchies; intra-layer edges encode reward/information sharing while inter-layer edges encode subordinate membership. Three mini-games of increasing social complexity (Social Structure with pre-defined changing topologies, Contract where agents pick group partners, and Negotiation where agents bargain over reward splits) instantiate the framework. For baselines, RL agents (PPO, RecPPO, MAPPO, Rainbow) use a CNN for grid encoding and a GCN for social-state encoding, while a proposed Curriculum Learning algorithm first trains under shared rewards, then gradually randomizes social structure, and finally frees social actions. An LLM-C agent (GPT-4 + rule-based controller) converts observations into natural-language prompts for high-level planning, with a monitoring function detecting and regenerating infeasible plans.

## Results

In the Contract mini-game, Curriculum Learning (CL) achieves a normalized reward of 0.9136 on the Easy task and 0.2773 on the Hard task, substantially outperforming standard RL baselines (PPO: 0.2286/0.1151; Rainbow: 0.1987/0.0868). In Negotiation, CL reaches 0.3543 (Easy) and 0.1945 (Hard), again leading all RL methods. The LLM-based controller (LLM-C) achieves 0.8433 on Contract-Easy and 0.8733 on Negotiation-Easy, approaching or surpassing CL, but underperforms CL on Contract-Easy (0.8433 vs. 0.9136) and shows higher variance across runs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CL (built on RecPPO with a three-stage curriculum) decisively outperforms PPO on all four task variants: by ~4x on Contract-Easy (0.9136 vs. 0.2286) and by ~2.4x on Contract-Hard (0.2773 vs. 0.1151). Against Rainbow, the gap is even wider, with CL achieving roughly 4.6x the reward on Contract-Easy (0.9136 vs. 0.1987) and 3.2x on Contract-Hard (0.2773 vs. 0.0868). In Negotiation, CL leads PPO by ~55% on Easy (0.3543 vs. 0.2276) and ~78% on Hard (0.1945 vs. 0.1093), and leads Rainbow by ~80% on Easy and ~115% on Hard. The LLM-C framework (GPT-4 + rule-based controller) achieves 0.8433 on Contract-Easy, approaching CL's 0.9136 but falling short, and reaches 0.8733 on Negotiation-Easy, exceeding CL's 0.3543 by a wide margin, suggesting LLMs leverage social reasoning more effectively in negotiation settings. Overall, all RL methods struggle significantly with the social-structure aspects of AdaSociety, with standard PPO and Rainbow stuck at low-reward local equilibria, confirming the environment's difficulty for current algorithms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Markov Game framework (Littman, 1994) by introducing the Growing-MG formulation, which allows state and action spaces to expand dynamically, and further enriches it with a multi-layer directed graph to model adaptive social structures. It draws on single-agent adaptive environments (Crafter, MineDojo, Conan) that generate new tasks through agent exploration, and on multi-agent environments (Melting Pot, SMAC, Diplomacy, Neural MMO) that test coordination and competition, but uniquely combines both adaptive physical expansion and adaptive social connections in a single platform. The multi-layer graph representation for social states is inspired by complex network theory and multilayer network research from social sciences, applied here to explicitly and quantitatively model hierarchical agent-organization relationships.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AdaSociety is the first environment to jointly feature expanding physical state/action spaces and adaptive, agent-controlled social structures, opening research directions in coalition formation, hierarchy emergence, and social intelligence that were previously intractable in fixed-topology multi-agent settings. Preliminary results reveal that both RL and LLM-based methods struggle to effectively leverage social structures for performance gains, suggesting a significant gap in current decision-making algorithms for socially structured environments. Key open questions include how to design algorithms that balance physical exploration with social connection management, whether the Growing-MG formulation admits useful equilibrium or convergence guarantees, and how the environment scales with survival pressures and human-in-the-loop interaction.
