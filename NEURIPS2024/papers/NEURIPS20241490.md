## Problem

Three fundamental classification questions in kernel methods and algorithm design remained open: (1) whether low-degree polynomials are the only functions that preserve low rank when applied entrywise to low-rank matrices (critical for fast attention in LLMs), (2) whether completely monotone functions are the only positive definite kernels of Manhattan distance, and (3) whether Bernstein functions are the only functions transforming Manhattan distances to Manhattan distances. Prior work by Guillot, Khare, and Rajaratnam left gaps for non-smooth functions, and the Manhattan kernel and metric transform classifications had been partially known since 1980 without full characterization.

## Input / Output

The method takes an arbitrary function f: R → R (with minimal regularity assumptions, e.g., piecewise continuity) and a family of kernel matrices D constructed from Manhattan distances between vertices of d-dimensional hyperrectangles (size 2^d × 2^d, rank d+1). The output is a classification theorem: f must be a polynomial of degree ≤ ⌈log₂(n)⌉ to preserve low rank, completely monotone to be a positive definite Manhattan kernel, or Bernstein to be a Manhattan-to-Manhattan metric transform. The key structural constraint is that the hyperrectangle side lengths a ∈ R^d_{>0} are free parameters, allowing the eigenvalues of D^f to encode arbitrary finite differences of f at any point.

## Method

The core tool is the 'representation theory of the hyperrectangle': the Manhattan distance matrix between vertices of a d-dimensional hyperrectangle has the columns of the Walsh-Hadamard matrix H_d as its eigenvectors (via Schur's lemma for the reflectional symmetry group), and its eigenvalues under entrywise application of f reduce to signed sums of f evaluated at linear combinations of the side lengths. This converts algebraic rank/positivity conditions on D^f into sign constraints on finite differences of f, which then characterize polynomials, completely monotone functions, and Bernstein functions respectively. The Baire Category Theorem is applied to the zero-sets of individual eigenvalues to handle the piecewise-continuous case and upgrade 'piecewise polynomial' to 'polynomial.' The same hyperrectangle eigenvalue machinery simultaneously yields new proofs of the classical Schoenberg classification of Euclidean kernels and metric transforms, unifying all three main results under a single linear-algebraic framework.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical classifications. First, Theorem 2.5 proves that low-degree polynomials of degree at most ⌈log₂(n)⌉ are the *only* piecewise continuous functions that preserve low-rank n×n matrices, giving a converse to the polynomial method (with a small constant-factor gap: Fact 2.1 shows degree ~½log₂(n) suffices, while Theorem 2.5 shows degree < log₂(n) is necessary). Second, Theorem 3.4 gives the first full classification of positive definite Manhattan kernels: f is such a kernel if and only if f is completely monotone. Third, Theorem 4.4 proves that Bernstein functions are exactly the functions that transform Manhattan distances to Manhattan distances (and equivalently to squared Euclidean distances), completing partial results from Schoenberg (1938) and Assouad (1980).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental baselines. The key comparisons are against prior partial results that the paper completes. Theorem 2.5 extends Guillot, Khare, and Rajaratnam (GKR17, Theorem B), which required f to be (n−1)-times differentiable or M^f to be positive semi-definite; the new result removes both restrictions, covering all piecewise continuous functions including non-smooth activations like ReLU and ELU. Theorem 3.4 completes the Manhattan kernel classification: prior work (Schoenberg 1938, Assouad 1980) showed completely monotone functions are Manhattan kernels, but the converse (that they are the *only* ones) was open. Theorem 4.4 completes Assouad's (1980) partial categorization of Manhattan-to-Manhattan metric transforms and Schoenberg's (1938) partial categorization of Manhattan-to-Euclidean transforms, proving that Bernstein functions are the unique class in both settings. The paper also provides a new proof of the classical Schoenberg classification of Euclidean kernels, replacing traditional infinite-dimensional harmonic analysis arguments with the hyperrectangle representation technique.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the polynomial method (a folklore result in algorithm design) by proving its converse: low-degree polynomials are the only piecewise continuous functions that preserve low-rank matrices under entry-wise application. It extends the partial negative result of Guillot, Khare, and Rajaratnam [GKR17], which required (n-1)-times differentiability, to all piecewise continuous functions. For kernel methods and metric transforms, the paper generalizes Schoenberg's foundational Euclidean classifications to the Manhattan (ℓ₁) setting, completing partial categorizations by Assouad [Ass80] that had remained open since 1980. The core technical tool—a new 'representation theory of the hyperrectangle' leveraging Schur's lemma for Abelian groups and Walsh-Hadamard eigenvectors—is entirely new and unifies all three applications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The negative result that only polynomial-approximable functions can yield low-rank fast attention closes a major open question in the LLM/transformer community, redirecting research toward polynomial approximations of softmax rather than seeking fundamentally new kernel functions. The stable rank result (Theorem 2.8) identifies the first strict generalization of the polynomial method, showing non-polynomial functions like x^c can preserve stable rank, though the authors note it is unclear how to exploit this for subquadratic algorithms. Open questions include closing the constant-factor gap between the sufficient and necessary polynomial degrees, and finding practical algorithmic applications of the stable rank preservation. The Manhattan kernel and metric transform completions provide a definitive reference for kernel method practitioners working with ℓ₁ distances.
