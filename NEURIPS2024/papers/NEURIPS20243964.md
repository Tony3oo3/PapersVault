## Problem

While prior work characterized GNN expressiveness via non-uniform graph distinguishability (1-WL) or convergence to continuous limit objects, no one had established how real-valued GNN classifier outputs behave as graph size grows under random graph models. This paper asks: do GNN outputs on larger random graphs eventually become independent of the specific input graph, and if so, what does this imply for what tasks GNNs can uniformly express?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243964/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes featured graphs (n nodes with d-dimensional i.i.d. bounded feature vectors) drawn from random graph models—Erdős-Rényi at various sparsity levels, Barabási-Albert preferential attachment, or stochastic block models—and produces a real-valued class probability vector via a softmax/sigmoid head over pooled node embeddings. The key structural property is that the output is shown to converge asymptotically almost surely to a fixed constant vector, independent of the particular graph drawn, so the 'input' is effectively a distribution over graph sizes rather than a single graph.

## Method

The paper introduces an aggregate term language AGG[WMEAN] built from node features, constants, Lipschitz function symbols, and local/global weighted-mean aggregators, then proves an 'almost sure convergence' theorem: every closed term converges a.a.s. to a constant on the target random graph models. The proof proceeds by induction on term depth, showing each open term degenerates to a Lipschitz function of node features and graph type (non-sparse cases, via concentration inequalities and a regularity lemma) or of features and local neighbourhood isomorphism type (sparse cases, via weak local convergence). This term language subsumes MeanGNNs, GATs, GPS+RW graph transformers, and GCNs, so the convergence result transfers directly to these architectures. The paper also shows the result is tight: adding sum aggregation or non-smooth functions breaks convergence, and even within the language, certain ER growth rates (e.g., alternating p(n)) prevent convergence.

## Results

The paper empirically validates its theoretical convergence theorems by training five randomly-initialized models (MeanGNN, GAT, GPS+RW) with 128 hidden dimensions, 3 layers, and 5 output classes on random graphs of sizes up to 10,000 (100 samples per size). Across all architectures and graph distributions (dense ER, log-growth ER, sparse ER, BA, SBM), class probabilities converge to constant values as graph size increases, with standard deviations shrinking toward zero. Attention-based models (GAT, GPS+RW) exhibit noticeably delayed convergence and greater standard deviation compared to MeanGNN, attributed to higher variance in attention-weighted aggregation. On the TIGER-Alaska real-world dataset (graphs of 1K–90K nodes), the same convergence phenomenon is observed but at a slower pace.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical convergence paper rather than a benchmark comparison, so there are no traditional baselines. The empirical section compares three architecture families—MeanGNN, GAT, and GPS+RW—under identical hyperparameters (128 hidden dim, 3 layers, 5-class softmax, ReLU) across five random graph models. MeanGNN converges fastest with the lowest variance, while GAT and GPS+RW show delayed convergence and higher standard deviation, consistent with the intuition that attention weighting introduces additional variance. A notable distinction is that the converged constant values are identical for dense ER (p=0.1) and logarithmic-growth ER (p=log n/n) but differ for sparse ER (p=1/50n), which the authors relate to the different proof strategies (graph-type controllers vs. local neighbourhood isomorphism types). The TIGER-Alaska experiment (planar geographic graphs, 1K–90K nodes) confirms that convergence is not an artifact of random graph structure, though it proceeds more slowly than on synthetic models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the zero-one law results of Adam-Day et al. (NeurIPS 2023) from binary GNN classifiers to real-valued classifiers, and from a single random graph model to a broad family including sparse/dense Erdős-Rényi, Barabási-Albert, and stochastic block models. Rather than proving convergence per architecture, it introduces a unified aggregate term language AGG[WMEAN] that subsumes MeanGNNs, GATs, GPS graph transformers, and GCNs, then proves a single 'almost sure convergence to a constant' theorem for the entire language. The approach draws on convergence laws for logical languages (Fagin, Lynch) but is the first to handle aggregation operators, and it departs fundamentally from prior GNN convergence work (Keriven, Levie, Maskey) that studies convergence to continuous analogues in function spaces rather than a.a.s. constancy in Euclidean space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides a sharp impossibility theorem: any GNN expressible in the term language (which includes all standard message-passing and graph transformer architectures with mean/attention aggregation) can only express asymptotically constant classifiers on the covered random graph models, fundamentally bounding their uniform expressiveness. The i.i.d. node feature assumption is a key limitation, and the paper explicitly notes that adding sum aggregation or non-smooth functions breaks the convergence, leaving open whether more expressive architectures can escape this bound. The empirical observation that convergence also appears on real-world planar graphs (TIGER-Alaska) suggests the phenomenon may be more general than the theory currently covers, pointing to an open question about what structural properties of real graphs drive this behavior.
