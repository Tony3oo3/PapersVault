## Problem

Despite the widespread use of multi-task learning (MTL) and pretraining+finetuning (PT+FT), the inductive biases these training paradigms impose on learned solutions remain poorly characterized. While single-task implicit regularization (e.g., ℓ₁ vs. ℓ₂ biases in diagonal linear networks) is well understood, it is unclear how the presence of an auxiliary task reshapes the regularization landscape, whether MTL and PT+FT induce qualitatively different biases, and under what conditions finetuning preferentially reuses a sparse subset of pretrained features.

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on diagonal linear networks (D-dimensional input, O∈{1,2} outputs) and single-hidden-layer ReLU networks (H hidden units, D-dimensional input), taking as input the pretrained weight vector (or feature directions and magnitudes) and the main/auxiliary task data matrices. The empirical validation uses 32×32 RGB images from CIFAR-100 (binary classification of 2 randomly sampled classes as the main task, the remaining 98 as auxiliary) and ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-18/VGG11/ViT models finetuned on CIFAR-100 or STL-10. The key unusual input manipulation is a scalar weight-rescaling factor applied to all hidden-layer weights between pretraining and finetuning, which shifts the network into different feature-reuse regimes.

## Method

The paper derives closed-form regularization penalties for MTL and PT+FT: MTL induces a group-lasso (ℓ_{1,2}) norm over per-feature task coefficients, while PT+FT in diagonal linear networks induces a novel Q-norm depending on the ratio of main-task to auxiliary-task coefficients, and PT+FT in ReLU networks induces a penalty that depends on both feature magnitudes and the cosine similarity between pre- and post-finetuning feature directions. A key theoretical result is a 'conservation law' (ℓ-order + feature-dependence = 1) that creates an exact tradeoff between sparsity bias and feature-reuse bias, predicting a 'nested feature selection' regime where PT+FT (but not MTL) preferentially extracts a sparse subset of pretrained features. The authors validate this with teacher-student experiments showing that rescaling pretrained weights by a factor < 1 before finetuning elicits the nested regime, and confirm the prediction in deep networks: ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100 improves with weight rescaling and exhibits decreased participation ratio (PR) and high effective number of shared dimensions (ENSD) post-finetuning, while ViT already operates in the nested regime without rescaling and does not benefit from it.

## Results

In CIFAR-100 experiments with ResNet-18, both MTL and PT+FT improve sample efficiency over single-task learning (STL), and weight rescaling by factors less than 1 following pretraining further improves finetuning accuracy (Fig. 5b). In ImageNet-pretrained networks finetuned on CIFAR-100 and STL-10, rescaling by a factor of 0.5 consistently improves performance across ResNet-18 and VGG-11 architectures (Fig. 10). Representational analysis (participation ratio and effective number of shared dimensions) confirms that rescaled ResNets enter a nested feature selection regime where post-finetuning dimensionality drops below pretraining dimensionality and nearly all finetuned dimensions are inherited from pretraining, while ViTs already exhibit this regime at rescaling factor 1.0 and degrade when rescaled further.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MTL, PT+FT, and STL (single-task learning) across teacher-student and deep network settings. In the fully-overlapping-features regime, both MTL and PT+FT outperform STL, with PT+FT (Linear Probing) matching PT+FT performance, confirming a weighted ℓ₂ bias toward pretrained features. In the nested feature selection regime (main task features are a subset of auxiliary task features), PT+FT with weight rescaling significantly outperforms both STL and MTL, while MTL shows only a narrow regime of benefit. For correlated (but not identical) features in ReLU networks, PT+FT outperforms STL whereas MTL does not, demonstrating that PT+FT can 'softly' share features while MTL cannot. In deep networks, weight rescaling improves ResNet-18 finetuning but degrades ViT performance, because ViTs already operate in the nested feature selection regime at the default initialization scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-task implicit regularization framework of Woodworth et al. (lazy vs. rich regimes in diagonal linear networks) and Azulay et al. (initialization shape effects) to the multi-task setting, deriving explicit regularization penalties for both MTL (group lasso / ℓ₁,₂ norm) and PT+FT (a novel Q-norm for diagonal linear networks, and a correlation-dependent penalty for ReLU networks). It builds on Shenouda et al.'s characterization of multi-output ReLU network parameter norms to connect the MTL penalty to the group lasso. The key conceptual departure is identifying a 'nested feature selection' regime intermediate between the previously characterized lazy and rich regimes, governed by a conservation law (ℓ-order + feature dependence = 1) that creates a fundamental tradeoff between sparsity and feature reuse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The nested feature selection regime provides a theoretical explanation for why pretraining+finetuning can outperform both single-task learning and linear probing, and why the benefit depends on the relative scale of pretrained vs. task-specific features. The practical weight rescaling technique is trivially implementable and shows consistent improvements across ResNet-18 on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet-pretrained models, suggesting a broadly applicable finetuning strategy. Open questions include extending the theory from explicit to implicit regularization in ReLU networks, handling cross-entropy loss, and understanding why different architectures (ResNet vs. ViT) require different rescaling values to enter the nested regime. The theory-practice gap remains: all theoretical results are for shallow networks, and the deep network validation, while suggestive, uses limited architectures and binary classification tasks.
