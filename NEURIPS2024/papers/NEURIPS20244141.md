## Problem

Existing debiased recommendation methods fail when unobserved features (hidden confounders) simultaneously affect both user selection and feedback. Sensitivity-analysis approaches require strong, often violated assumptions on confounding strength, while RCT-based calibration methods depend on prohibitively expensive randomized data. This paper identifies that real-world recommendation data is naturally heterogeneous—some users have complete profiles (no hidden confounding) while others do not—and proposes to exploit this structure without either assumption.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244141/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes heterogeneous observational data where each user-item pair carries a binary data-source indicator g_{u,i} (1 = all confounders observed, 0 = hidden confounders present), alongside standard features x_{u,i}, observation indicator o_{u,i}, and observed ratings r_{u,i}. Missing feature values are zero-filled so both subgroups share the same input dimensionality. The output is a predicted rating for every user-item pair in the target population, produced via an oracle error imputation model that estimates E[e_{u,i} | x_{u,i}] across the full space, enabling unbiased training even where ratings are unobserved.

## Method

MetaDebias defines an identifiable propensity score π(x,g) and a naive error imputation m(x,g) that jointly model the two distinct missing mechanisms (with and without hidden confounding), then derives the oracle error imputation E[e_{u,i}|x_{u,i}] through a residual term η(x) that captures the bias gap between the confounded subpopulation and the target population (Lemma 1). A second identity (Lemma 2) provides a robust, doubly-robust-style training signal by incorporating the actual observation o_{u,i} and naive prediction error o_{u,i}·e_{u,i} with a zero-mean noise term. Training uses a bi-level optimization scheme: the propensity model is pretrained independently, then assumed updates of the naive and oracle imputation models enable gradient back-propagation to update the prediction model θ via the oracle-imputation loss, followed by joint updates of the naive imputation, oracle imputation, and residual models. Five neural modules (propensity, naive imputation, oracle imputation, residual, prediction) are trained end-to-end, with the key insight that the heterogeneous data structure itself—rather than RCT data or confounding-strength bounds—provides the identifiability needed to separate true preference from hidden-confounding bias.

## Results

On KUAIREC (the largest dataset, 4.7M interactions), MetaDebias achieves AUC 0.852, NDCG@50 0.584, and Recall@50 0.544, outperforming the strongest RCT-based baseline Res-DR (AUC 0.850, NDCG@50 0.580, Recall@50 0.538) and the sensitivity-analysis baseline BRD-DR (AUC 0.848, NDCG@50 0.578). On YAHOO! R3, MetaDebias reaches AUC 0.722 and NDCG@5 0.544, and on COAT it achieves AUC 0.718 and NDCG@5 0.467, all statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05) over the best baselines. Notably, MetaDebias attains these results without any RCT data, whereas RCT-based methods (Res-DR, Bal-DR) show no significant advantage over their RCT-free counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MetaDebias outperforms all 13 baselines across all three datasets and all three metrics. Among RCT data-free methods, BRD-DR (sensitivity analysis) is the strongest competitor, but MetaDebias still leads by 0.004 AUC on KUAIREC and 0.007 AUC on YAHOO! R3; BRD-DR's performance degrades sharply under stronger hidden confounding due to its reliance on bounded-confounding assumptions. Among RCT data-based methods, Res-DR (residual calibration) is the closest baseline, trailing MetaDebias by 0.002 AUC and 0.004 NDCG@50 on KUAIREC, yet it requires expensive RCT data that MetaDebias does not. Standard DR and its variants (Stable-DR, TDR, Multi-DR, ESCM²-DR) all underperform substantially (e.g., DR achieves only 0.830 AUC vs. 0.852 for MetaDebias on KUAIREC), confirming that ignoring hidden confounding leads to inevitable degradation. RCT-based model-selection methods (KD-Label, AutoDebias, LTD-IPS/DR) and calibration methods (Bal-IPS/DR) do not demonstrate significant improvements over their RCT-free counterparts, suggesting that effective calibration is difficult with MNAR data. MetaDebias also shows the most stable performance across varying hidden confounding strength, data proportions, and training set sizes (including as low as 10% data), where BRD-DR deteriorates most severely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the doubly robust (DR) debiasing framework (Wang et al., ICML 2019) to the hidden confounding setting by introducing a heterogeneous observational data setup where some samples have complete features (no hidden confounding) and others do not. It departs from two existing lines of work addressing hidden confounding: sensitivity analysis methods (BRD-DR, Ding et al., KDD 2022) that require strong boundedness assumptions on confounding strength, and RCT-calibration methods (Res-DR, Bal-DR, Li et al., NeurIPS 2023 / WWW 2023) that depend on expensive randomized trial data. The bi-level optimization with assumed updates is inherited from AutoDebias (Chen et al., SIGIR 2021) and Wang et al. (WSDM 2021), but is repurposed here to jointly train an oracle error imputation model and a residual bias model specific to the hidden-confounding subgroup.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The heterogeneous observational data setup is arguably more realistic than requiring RCT data or bounding hidden confounding strength, making the approach more deployable in industrial recommender systems where user profiles are naturally incomplete for some users. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is the assumption that all confounders can be captured through sufficient feature collection, which remains theoretically feasible but practically challenging. Open questions include how to relax this completeness assumption, how the method behaves when the g_{u,i} indicator is noisy or partially unknown, and whether the bi-level optimization overhead (noted in Table 5) becomes prohibitive at larger scale.
