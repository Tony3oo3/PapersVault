## Problem

Adam consistently outperforms SGD on language models by a larger margin than on vision tasks, but the underlying mechanism has remained unclear. This paper identifies heavy-tailed class imbalance—where rare words follow a Zipf-like power-law and collectively account for most of the data—as the key factor: SGD makes slow progress on low-frequency classes, and because those classes dominate the data, overall training loss decreases slowly, while Adam and sign-based methods are largely unaffected.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on text token sequences (WikiText-103, PTB), image-label pairs (MNIST, ImageNet), and synthetic uniform vectors, with data split into frequency-ranked groups (e.g., deciles) for per-group loss tracking. The primary outputs are training loss curves decomposed by class-frequency group, per-row gradient-norm vs. Hessian-trace scatter plots revealing the emerging correlation, and theoretical convergence rate bounds comparing gradient flow (Θ(1/π_k t)) to sign descent (Θ(e^{-ct})) for each class k.

## Method

The paper establishes that heavy-tailed class imbalance causes SGD to be slow across architectures (GPT2-Small, CNNs, ResNets, ViTs, and linear models) while Adam and sign descent remain robust, and that the effect is deterministic rather than stochastic. On a softmax linear model, it identifies an 'assignment mechanism': as the model learns to assign samples to their correct classes, the gradient and Hessian blocks w.r.t. each parameter row w_k become correlated with class frequency π_k, making the gradient magnitude a reasonable proxy for the Hessian diagonal. This correlation is formalized in Proposition 2, which derives explicit expressions for the gradient and Hessian blocks under a correct-assignment assumption, showing that the gradient norm scales as (1/p)·Tr(Hessian) in the limit of many classes. The paper further proves (Theorem 3) that in continuous time on a simplified imbalanced problem, gradient flow converges at rate Θ(1/π_k t) for class k—slow for rare classes—while sign descent converges at rate Θ(e^{-ct}) independent of class frequency, formally establishing the benefit of sign-based updates under heavy-tailed imbalance.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is Theorem 3: on a simple imbalanced setting, gradient flow minimizes the loss of class k at rate Θ(1/π_k t) (sublinear, dependent on class frequency), while continuous-time sign descent achieves Θ(e^{-ct}) (linear, independent of class frequency). Empirically, on GPT2-Small trained on WikiText-103, SGD's loss on the least-frequent 10% of classes remains above its initialization value even after 15k steps, while Adam drives all frequency groups to low loss. This separation is reproduced across CNNs on MNIST, ResNet18 on ImageNet, vision transformers, and even a softmax linear model on synthetic data, confirming that heavy-tailed class imbalance alone (not architecture or stochasticity) is sufficient to create the performance gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) consistently outperforms GD/SGD on low-frequency classes across every setting tested: GPT2-Small on WikiText-103, CNNs on imbalanced MNIST, ResNet18 on heavy-tailed ImageNet, and a linear model on synthetic data. The paper isolates the mechanism by comparing against sign descent (Tieleman & Hinton, 2012) and normalized GD (Nacson et al., 2019): sign descent matches Adam's performance on low-frequency classes, while normalized GD (which changes only the update magnitude, not direction) provides a smaller improvement. This shows that the critical component of Adam's advantage is the per-coordinate direction change (normalization by gradient magnitude), not merely rescaling the step size. The paper further shows that upweighting the loss of rare classes by 1/√π_k partially closes the gap for SGD, supporting the class-imbalance hypothesis. On uniform (balanced) datasets like plain MNIST or uniform ImageNet subsets, GD and Adam perform similarly, confirming that the gap is specifically driven by heavy-tailed imbalance rather than being a universal property of Adam.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the class imbalance optimization literature from binary or few-class settings (Anand et al., 1993; Francazi et al., 2023) to the heavy-tailed, many-class regime characteristic of language modeling with 100k+ tokens. It departs from prior explanations for the Adam-SGD gap on transformers—noise resilience (Zhang et al., 2020b), gradient-Hessian magnitude correlation (Zhang et al., 2020a; Crawshaw et al., 2022), and sign-descent equivalence (Kunstner et al., 2023)—by identifying the data distribution itself (Zipf-like class frequencies) as the root cause and providing a mechanistic account (the 'assignment mechanism') for how gradient-Hessian correlation emerges during training. The theoretical analysis builds on the normalized-update convergence results of Nacson et al. (2019) but introduces the novel dependence on class frequencies π_k.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first concrete, reproducible mechanism explaining why Adam outperforms SGD specifically on language tasks, with evidence spanning transformers, CNNs, ResNets, ViTs, and linear models across both language and vision data. The practical implication is that future optimizers targeting language or other heavy-tailed-class problems should explicitly account for class-frequency-dependent curvature, and that simple interventions like upweighting rare classes can partially close the gap. Open questions include the interaction between stochasticity and class imbalance, whether the training-loss dynamics translate to generalization in overfitting-prone regimes, and whether other heavy-tailed structures in text (input token frequencies, latent rules) contribute additional optimization difficulties beyond the output class distribution.
