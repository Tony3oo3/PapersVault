## Problem

A broad class of established ML algorithms—self-training, active learning, multi-armed bandits, Bayesian optimization—iteratively modify their training data based on the current model fit, yet no unified framework exists to analyze their convergence, and no rigorous guarantees of when (or whether) they reach a stable point are available. The paper identifies this shared structure as 'reciprocal learning' and asks: under what conditions do these algorithms converge, and how close is the converged solution to the optimal data-parameter combination?

## Input / Output

The framework takes an initial labeled sample, a loss function (assumed strongly convex with Lipschitz gradients), a data selection criterion c(x,θ), and a model parameter space Θ. It iteratively produces a joint fixed point (θ*, P*) where the model parameters and the empirical training distribution are mutually consistent—i.e., θ* solves ERM on the sample it itself induces. The output is thus not just a parameter vector but a stable data-parameter pair, with the training set size held constant in the non-greedy variant (one instance added, one removed per iteration).

## Method

The paper formulates reciprocal learning as two interdependent decision problems: parameter selection (ERM) and data selection (choosing which features to add/remove based on the current model). The key technical contribution is proving that the sample adaption function f—which maps (θ_{t-1}, P_{t-1}) to P_t—is Lipschitz-continuous when (1) data selection is non-greedy (both adds and removes instances), (2) predictions are probabilistic (soft labels via a sigmoid), and (3) the selection criterion is either randomized (stochastic drawing) or regularized (adding a strongly convex penalty to the criterion). Under these conditions, the full reciprocal learning operator becomes a contraction in the Wasserstein-1 metric, and the Banach fixed-point theorem guarantees linear convergence to a unique fixed point. The paper further shows the converged parameter is within O(L/γ) of the globally optimal data-parameter combination, and introduces 'data regularization' as a novel concept symmetrical to classical parameter regularization.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: it proves that non-greedy reciprocal learning converges at a linear rate (Theorem 3) when the sample adaption function is Lipschitz-continuous with constant L ≤ (1+β/γ)⁻¹, and that the convergent solution is within ‖θ_c − θ*‖₂ ≤ 2L_ℓL/γ of the optimal data-parameter combination (Theorem 4). Empirically, on the Banknote Authentication dataset (90%, 80%, 70% unlabeled), self-training with a regularized Bayesian selection criterion exhibits noticeably lower in-experiment L2-norm variation of θ_t compared to its unregularized counterpart, confirming the stabilizing effect of data regularization. However, the regularized criterion shows higher between-experiment variation across 40 restarts, suggesting a stability–diversity trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The empirical comparison (Appendix C, Figures 6–7) pits the proposed regularized Bayesian criterion against four unregularized selection criteria on the Banknote Authentication dataset: the unregularized Bayesian criterion [83], the Likelihood criterion from superset learning [35], the Predictive Variance criterion [80], and the Probability Score criterion [103]. The regularized Bayesian criterion (gold) consistently shows the lowest in-experiment L2-norm fluctuation of θ_t across all three unlabeled-data fractions, confirming the theoretical prediction that data regularization enforces Lipschitz-continuity of the sample adaption. The unregularized Bayesian criterion (red) and the other three baselines exhibit larger oscillations in parameter norm, particularly in early iterations. Notably, the regularized criterion's lower in-experiment stability comes at the cost of higher between-experiment variance (wider 95% confidence bands over 40 restarts), a trade-off the authors flag but do not resolve. The paper does not report standard predictive accuracy metrics; the comparison is purely about parameter trajectory stability, consistent with the paper's focus on convergence rather than generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies self-training, active learning, multi-armed bandits, Bayesian optimization, and superset learning under a single decision-theoretic framework called reciprocal learning, where both parameters and training data are iteratively updated. It builds most directly on performative prediction (Perdomo et al. 2020; Perdomo 2023), borrowing the Lipschitz-continuity and Wasserstein-distance machinery, but departs conceptually by studying in-sample (rather than population-level) reflexivity of both data and parameters. The introduction of 'data regularization' as a symmetrical counterpart to classical parameter regularization is a novel conceptual contribution that has no direct precedent in the ERM literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous convergence guarantees for a broad class of iterative data-adaptive algorithms, identifying that non-greedy (add-and-remove) operation, probabilistic predictions, and either randomized or regularized data selection are jointly sufficient for linear convergence to an approximately optimal model. The negative result that greedy (add-only) algorithms provably cannot converge is practically significant, as it explains why amending self-training strategies are preferred in practice. However, the assumptions (strong convexity, Lipschitz gradients with a specific bound) are restrictive, and the empirical validation is limited to a single tabular dataset with a generalized additive model, leaving open whether these design principles transfer to deep learning settings. The stability of reciprocal learning with respect to perturbations in initial data remains an open question.
