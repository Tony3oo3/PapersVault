## Problem

fMRI-based visual decoding has achieved strong reconstruction performance but is unportable, expensive, and limited by low temporal resolution, making it impractical for real-time brain-computer interfaces. EEG offers portability, low cost, and millisecond-level temporal resolution, yet prior EEG/MEG decoding frameworks have not been thoroughly explored, leaving a significant performance gap relative to fMRI. This paper addresses that gap by proposing the first end-to-end zero-shot EEG-based framework that unifies classification, retrieval, and image reconstruction from a single trained encoder.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw EEG (64-channel, 250 Hz, 0–1000 ms post-stimulus, band-pass filtered to 0.1–100 Hz) or MEG (271-channel, 200 Hz) signals without any segmentation—each full time series is treated as a single patch. The trained ATM encoder produces a 1024-dimensional CLIP-aligned embedding per trial, which is then used zero-shot for three tasks: cosine-similarity classification against text embeddings, retrieval against image embeddings in a 200-image pool, and as the conditioning signal for a two-stage diffusion pipeline that outputs full-resolution RGB images. No task-specific fine-tuning is required after the initial contrastive pre-training.

## Method

The core encoder, ATM (Adaptive Thinking Mapper), combines a channel-wise Transformer with sinusoidal positional embeddings, a channel-attention module for cross-electrode integration, a plug-and-play Temporal-Spatial convolution backbone (interchangeable between ShallowNet and EEGNetV4), and a residual MLP projector with LayerNorm, totaling ~3.2 M parameters. Training uses a combined CLIP contrastive loss (for alignment) and MSE loss (for regression consistency), with subject-specific tokens enabling both within-subject and cross-subject inference. For image generation, a two-stage pipeline is employed: Stage I trains a lightweight U-Net prior diffusion model to denoise CLIP embeddings conditioned on the EEG embedding (p(z_I|z_E)) using classifier-free guidance; Stage II feeds the generated CLIP embedding into a frozen SDXL + IP-Adapter model, augmented with an img2img low-level VAE-latent pipeline and an optional GIT caption for semantic guidance, to synthesize the final image. This separation of high-level semantic features (CLIP space) and low-level pixel features (VAE latent space) is the key architectural insight that enables reliable reconstruction from less than 500 ms of EEG.

## Results

On the THINGS-EEG dataset, the proposed ATM-S encoder achieves a reconstruction CLIP score of 0.786 and SSIM of 0.345, outperforming the NICE baseline (CLIP 0.722, SSIM 0.276) and EEGNetV4 (CLIP 0.773, SSIM 0.302). For zero-shot retrieval on 200 images, ATM-S reaches 26.13% Top-1 and 55.32% Top-5 accuracy (in-subject, batch size 1024), surpassing NICE (21.52%/51.57%) and EEGNetV4 (19.51%/48.99%). On THINGS-MEG, the method improves pixel correlation from 0.058 (Benchetrit) to 0.104 while maintaining competitive SSIM (0.340 vs. 0.327). Temporal analysis shows that reliable image reconstruction is achievable with as little as 500 ms of EEG data, with classification accuracy plateauing at ~30% Top-1 after this window.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the NICE encoder [5] on THINGS-EEG, ATM-S wins on every reconstruction metric: PixCorr (0.160 vs. 0.142), SSIM (0.345 vs. 0.276), CLIP (0.786 vs. 0.722), and SwAV (0.582 vs. 0.612, lower is better). Against EEGNetV4 [31], the gains are more modest but consistent across all seven metrics (e.g., SSIM 0.345 vs. 0.302, Inception 0.734 vs. 0.713). On THINGS-MEG, the method improves pixel correlation substantially over Benchetrit et al. [12] (0.104 vs. 0.058) and SSIM (0.340 vs. 0.327), but trails on high-level semantic metrics like AlexNet(5) (0.672 vs. 0.753) and CLIP (0.603 vs. 0.700), suggesting the MEG reconstruction captures low-level structure better than high-level semantics. In zero-shot retrieval (Table 8), ATM-S leads all competing EEG encoders by a wide margin: 26.13% Top-1 vs. NICE's 21.52% and EEGNetV4's 19.51%, with the gap widening further in cross-subject settings (8.24% vs. 8.24% for NICE, 6.13% for EEGNetV4). Compared to fMRI baselines (NSD-fMRI), the EEG method remains substantially below on all metrics (e.g., CLIP 0.786 vs. 0.917–0.942), confirming that fMRI still holds an advantage in reconstruction fidelity, though the gap is notably narrower than in prior EEG work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the two-stage generation pipeline from Mind's Eyes (fMRI-to-image via prior diffusion on CLIP embeddings, then SDXL synthesis) to the EEG/MEG modality, building on the contrastive EEG-to-CLIP alignment paradigm established by Benchetrit et al. and Song et al. The key architectural departure is the ATM encoder, which combines channel-wise Transformer attention, plug-and-play temporal-spatial convolution (ShallowNet or EEGNetV4), and an MLP projector into a unified, modular design that outperforms each component individually. Unlike prior EEG decoding work that was limited to small supervised category sets or linear decoders, this framework positions EEG decoding squarely within the zero-shot CLIP embedding space, enabling open-vocabulary classification, retrieval, and reconstruction without task-specific fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that portable, low-cost EEG can achieve zero-shot visual decoding and reconstruction performance approaching MEG and, on some metrics, competitive with fMRI, which has significant practical implications for real-world BCI deployment. The temporal analysis revealing category-dependent processing speeds (e.g., jelly beans at 200 ms vs. aircraft carriers at 500 ms) and the counterintuitive finding that EEG outperforms MEG on visual tasks (opposite to speech decoding) offer genuine neuroscience insights. Key limitations include a substantial cross-subject performance gap, reconstruction quality still well below fMRI (PixCorr 0.16 vs. 0.31), and evaluation restricted to the THINGS dataset; open questions include whether EEG source localization can bridge cross-dataset generalization and whether transfer/meta-learning can close the inter-subject variability gap.
