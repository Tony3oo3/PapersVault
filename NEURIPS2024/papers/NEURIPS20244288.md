## Problem

In batched best arm identification (BBAI), arm pull results are only revealed at the end of each batch, making fully sequential algorithms like Track-and-Stop impractical. Prior batched BAI algorithms either fail to achieve the asymptotically optimal sample complexity T*(μ)·log(1/δ), or require a number of batches that grows with δ or other problem parameters. Additionally, in the non-asymptotic (finite δ) setting, existing batched algorithms' complexity guarantees are conditioned on the event that the best arm is returned, leaving the complexity potentially unbounded when a sub-optimal arm is returned.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes n arms whose reward distributions belong to a known one-parameter exponential family (e.g., Bernoulli, Gaussian) and a pre-specified confidence parameter δ > 0. Arms are pulled in batches: within a batch, multiple arms can be pulled in parallel, but all results are revealed only after the batch completes. The output is the index of the best arm (guaranteed correct with probability ≥ 1−δ), along with the realized sample complexity (total pulls) and batch complexity (total batches), which the algorithm aims to minimize simultaneously.

## Method

Tri-BBAI achieves asymptotic optimality in 3 batches: Stage I performs uniform exploration to estimate the optimal allocation w*(μ) and T*(μ) from Track-and-Stop; Stage II pulls each arm i proportionally to w_i*(b^q)·T*(b^q)·log(1/δ) across a small number of batches until the allocation stabilizes; Stage III applies Chernoff's stopping rule to test whether sufficient statistical evidence has been gathered, returning the best arm if so. Opt-BBAI refines Stage IV (triggered when the stopping rule fails) with an exponential gap elimination procedure that progressively eliminates sub-optimal arms, combined with a novel 'checking for best arm elimination' subroutine that re-pulls previously eliminated arms and adaptively tightens confidence bounds to guarantee bounded expected sample and batch complexity even on the low-probability event that the best arm is eliminated. This yields near-optimal non-asymptotic sample complexity O(Σ_{i>1} Δ_i^{-2} log(n log Δ_i^{-1})) and O(log(1/Δ_2)) batches, while recovering the asymptotic 3-batch, T*(μ)·log(1/δ) behavior as δ→0.

## Results

Tri-BBAI achieves the asymptotically optimal sample complexity of T*(μ)log(1/δ) + o(1/δ) while running in only 3 batches in expectation (with probability 1 − 1/log(1/δ²)), which is the first batched algorithm to achieve this. Opt-BBAI extends this to the non-asymptotic setting, achieving sample complexity O(Σ_{i>1} log(n·log Δ_i^{-1})/Δ_i²) and batch complexity O(log(1/Δ_2)) for finite δ, while recovering the same 3-batch asymptotic behavior as δ→0. Notably, the non-asymptotic complexity of Opt-BBAI is unconditional (not conditioned on the event of returning the best arm), unlike prior batched algorithms where complexity could be unbounded on the δ-probability failure event.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the sequential Track-and-Stop [17], Tri-BBAI matches the asymptotic sample complexity T*(μ)log(1/δ) but reduces batch complexity from O(T*(μ)log(1/δ)) (one batch per pull) to a constant 3, a dramatic improvement for parallel implementations. Compared to Jin et al. [22] (Top-k δ-Elimination), Opt-BBAI improves the batch complexity from O(log*(n)·log(1/Δ₂)) to O(log(1/Δ₂)) and, critically, provides unconditional complexity guarantees rather than ones conditioned on the best arm being returned. Against Wang et al. [42] and Agrawal et al. [2], which also achieve asymptotic optimality, Opt-BBAI uses a constant 3 batches versus O(√log(1/δ)) and T*(μ)log(1/δ)/m batches respectively. In the non-asymptotic regime, Opt-BBAI matches Karpov et al. [32] on sample complexity O(Σ_{i>1} log(n log Δ_i^{-1})/Δ_i²) and batch complexity O(log(1/Δ₂)), but additionally achieves asymptotic optimality, which Karpov et al. do not address. The batch complexity of Opt-BBAI matches the lower bound of Tao et al. [39] (Ω(log(1/Δ₂))) up to a log log factor, and the sample complexity matches the known near-optimal bound (1.4) within a log(·) factor. Numerical experiments confirm that Opt-BBAI significantly outperforms Track-and-Stop in batch complexity while maintaining comparable sample complexity, and improves sample complexity over [22] with similar batch counts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Track-and-Stop (Garivier & Kaufmann, 2016) for its core allocation strategy (pulling arms proportional to w*(μ)T*(μ)) and Chernoff's stopping rule, and on Even-Dar et al. (2002) for the successive elimination procedure in the non-asymptotic stage. It departs from prior batched BAI work (Jin et al. 2019, Karpov et al. 2020, Tao et al. 2019) that achieved near-optimal non-asymptotic bounds but could not attain asymptotic optimality, and from sequential algorithms like Track-and-Stop that achieve asymptotic optimality but require unbounded batches as δ→0. The key conceptual departure is the 'checking for best arm elimination' procedure, which makes the sample and batch complexity unconditional rather than conditioned on the low-probability event of returning the best arm—a subtlety overlooked in prior batched algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that asymptotic optimality is achievable in a constant (3) number of batches resolves a natural open question in batched bandits and has direct practical implications for applications where policy switching is expensive (medical trials, crowdsourcing). The unconditional complexity guarantee addresses a real gap: prior batched algorithms could have unbounded complexity on the δ-probability event of returning a sub-optimal arm, making them unreliable in practice. Open questions include whether the sample complexity can more closely match Track-and-Stop while maintaining constant batches, and whether the 'checking for best arm elimination' technique generalizes to other elimination-based algorithms or to streaming bandit settings.
