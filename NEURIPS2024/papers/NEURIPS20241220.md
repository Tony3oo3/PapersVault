## Problem

Iterative self-improvement methods for LLMs (self-instruct, self-refine, iterative DPO, etc.) show empirical gains but lack a systematic theoretical explanation of why they work and what biases they amplify. As LLMs increasingly learn from data generated by other LLMs or themselves, understanding the long-term evolutionary dynamics of these processes is critical for alignment and bias mitigation, yet no unified framework existed to predict how subtle prior biases compound across generations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241220/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input an instruction prompt w, a set of few-shot data examples d^{t-1} generated by a previous-generation LLM (or the same model), and an optional interaction-phase design that filters or re-ranks transmitted data. It produces a posterior distribution P_{lmw}(h) over all possible input-output mappings, a convergence guarantee identifying which hypothesis h^{T*} the system will lock onto, and predictions about how the strength of prior bias versus likelihood determines the speed and direction of amplification. In the explicit-h ACRE experiments, the posterior is read directly from token-level probabilities; in the implicit-h and DPO settings, bias amplification is tracked through proxy metrics (proportion of easy acronyms, average response length, win rate).

## Method

The paper establishes a formal bridge between LLM in-context learning and Bayesian iterated learning by proving (Proposition 2) that ICL sampling decomposes into MAP estimation of a hidden hypothesis h followed by generation from p(d|h*), reusing the de-marginalization technique from Xie et al. (2022). It then proves (Proposition 1) that imitation-only iterated learning of Bayesian agents converges to a stationary point of the prior P_0(h) by drawing a structural analogy to the EM algorithm, where the transmission phase acts as the E-step and the imitation phase as the M-step. The interaction phase is modeled as imposing a constraint h ∈ H_eff, which redirects convergence to the argmax of P_0(h) restricted to H_eff, thereby allowing practitioners to steer which bias is amplified. The theory is validated across three experimental regimes: explicit-h inductive reasoning (ACRE task with GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3-haiku, Mixtral-8x7b), implicit-h self-data-augmentation (acronym brainstorming), and in-weights preference optimization (on-policy DPO on Llama-2-7B with Anthropic-HH), demonstrating that prior bias is amplified generation-by-generation and that a well-designed interaction phase can redirect or suppress it.

## Results

On the ACRE inductive reasoning task (M=5 objects, 243 possible hypotheses), hypothesis-search achieves 7.4–7.7 correct predictions out of 8 on d⁰ and a 45–55% 'BOTH' rate (correct + bias-amplified) across GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3-haiku, and Mixtral-8x7b, compared to only 0–30% for imitation-only learning. The posterior entropy H(P_lm(h)) decreases to near zero over 6 generations, confirming convergence to a one-hot distribution as predicted by Proposition 1. In the on-policy DPO experiment (Llama-2-7B on Anthropic-HH), self-iterated DPO amplifies average response length far beyond the SFT baseline and RLHF, while adding a conciseness constraint (H_short) reduces length growth while maintaining high win rate against SFT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between three interaction-phase designs on the ACRE task: imitation-only (no filtering), self-refine (model evaluates its own output), and hypothesis-search (external ground-truth interpreter). Hypo-search consistently outperforms both baselines on all four LLMs: it achieves 45–55% BOTH rate versus 0–30% for imitation-only and 15–35% for self-refine, while also attaining the highest training accuracy (7.4–7.7/8 vs. 4.8–6.4 for imitation-only). This demonstrates that a more accurate H_eff (external interpreter) is crucial for guiding evolution. In the on-policy DPO experiment (Section 7), the paper compares self-iterated DPO against non-self-iterated RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022): on-policy DPO significantly amplifies the length bias (average response length grows much larger than both SFT and RLHF baselines), confirming that self-iteration is the key driver of bias amplification. Adding a conciseness constraint to the annotator prompt (imposing H_helpful ∩ H_short) successfully restrains length growth while preserving helpfulness, whereas an overly strict constraint (H_helpful ∩ H_veryshort = ∅) degrades helpfulness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: the Bayesian Iterated Learning (IL) framework from cognitive science (Kirby et al. 2007, 2015), which models how cultural biases amplify across generations of learners, and the Bayesian interpretation of in-context learning (Xie et al. 2022), which shows LLMs perform implicit posterior updates over latent hypotheses. By formally connecting these (Proposition 2), the paper recasts iterative self-improvement methods (self-instruct, self-refine, RAFT, ReST, iterative DPO) as instances of Bayesian-IL, providing the first convergence guarantee (Proposition 1) for the full process including an interaction phase. The proof technique draws a direct parallel between imitation-only IL and the EM algorithm, a connection not previously made in the LLM self-improvement literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The guaranteed bias amplification result (Proposition 1) provides a principled explanation for well-observed but previously unexplained phenomena in iterative self-improvement: mode collapse, loss of creativity, and accumulation of spurious correlations across generations. The practical implication is that the interaction phase design (H_eff) is the primary lever for steering evolution, which the paper validates across four LLM families and in a real on-policy DPO setting. Key open questions remain: how to identify and counteract hidden biases that are amplified without being explicitly known, whether the framework extends to heterogeneous-agent settings (e.g., weak-to-strong generalization), and how temperature and sample size interact with the convergence dynamics. The experiments, while spanning multiple models, rely on relatively controlled tasks (ACRE, acronym generation), leaving open whether the qualitative predictions hold in fully open-ended generation.
