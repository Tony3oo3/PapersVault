## Problem

Existing multimodal learning frameworks either rely on a single modality or require all modalities to be present, failing to exploit the complementary information in arbitrary subsets of available modalities. In real-world clinical settings, patients exhibit diverse and incomplete modality profiles, yet prior methods overlook how specific observed-modality combinations interact and what unique knowledge each combination carries for downstream prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243134/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

For ADNI, the method ingests 3D MRI volumes (91×109×91), high-dimensional SNP arrays (~567K features), tabular clinical records (demographics, exams, medications, vitals), and biospecimen biomarkers (ApoE genotypes, tau levels), producing a three-class AD stage label (CN, MCI, Dementia). For MIMIC-IV, it takes lab/vital values, free-text clinical notes, and ICD-9 codes to predict binary one-year mortality. Critically, any subset of these modalities may be absent per sample; missing modalities are not zero-padded or imputed but replaced with combination-specific learnable embeddings drawn from a bank indexed by the observed modality set.

## Method

Flex-MoE combines a missing modality bank with a two-stage Sparse MoE training procedure. The bank (B ∈ R^(2^|M|−1 × |M|)) stores learnable embeddings for each missing modality conditioned on the observed combination, so each modality encoder is trained exclusively on its own observed inputs without synthetic padding. The Transformer backbone replaces its FFN with an SMoE layer whose experts are indexed by modality combinations. Training follows a curriculum: first, a Generalized Router (G-Router) trains all experts on full-modality samples using standard top-k gating with load and importance balancing losses; then a Specialized Router (S-Router) handles fewer-modality samples by fixing the top-1 gate to the expert matching the observed combination (enforced via a cross-entropy loss on the router's gating scores) while balancing the remaining k−1 experts. This yields experts that retain globally shared knowledge from complete data while acquiring combination-specific expertise, and at inference the designated top-1 expert is activated alongside other selected experts to fuse both general and specialized signals.

## Results

On the ADNI dataset (4 modalities: Image, Genetic, Clinical, Biospecimen), Flex-MoE achieves 66.11% accuracy in the full-modality setting, outperforming the best baseline MAG (61.44%) by 4.7 points and FuseMoE (59.52%) by 6.6 points. On MIMIC-IV (3 modalities: Lab/Vital, Clinical Notes, ICD-9 Codes), Flex-MoE achieves 76.81% accuracy for full-modality one-year mortality prediction, surpassing the strongest baseline LIMoE (61.61%) by over 15 points. Flex-MoE also maintains strong performance under partial modality availability, e.g., 74.96% on MIMIC-IV with only 2 of 3 modalities, while baselines drop to ~60–65%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Flex-MoE outperforms all nine baselines across both datasets and all modality combinations. On ADNI full modality, the closest competitor is MAG (61.44% ACC), which fuses features via an adaptation vector; Flex-MoE leads by 4.7 points. FuseMoE, the most recent MoE-based multimodal method, achieves 59.52% on ADNI full modality and 63.90% on MIMIC-IV full modality, trailing Flex-MoE by 6.6 and 12.9 points respectively; notably, FuseMoE performs worse with 4 modalities than with 3 on ADNI, indicating it cannot effectively exploit additional modalities. On MIMIC-IV, LIMoE (contrastive MoE) is the strongest baseline at 61.61–64.89% ACC, yet Flex-MoE exceeds it by 10–15 points across all modality subsets. AD-specific baselines (Venugopalan [59], Lee [33]) rely on intersection data and fixed modality sets, yielding 52–56% on ADNI full modality. General multimodal fusion methods (TF, MulT, mmFormer, ShaSpec) range from 46–61% on ADNI and 58–65% on MIMIC-IV, consistently underperforming Flex-MoE by 5–15 points, particularly when modalities are missing.

## Relation to Prior Work

Flex-MoE builds on the Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) architecture and positions itself against two lines of multimodal work: (1) fusion-based methods (Tensor Fusion, MulT, MAG, LIMoE) that typically require complete or imputed inputs, and (2) the closest MoE-based competitor, FuseMoE, which handles missing modalities but does not explicitly model each modality combination as a distinct routing target. The key departure is the two-stage expert training paradigm (G-Router for generalization on complete data, S-Router for specialization on partial combinations) combined with a combination-indexed missing modality bank, which replaces the standard zero-padding or global imputation used by prior missing-modality methods like ShaSpec and mmFormer. The curriculum-learning-inspired sorting of samples by modality count is borrowed from the broader curriculum learning literature but applied here specifically to the MoE expert training schedule.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: real-world medical datasets (ADNI, MIMIC-IV) inherently contain heterogeneous modality availability per patient, yet most multimodal models either discard incomplete samples or rely on crude imputation. Flex-MoE's combination-specific expert design and missing modality bank offer a principled way to exploit every available subset without discarding data, and the 89% parameter reduction over FuseMoE while achieving higher accuracy makes it attractive for deployment. However, the MIMIC-IV results are notably weaker (Flex-MoE underperforms LIMoE and MulT on Macro-F1 and AUC in several modality combinations), raising questions about whether the approach generalizes beyond the AD domain or whether the gains are partly dataset-specific. Open questions include scaling to many more modalities (the 2^|M| bank grows exponentially), whether the two-stage training is necessary versus a single unified router, and how the method behaves with truly dynamic modality availability at inference time.
