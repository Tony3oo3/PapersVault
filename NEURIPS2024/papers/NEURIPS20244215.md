## Problem

Diffusion models trained via Federated Learning introduce a new and more severe privacy vulnerability than discriminative models: an attacker with access to a single compromised client can exfiltrate thousands of high-quality private images from the released global model, even under strict data management that prohibits direct data transfer. Existing distance-based FL defenses (Krum, Multi-Krum, Foolsgold, RFA, Multi-metrics) can filter out brute-force model poisoning, but no prior work addresses how to stealthily implant multiple Trojans into diffusion models while evading these defenses.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a global diffusion model, a local benign dataset, and a local backdoor dataset (target images paired with combinatorial triggers) as inputs. Each trigger is a small patch (3×3 for 32×32 CIFAR-10, 5×5 for 64×64 CelebA, 25×25 for 256×256 LSUN Bedroom) formed by selecting q=2 points from p=225 candidate grid positions, yielding up to 25,200 distinct triggers. The output is a set of reconstructed private images generated by feeding the corresponding Trojan noise (trigger blended with Gaussian noise via weight γ) into the released global model, evaluated by MSE against ground truth and FID for overall generation quality.

## Method

AdaSCP combines three key mechanisms to defeat distance-based FL defenses. First, it identifies critical parameters via Taylor-expansion-based importance scores accumulated over dominant timesteps (where loss exceeds a threshold T), training only the top-τ parameters (~80%) rather than the full network to reduce the update magnitude. Second, it implants a lightweight indicator—a neuron with near-zero gradient and Hessian selected from uncritical parameters—and magnifies its update by a factor k=50; the returned indicator update from the server reveals whether the malicious update was accepted or rejected and implicitly encodes the client's aggregation weight. Third, it adaptively optimizes the scale factor s_r using the indicator feedback: when accepted, s_r moves toward the theoretically derived target (Σλ_i/λ_adv) via a learning-rate-based update; when rejected, s_r decreases toward zero. Historical accepted/rejected scales stabilize the optimization, and a weight decay d reduces the learning rate upon state transitions, preventing oscillation and enabling seamless integration of backdoor gradients into the global model.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (Non-IID), AdaSCP achieves a mean MSE of 0.0657 across six defenses, substantially outperforming Data Poison (0.1304), PGD Poison (0.1232), and BC Layer Substitution (0.1441) in data recovery quality, while maintaining a mean FID of 16.55. On CelebA, AdaSCP reaches a mean MSE of 0.0499 with FID 7.73, and on LSUN Bedroom (256×256), it achieves MSE 0.0941 with FID 29.07. Model Poison collapses under most defenses (mean FID 94.02 on CIFAR-10, 110.00 on LSUN Bedroom), while AdaSCP remains effective under Krum, Multi-Krum, Foolsgold, RFA, and Multi-metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaSCP achieves the lowest mean MSE (best data recovery) across all three datasets: 0.0657 on CIFAR-10, 0.0499 on CelebA, and 0.0941 on LSUN Bedroom, outperforming Data Poison (0.1304/0.1317/0.1297), PGD Poison (0.1232/0.1279/0.1368), and BC Layer Substitution (0.1441/0.1137/0.1328). Model Poison achieves the best MSE under FedAvg alone (0.0069 on CIFAR-10) but catastrophically fails under Foolsgold (FID 459.64) and Multi-metrics (FID 70.98), yielding a mean FID of 94.02 on CIFAR-10. Data Poison and PGD Poison maintain low FID across defenses but cannot effectively implant multiple backdoors, resulting in MSE values roughly 2× higher than AdaSCP. BC Layer Substitution causes training collapse under Krum (FID 132.02 on CIFAR-10, 76.49 on CelebA) and is unsuitable for diffusion models. AdaSCP's adaptive scaling allows it to defeat Multi-Krum, Foolsgold, RFA, and Multi-metrics simultaneously, whereas no other baseline succeeds under more than two defenses without severe FID degradation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Trojan attacks on diffusion models (TrojDiff, BadDiffusion) from single trigger-target pairs to combinatorial triggers enabling massive data mapping, and adapts the model poisoning paradigm from Bagdasaryan et al. by replacing the fixed scale factor with an adaptive, indicator-guided optimization loop. It borrows the indicator feedback mechanism from 3DFed but repurposes it for a single-attacker setting, and adopts diffusion-specific importance estimation from structural pruning work to identify critical parameters for targeted training. The key departure is framing the problem as data exfiltration from FL-trained diffusion models rather than classification backdoors, which requires handling the unique sensitivity of diffusion training to gradient scaling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to demonstrate that diffusion models in FL—chosen precisely for their privacy-preserving generative properties—actually enable more severe data leakage (thousands of high-quality images) than discriminative models, inverting the assumed security benefit. The adaptive defense evasion against multiple distance-based defenses (Krum, Multi-Krum, Foolsgold, RFA, Multi-metrics) is practical and well-evidenced across three datasets and resolutions. Open questions include whether the FID degradation (+10.31 on CIFAR10) can be reduced with better trigger design, whether multi-attacker scenarios change the threat landscape, and whether diffusion-specific defenses (beyond generic distance-based methods) can close this gap without breaking convergence.
