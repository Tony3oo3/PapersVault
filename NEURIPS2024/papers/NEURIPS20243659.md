## Problem

LLMs achieve strong KBQA performance but incur prohibitive API or compute costs and may lack domain-specific knowledge, while lightweight knowledge-graph models (KGMs) are cheap but less accurate. The core challenge is that inferential accuracy and cost are two distinct, non-commensurable metrics that resist joint optimization, and no principled mechanism exists to automatically route each question to the model best suited to its specific knowledge demands.

## Input / Output

The system receives a natural-language multiple-choice question and an external knowledge graph (e.g., ConceptNet) as input. The question is encoded into a fixed-dimensional vector via a frozen RoBERTa encoder, which serves as the contextual feature for the bandit policy. The output is a two-stage decision: first a cluster choice (LLM vs. KGM), then a specific model arm within that cluster, followed by the model's answer. A hard budget B constrains the total token-level API fees (or call count for open-source LLMs) spent across all questions in a session.

## Method

Coke formulates question-specific model selection as a tailored multi-armed bandit problem whose per-iteration expectation decomposes into three additive terms. (1) A cluster-level Thompson Sampling step draws θ_c from a Beta(α_c, β_c) posterior updated by historical success/failure counts to decide whether to invoke an LLM or a KGM, balancing exploration and exploitation at the coarsest level. (2) A context-aware expert-distinguishing step computes E_a(r|q) = q·μ_a + η_a, where μ_a is updated via ridge regression on the arm's historical question-reward pairs and η_a is an upper-confidence-bound noise term that encourages exploration of under-used arms. (3) A cost-regret penalty λ·R_a subtracts the ratio of failure-incurred cost to total cost for arm a, subject to a cumulative budget constraint, thereby discouraging arms that waste expensive LLM calls on questions they answer incorrectly. The three terms are combined as E(r|q) = E_c + E_a − λ·R_a, and the arm maximizing this composite score is selected; the paper proves a selection-regret bound of O(γ + Σ E[r]) for the cluster-level component.

## Results

On the API-series comparison, Coke achieves 82.4% test accuracy on CommonsenseQA, 90.8% on OpenBookQA, and 77.8% on MedQA-USMLE, outperforming GPT-4 by +2.74%, +0.67%, and +1.03% respectively while saving 20.89%, 11.02%, and 4.32% of GPT-4 API costs. On the local-series (MedQA only), Coke with arms {HamQA, Llama2-7B, Llama3-8B} reaches 69.2% test accuracy, marginally exceeding Llama3-8B (69.1%) while reducing LLM calls by 0.66%. The ablation study confirms each component contributes: removing the cluster-level Thompson Sampling (E_c) drops cost savings by up to 47.59% on CSQA, while removing the context-aware policy (E_a) reduces MedQA accuracy from 77.8% to 76.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Coke outperforms GPT-4 on all three datasets in both accuracy and cost: +2.74% accuracy with 20.89% cost savings on CSQA, +0.67% with 11.02% savings on OBQA, and +1.03% with 4.32% savings on MedQA. Against the strongest KGM baseline HamQA (73.9% CSQA, 84.6% OBQA, 38.5% MedQA), Coke achieves substantially higher accuracy on all three datasets, confirming that the LLM arms are necessary for complex questions. Compared to GPT-3.5 (71.0% CSQA, 60.0% OBQA, 48.7% MedQA), Coke improves by 11.4%, 30.8%, and 29.1% respectively, showing the benefit of combining KGMs with top-tier LLMs rather than relying on a single mid-tier LLM. Among fine-tuned LMs (RoBERTa-large: 68.7% CSQA, 64.8% OBQA, 36.1% MedQA) and other KGMs (JointLK, GreaseLM, GrapeQA), Coke dominates across the board. On the local-series MedQA comparison, Coke (69.2%) marginally beats Llama3-8B (69.1%) while saving 0.66% in LLM calls, a much smaller gap than the API-series results due to the large performance gap between KGMs and LLMs on medical questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

Coke repurposes multi-armed bandit theory (Thompson Sampling, contextual bandits with ridge regression) for a new application: routing questions between LLMs and knowledge-graph-based models (KGMs) under a budget constraint. Prior KBQA work (MHGRN, QA-GNN, HamQA, GreaseLM) focuses on improving a single model's reasoning over KGs, while LLM-based QA work focuses on prompt design or fine-tuning; neither addresses the meta-level decision of which model to invoke per question. The cost-regret formulation, which penalizes arms proportionally to their failure-weighted expenditure, is a novel constraint not seen in prior bandit-for-model-selection literature, and the cluster-level Beta-distribution prior over LLM-vs-KGM groups is a tailored extension of standard Thompson Sampling to a two-tier hierarchy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM API costs remain prohibitive for high-throughput deployment (e.g., e-commerce, customer service), a principled routing mechanism that provably moves the accuracy-cost Pareto frontier is practically valuable. The framework is 'fast-pluggable'—new models can be added as arms without retraining—but its effectiveness is bounded by the performance gap between the best KGM and the best LLM; on MedQA, where that gap reaches 36.5%, the framework degenerates toward always calling GPT-4, yielding only marginal savings. An open question is whether the approach retains value as open-source LLMs close the gap with proprietary APIs, and whether the bandit formulation generalizes to multi-turn or agentic QA settings where cost accumulates across steps.
