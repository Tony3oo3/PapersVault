## Problem

In-context learning with generative AI is widely used but offers no principled way to estimate the probability that a model will hallucinate on a given query. Existing hallucination detection methods rely on external auxiliary classifiers or are restricted to specific task types, leaving a gap in understanding when and how often ICL produces unreliable responses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240982/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an ICL problem consisting of a conditional generative model (e.g., Llama-2-7B), a context of n query-response pairs, and a new prediction query, and produces a scalar posterior hallucination rate (PHR) estimate between 0 and 1. Crucially, it requires only sampling responses from the CGM and evaluating their log-probabilities under extended contexts—no access to the true data-generating mechanism or the posterior over mechanisms is needed. The method is parameterized by a hallucination threshold ε (defining the (1−ε)-likely set), a Monte Carlo budget M for context samples, K for response samples, and a maximum context length N.

## Method

The paper adopts a Bayesian interpretation of ICL in which the CGM approximates the posterior predictive of an unknown Bayesian model over latent mechanisms, and defines a hallucination as a response falling outside a (1−ε)-likely set under the true mechanism. The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 2) leverages Doob's theorem to show that the posterior hallucination rate—normally requiring intractable integration over the posterior of mechanisms—can be equivalently expressed using only the predictive distribution, by replacing the mechanism with an infinite sequence of future data points. Practically, Algorithm 1 estimates the PHR by (1) sampling N−n imagined future query-response pairs from the CGM's predictive distribution to form an extended context, (2) sampling K responses from the original context, and (3) checking whether each response's log-probability under the extended context falls below the ε-quantile of log-probabilities of responses sampled from the extended context itself. This reduces the problem to repeated sampling and log-probability evaluation, making it applicable to any CGM that supports autoregressive generation.

## Results

On synthetic regression tasks, the PHR estimator closely tracks the True Hallucination Rate (THR) across the domain, with both decreasing as context size n increases; however, the PHR systematically underestimates the THR, with R² degrading as n grows (e.g., strong alignment at n=2 but lower R² at n=100). On natural language ICL tasks with Llama-2-7B, the PHR closely follows the Model Hallucination Rate (MHR) across all six tasks (SST2, Subjectivity, AG News, Medical QP, RTE, WNLI) at ε=0.05. At ε=0.75, the PHR accurately predicts the empirical error rate for SST2, Subjectivity, and AG News, but diverges for Medical QP, RTE, and WNLI—precisely the tasks where Llama-2-7B performs near random guessing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against other hallucination detection or uncertainty quantification methods; instead, it validates the PHR estimator against ground-truth and proxy metrics. In the synthetic regression setting, the PHR is compared directly to the analytically computable THR, showing close alignment at small context sizes but a consistent underestimation bias at larger n, attributed to the gap between the learned p_θ and the true distribution p. In the natural language setting, the PHR is compared to two proxies: the Model Hallucination Rate (MHR, which requires an evaluation dataset unavailable at test time) and the empirical error rate. The PHR matches MHR across all tasks regardless of model performance, and matches the error rate only when the LLM substantially outperforms a random classifier. The paper positions itself as complementary to prior uncertainty-based hallucination detection methods (e.g., semantic uncertainty [33], SelfCheckGPT [37]) by requiring no auxiliary classifiers and being applicable beyond language tasks, but does not provide head-to-head quantitative comparisons with those approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes two prior threads: the Bayesian interpretation of in-context learning (Xie et al. [8], Müller et al. [7]), which treats a CGM as implicitly computing a posterior predictive over latent mechanisms, and the predictive resampling framework of Fong et al. [39] (martingale posteriors), which shows how to estimate posterior quantities using only samples from the predictive distribution. The key departure from prior hallucination-detection methods (semantic uncertainty [33], self-consistency [37], auxiliary classifiers [36]) is that the PHR requires no external information beyond the CGM itself and is grounded in a formal Bayesian estimand rather than heuristic similarity metrics. The paper also positions itself against Falck et al. [41], who proposed stricter tests for whether ICL is Bayesian; here the authors argue that even when ICL does not perfectly conform to Bayesian inference, adopting the Bayesian perspective still yields a practically useful hallucination estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The PHR provides a theoretically grounded, self-contained way to estimate hallucination probability for any ICL problem using only the CGM's predictive distribution—no ground-truth mechanism, auxiliary classifier, or evaluation set is needed at test time. However, the method's accuracy is tightly coupled to the assumption that the CGM faithfully approximates the true posterior predictive; in experiments, the PHR underestimates the true hallucination rate (especially at larger context lengths) and fails to predict error rates on tasks where the model performs near-randomly (Medical QP, RTE, WNLI), revealing that it captures only 'special' epistemic uncertainty (insufficient context) and not 'general' epistemic uncertainty (model incapacity). Open questions include quantifying how divergences between p_θ and p_ICL degrade the estimator, extending the framework to handle general epistemic uncertainty and extrinsic hallucinations, and understanding under what conditions the de Finetti/exchangeability assumption breaks down for transformer architectures.
