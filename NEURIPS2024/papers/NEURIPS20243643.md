## Problem

While rapid post-event flood mapping and river streamflow forecasting have advanced significantly, there is no dataset or benchmark for the ahead-of-time prediction of flood inundation extent maps. This gap is particularly acute for (near-)coastal regions, where compound flood drivers (storm surge, heavy rain, high tides) make forecasting challenging and where early warning systems are most needed for disaster preparedness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243643/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes two distinct spatial scales: coarse context at 5–30 km resolution (20-day ERA5/ERA5-Land time series, HydroATLAS basin attributes, downsampled CopDEM30) and fine local data at 10 m resolution (pre-flood Sentinel-1 SAR, CopDEM30, HAND drainage maps), a three-orders-of-magnitude gap with no prior solution handling both simultaneously. The output is a binary flood segmentation map (background vs. water) at 10 m resolution in 224×224 pixel tiles, predicted at a variable lead time d days after the pre-flood SAR observation. Permanent water pixels from ESA WorldCover are masked out during evaluation to focus on class-change pixels.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-level pipeline platform that decouples coarse-context encoding from fine-local segmentation. A context network (any spatio-temporal backbone: U-TAE, LSTM, 3DConv, MaxViT) processes the 20-day multi-temporal coarse sequence, and its output embedding is concatenated with high-resolution local inputs fed to a local segmentation network of the same backbone architecture (no shared weights). The prediction lead time d is injected via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) conditioning. The dataset itself is generated by ensembling a masked auto-encoder ViT and a SNUNet change detector on pre/in-event Sentinel-1 pairs, followed by tiling-artifact removal via 50%-overlap logit averaging, majority filtering, and permanent-water merging. Five baselines are benchmarked under a 5-fold cross-validation split defined by HydroATLAS level-4 basins, with two tracks: global flood prediction and a coastal-vs-inland separation at a 10 km distance threshold.

## Results

On the GFF dataset (Track 1, global flood extent forecasting), U-TAE achieves the best overall F1 score of 0.77±0.04, with a background-class F1 of 0.97±0.00 and a water-class F1 of 0.57±0.07. LSTM U-Net and 3DConv U-Net follow closely at F1 0.76±0.04, while logistic regression lags significantly at F1 0.66±0.04. On the Kuro Siwo hand-annotated subset, LSTM U-Net edges ahead (F1 0.73±0.03) over U-TAE (0.72±0.02), suggesting comparable performance between derived and expert labels. Adding GloFAS river discharge to U-TAE slightly reduces overall F1 to 0.76±0.05, indicating that explicit hydrological modeling does not straightforwardly improve extent prediction in this setup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

U-TAE (temporal attention) achieves the highest overall F1 of 0.77 on Track 1, narrowly beating LSTM U-Net (0.76) and 3DConv U-Net (0.76) by ~0.01, with the gap within one standard deviation. On the Kuro Siwo hand-annotated subset, LSTM U-Net reverses the ranking to 0.73 vs. U-TAE's 0.72, and MaxViT U-Net also reaches 0.73, indicating that the derived ensemble labels and expert labels yield broadly consistent model rankings. Logistic regression, operating only on mono-temporal local features, trails all deep baselines by 0.10–0.11 in F1, confirming that spatio-temporal context is essential for flood extent forecasting. On Track 2 (coastal vs. inland), U-TAE and 3DConv U-Net tie at F1 0.80 for coastal tiles, while U-TAE leads inland at 0.76 vs. 0.74 for 3DConv U-Net; overall coastal performance exceeds inland by ~0.03–0.04 F1 across all models. Adding GloFAS river discharge to U-TAE does not improve (and slightly degrades) the overall F1 from 0.77 to 0.76, suggesting that the two-stage hydrological-to-extent pipeline does not yet outperform direct end-to-end learning in this benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of work: rapid flood mapping (post-event change detection via Sentinel-1, as in Kuro Siwo and SNUNet) and river streamflow/runoff forecasting (LSTM-based models predicting discharge at gauges). It repurposes the Kuro Siwo rapid-mapping models to generate training labels, then reframes the problem as pre-event spatial forecasting of inundation extent at a lead time, a task the authors argue is absent from prior literature. The two-level pipeline combining coarse multi-temporal context (ERA5, ERA5-Land, HydroATLAS, GloFAS at 5–30 km) with fine local data (Sentinel-1, CopDEM30, HAND at 10 m) is a novel architectural framing, as no prior work models spatial relationships across such a resolution gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GFF dataset and its two-track benchmark (global vs. coastal-focused) fill a critical gap identified by the UN Early Warnings for All initiative: the absence of a common platform for evaluating ahead-of-time flood extent prediction. The finding that coastal regions are actually easier to forecast than inland ones (F1 0.80 vs. 0.76 for U-TAE) is somewhat counterintuitive given the compound drivers of coastal flooding, and the modest benefit of adding GloFAS river discharge suggests that explicit hydrological modeling may not be necessary for extent prediction. Key limitations include the use of reanalysis rather than reforecasts (which may overestimate operational performance), imperfect label generation from ensembled rapid-mapping models, and the binary flood/non-flood formulation that conflates transient inundation with semi-persistent water in wetlands.
