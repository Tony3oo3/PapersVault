## Problem

Standard SFT for LLM alignment treats demonstration data via supervised learning (behavior cloning), which suffers from distribution shift and cannot distinguish preferred from non-preferred continuations. The paper asks whether reward learning — typically reserved for the RLHF/preference-learning stage — can also benefit the SFT stage when only demonstration data is available, and answers affirmatively by showing that an inverse RL formulation over demonstration data yields a reward model that improves alignment quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243947/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only demonstration data (prompt + expert response pairs); no preference pairs are needed. In practice, the 'chosen' continuations from preference datasets (Anthropic-HH, UltraChat) serve as the demonstration set. The output is a fine-tuned LLM policy (IRFT, Algorithm 2) or jointly a reward model and policy (RFT, Algorithm 1). Unusually, the method never sees actual rejected data during training — instead, the current model generates synthetic continuations that serve as the negative/contrastive counterpart, so the contrastive signal is entirely self-generated.

## Method

The core formulation is a bilevel optimization: the upper level maximizes the likelihood of demonstration data under a policy π_θ, while the lower level defines π_θ as the KL-regularized optimal policy for a reward r(x,y;θ). A key reformulation (Lemma 3.1) converts this into a minimax problem where the reward is contrasted between expert continuations and self-generated continuations from the current policy. This yields two algorithms: RFT (Algorithm 1) explicitly trains a reward model and policy via alternating gradient updates, while IRFT (Algorithm 2) eliminates the explicit reward by showing (Lemma 3.2) that the IRL gradient equals the difference of log-likelihood ratios between expert and self-generated data, reducing to a contrastive loss h(log(π(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) − log(π(ỹ|x)/π_ref(ỹ|x))). A double-loop structure (generate every T outer iterations, train for K inner steps) balances generation cost against estimator bias, and the paper proves O(1/√(TK)) convergence to stationary points. Setting T=1 in IRFT recovers SPIN exactly, providing a theoretical foundation and convergence guarantee for that method.

## Results

On the HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard with zephyr-7b-sft-full, IRFT (Algorithm 2) with T=10 achieves a 61.03% average score, improving over the 59.48% base model by 1.55 percentage points, while SPIN (T=1) reaches 60.32%. On pythia-1.4b with Ultrachat200k, IRFT T=10 achieves 37.69% average versus 37.27% for both the base model and SFT. The implicit reward learned by IRFT also shows strong generalization: on the held-out Ultrafeedback preference dataset, IRFT T=5 achieves a 55.6% win-rate in distinguishing preferred from rejected continuations, compared to 42.6% for SFT and 42.8% for SPIN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard SFT (Ouyang et al., 2022), IRFT consistently outperforms: on zephyr-7b-sft-full, IRFT T=10 lifts the average from 59.48% to 61.03%, while SFT (in the LoRA setting, Table 6) only reaches 59.86%. Against SPIN (Chen et al., 2024), which is a special case of IRFT with T=1, the proposed method with more frequent generation (T=5–10) achieves further gains: IRFT T=10 scores 61.03% vs. SPIN's 60.32% on the 7B model, and 37.69% vs. 37.35% on pythia-1.4b. IRFT T=10 wins on 5 of 6 individual tasks (AI2_Arc, TruthfulQA, Winogrande, GSM8k, HellaSwag) compared to both baselines, but slightly underperforms the base model on MMLU (57.72% vs. 58.86%), a pattern shared with SPIN. The paper notes that the absolute gap over SPIN is modest because SPIN is algorithmically subsumed by IRFT; the primary contribution is the theoretical convergence guarantee and the IRL-based interpretation that unifies both methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the maximum-likelihood inverse reinforcement learning (ML-IRL) framework from the RL literature (Ziebart et al., 2008; Zeng et al., 2022) into the LLM alignment setting, formulating SFT as a bilevel optimization where a reward model and policy are learned jointly from demonstration data alone. A key conceptual contribution is showing that SPIN (Chen et al., 2024), originally motivated by a two-player game, is a special case (T=1) of the paper's implicit reward-learning algorithm (IRFT), which is derived from a single-agent IRL perspective. The paper also draws a structural parallel between the RLHF→DPO relationship and its own RFT→IRFT relationship, using the DPO identity (Rafailov et al., 2024) to eliminate the explicit reward model in the implicit variant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal IRL-based justification for contrastive fine-tuning methods like SPIN, supplying finite-time convergence guarantees that the original SPIN paper (framed as a two-player game) lacks. However, the empirical gains are modest (e.g., 59.48%→61.03% average on the Open LLM Leaderboard for Zephyr-7B), and the theory only guarantees convergence to a stationary point, leaving open what the limiting policy actually represents. The additional generation cost (comparable to one full policy-optimization pass) is non-trivial, and it remains unclear whether the approach scales to much larger models or more complex demonstration tasks. An open question is whether the implicit reward r = log(π_θ/π_ref) learned from demonstration data alone can serve as a reliable preference signal for downstream RLHF stages.
