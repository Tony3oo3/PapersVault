## Problem

Existing methods for explaining ML performance gaps across domains either provide only coarse aggregate decompositions (covariate shift vs. outcome shift) or detailed variable-level attributions that require knowledge of the full causal graph. No unified nonparametric framework simultaneously delivers both aggregate and detailed decompositions for both shift types without causal assumptions, and prior methods lack asymptotically valid confidence intervals for the attribution terms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244081/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired source and target domain datasets where features are partitioned into baseline variables W (e.g., demographics) and conditional covariates Z (e.g., diagnoses, income), along with a trained prediction model f and a loss function (e.g., 0-1 misclassification). It outputs a two-level hierarchy: aggregate terms Λ_W, Λ_Z, Λ_Y quantifying the contribution of each distributional factor, and variable-level Shapley values φ_{Z,j} and φ_{Y,j} attributing each aggregate term to individual features, all accompanied by confidence intervals. A key structural constraint is that the R²-based value functions cannot score partial shifts in the baseline variables W, limiting attribution to Z only.

## Method

The framework defines a unified scoring rule for candidate partial distribution shifts using a nonparametric R² measure: the value of an s-partial shift is the fraction of variance in the aggregate shift's stratum-specific performance difference explained by the partial shift's difference, making it a strictly proper scoring rule that does not require the causal DAG. Variable importance is then computed via Shapley attribution over these value functions, with computationally efficient estimation via subsampling linear in n (per Williamson and Feng). For the conditional outcome shift, the shifted distribution is parameterized through the source-domain risk Q = p₀(Y=1|W,Z) and the subset Z_s, and a binned variant of Q (B=20 bins) is used to restore pathwise differentiability. Debiased ML estimators are derived via one-step correction (AIPW-style) with influence functions, yielding asymptotically normal estimators that are multiply-robust to nuisance model misspecification, enabling valid confidence intervals for all decomposition terms.

## Results

In simulations, the debiased ML estimators achieve 90% CI coverage rates that converge to the nominal level with increasing sample size, while naive plug-in estimators fail to do so. In the conditional covariate shift simulation, only HDPD correctly identifies Z₁ as more important than Z₂ (R²-based value v_Z({1}) = 1), whereas MeanChange, Oaxaca-Blinder, and WuShift all incorrectly rank Z₂ higher. In the conditional outcome shift simulation, HDPD identifies Z₃ as the most important variable for explaining performance changes, while all other methods produce different (and misspecified) rankings. On real-world data, HDPD identifies 'Drug-induced or toxic-related condition' and 'Mental & substance use disorder in remission' as top covariate shift drivers in hospital readmission (accuracy drops 70%→53%), and annual income as the dominant outcome shift driver in insurance coverage prediction (accuracy drops 84%→66%); model revisions based on HDPD's top variables achieve the highest performance gains compared to revisions based on other methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares HDPD against six baselines: MeanChange (1−p-value for mean differences), Oaxaca-Blinder (linear logit model), WuShift [48] (Shapley over performance change from partial shifts), ParametricChange (logistic interaction coefficients), ParametricAcc (logistic on 0-1 loss), and RandomForestAcc (Shapley on a random forest predicting loss). For the conditional covariate shift, HDPD is the only method that correctly ranks Z₁ above Z₂; MeanChange ignores loss impact, Oaxaca-Blinder suffers from model misspecification, and WuShift scores partial shifts by their induced performance change without verifying they explain the aggregate shift. For the conditional outcome shift, no two methods agree on the top variable: HDPD ranks Z₃ first, ParametricChange ranks Z₁ first (largest logit-scale shift but not most important for loss), Oaxaca-Blinder ranks Z₃ second-to-last, and RandomForestAcc ranks Z₂ first (confusing predictive power with shift explanation). In terms of downstream utility, model revisions guided by HDPD's top variables achieve the highest performance gain in simulations (Table 3) and AUCs better or equal to RandomForestAcc on the ACS dataset (Table 4). HDPD also uniquely provides asymptotically valid confidence intervals for all decomposition terms, which none of the nonparametric baselines offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends two separate lines of work: aggregate decompositions of ML performance gaps (Cai et al., Liu et al., Wu et al.) and detailed variable-level attributions (Zhang et al., Quintas-Martinez et al., Oaxaca-Blinder). The key conceptual departure is the R²-based scoring rule for partial distribution shifts, which the authors prove is a proper scoring rule while showing (via a counterexample) that the prior loss-change-based scoring rule is not—meaning existing methods can assign higher importance to shifts that contradict the true causal structure. The estimation methodology builds on debiased ML / AIPW techniques (Chernozhukov et al.), but the derivation of a V-statistic-form estimator for the conditional outcome shift value function is new, as that estimand is not pathwise differentiable in its natural form and requires a binning trick to become amenable to semiparametric inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides ML deployment teams with a principled, nonparametric way to diagnose *why* a model's performance drops across populations and to target specific features for intervention, with statistically valid confidence intervals—something prior methods largely lack. Limitations include the requirement of overlapping covariate support, the inability to score partial shifts in the baseline variables W, and restriction to tabular data with a fixed loss function. Open questions the authors identify include extending to AUC and other performance metrics, handling unstructured data (images, text), and moving from explanation to optimal intervention design.
