## Problem

Existing medical NLP benchmarks evaluate LLMs on relatively simple tasks (short-text QA, NLI) that fail to capture the complexity of real clinical diagnosis, which requires reading long multi-section clinical notes, identifying relevant observations, and performing multi-step reasoning following established diagnostic guidelines. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a benchmark that evaluates both the diagnostic accuracy and the interpretability of LLMs' reasoning in a realistic clinical setting, revealing a significant gap between current LLMs and human physicians.

## Input / Output

The input is a clinical note R consisting of 6 fields from the SOAP format (chief complaint, history of present illness, past medical history, family history, physical exam, pertinent results; averaging ~1075 tokens) combined with a diagnostic knowledge graph that encodes premises, diagnoses, supporting edges, and procedural edges from clinical guidelines. The output is a predicted primary discharge diagnosis (PDD) from a set of 65 leaf-node diagnoses across 25 disease categories, accompanied by an entailment tree explanation composed of (observation, rationalization, diagnosis) triples that trace the step-by-step reasoning path from extracted text spans to the final diagnosis.

## Method

DiReCT is constructed from 511 MIMIC-IV clinical notes spanning 25 disease categories in 5 medical domains, each annotated by 9 physicians (verified by 3 senior experts) to extract observations and provide rationalizations forming a multi-evidence entailment tree. A diagnostic knowledge graph is built from existing clinical guidelines, where nodes are premises (medical statements) and diagnoses, connected by supporting edges (premise→diagnosis) and procedural edges (diagnosis→diagnosis) that form a tree per disease category. The baseline is a three-module AI-agent: a narrowing-down module (U) identifies the disease category, a perception module (W) extracts all relevant observations from the note, and a reasoning module (V) iteratively traverses the knowledge graph to derive the PDD with justifications at each step. Evaluation uses three metrics—diagnostic accuracy, observation completeness (Jaccard-like F1 over extracted observations), and explanation faithfulness (one-to-one matching of observation-rationalization-diagnosis triples)—and experiments across 7 LLMs (Llama3 8B/70B, Zephyr 7B, Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8×7B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) under three knowledge conditions (procedural graph only, full knowledge graph, or no external knowledge).

## Results

On DiReCT (511 clinical notes, 25 disease categories), GPT-4 turbo achieves the best overall performance with 61.0% diagnostic accuracy (Acc^diag) and 39.1% observation completeness (Obs^comp) under the procedural-graph setting (With G), while Llama3 70B matches it on category accuracy (82.2% vs. 80.4%) but lags on observation and explanation metrics. When the full knowledge graph K is supplied, GPT-4 turbo improves its explanation faithfulness (Exp^all) from 0.210 to 0.273, and Llama3 70B reaches the highest diagnostic accuracy at 65.2%. All models remain well below human physician performance, with the best Exp^all (0.273 for GPT-4 with K) indicating that even the strongest LLM aligns with doctors' reasoning on fewer than 30% of diagnostic steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks seven LLMs (Zephyr 7B, Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8×7B, Llama3 8B/70B, GPT-3.5 turbo, GPT-4 turbo) as backbones for an AI-agent pipeline. GPT-4 turbo dominates on observation completeness (0.391) and explanation faithfulness (0.210 with G; 0.273 with K), outperforming Llama3 70B by 11.2 and 8.6 points on Obs^comp and Exp^all respectively under the G setting. Llama3 70B achieves the highest category accuracy (0.822) and, with K, the highest diagnostic accuracy (0.652), but its lower Obs^pre (0.268) indicates it extracts many spurious observations. GPT-3.5 turbo sits in the middle, with Acc^diag of 0.455 (G) and 0.413 (K), roughly 15 points below GPT-4. Smaller open models (Zephyr 7B, Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8×7B) trail substantially, with Acc^diag below 0.31 and Exp^all below 0.07, highlighting a clear capability gap between frontier and mid-size LLMs on structured clinical reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the entailment tree explanation framework (Dalvi et al., 2021) from general NLP to multi-evidence medical diagnosis, combining it with a domain-specific diagnostic knowledge graph derived from clinical guidelines. It departs from prior medical reasoning benchmarks (MedMCQA, NLI4CT, NEJM CPC) that rely on short inputs and plain-text rationales by requiring models to process long SOAP-format clinical notes (~1074 tokens) and produce structured, step-by-step diagnostic reasoning aligned with a knowledge graph. The AI-agent baseline with narrowing-down, perception, and reasoning modules draws on recent LLM agent architectures (Xi et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023) but applies them to a structured graph-guided diagnostic flow rather than free-form reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DiReCT reveals a substantial and quantified gap between state-of-the-art LLMs (even GPT-4 at ~61% diagnostic accuracy, ~21–27% explanation faithfulness) and human physicians, underscoring that current models cannot yet serve as reliable diagnostic assistants in real clinical settings. The finding that diagnostic accuracy and explanation quality are dissociated—models can guess the right diagnosis without identifying the correct observations or rationales—highlights a critical interpretability failure mode. Key limitations include coverage of only 25 disease categories, a single primary discharge diagnosis per note, and the absence of inter-diagnostic relationships; future work must scale the dataset and develop models that can reason under incomplete observations, a common real-world condition.
