## Problem

Evaluating the quality of sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained for interpretability is difficult because no ground-truth collection of interpretable features exists for natural language models, forcing reliance on proxy metrics like sparsity and reconstruction fidelity. This paper addresses the gap by working in the setting of LMs trained on chess and Othello transcripts, where natural interpretable features (e.g., 'knight on e3', 'pin on the board') can be formally specified, enabling supervised metrics for SAE quality that prior proxy-based evaluations cannot provide.

## Input / Output

The SAEs consume 512-dimensional residual stream activations extracted after the post-MLP connection in layer 6 of 8-layer GPT models (8 heads) trained on 16M Lichess chess games in PGN format or 20M synthetically generated Othello transcripts. Beyond the standard sparse feature activations and reconstructed activations, the paper introduces two evaluation outputs: a coverage score (mean F1 of the best SAE feature as a binary classifier for each board state property) and a board reconstruction score (F1 of the predicted 64-square board state assembled from high-precision SAE features, evaluated on a held-out set of 1000 games separate from the 1000-game training set used to identify high-precision features).

## Method

The paper introduces p-annealing, a training technique that replaces the fixed L1 sparsity penalty with an L_p^p penalty where p is annealed from 1 (convex) down to 0.2 (non-convex, closer to the true L0 sparsity measure) over the course of training, based on the p-continuation method from compressive sensing. A coefficient annealing schedule adaptively rescales λ using a sliding window of recent feature activations to keep the effective sparsity penalty strength constant as p changes, preventing the penalty from becoming too weak at high p. The technique is architecture-agnostic and can be combined with the Gated SAE encoder, yielding a Gated-Annealed SAE. For evaluation, the paper defines two supervised metrics grounded in board state properties (BSPs): coverage, which for each BSP finds the SAE feature with the highest F1 as a thresholded binary classifier, and board reconstruction, which assembles a predicted board by OR-ing all SAE features that achieve ≥0.95 precision for each BSP on a training set, then scoring the full 64-square prediction against ground truth on a test set.

## Results

On chess, the best trained SAE achieves 0.48 coverage and 0.85 board reconstruction on board-state BSPs, while on Othello it reaches 0.52 coverage and 0.95 reconstruction. Linear probes substantially outperform SAEs, achieving 0.98/0.98 (chess) and 0.99/0.99 (Othello), indicating SAEs do not capture all board-state information. SAEs trained on a randomly initialized GPT perform near chance (0.11 coverage, 0.01 reconstruction on chess), confirming that SAE performance reflects genuine structure in the model's learned representations. Standard SAEs trained with p-annealing reach coverage scores comparable to Gated SAEs while using 50% less compute per forward pass.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four SAE training methods: Standard SAE with L1 penalty (Bricken et al. [9]), Standard SAE with p-annealing, Gated SAE (Rajamanoharan et al. [54]), and Gated-Annealed SAE. Standard SAEs trained with p-annealing consistently outperform those trained with a constant L1 penalty on both coverage and board reconstruction metrics, as well as on the unsupervised L0 and loss-recovered metrics. On coverage, p-annealed Standard SAEs reach scores comparable to Gated SAEs trained without p-annealing, effectively matching the more compute-intensive architecture. However, Gated SAEs still achieve higher loss-recovered scores than p-annealed Standard SAEs in some configurations (Figure 2), and the paper notes that Gated SAEs require 50% more compute per forward pass. The new coverage and board-reconstruction metrics reveal differences in SAE quality that standard proxy metrics miss: for example, SAEs with 8192 hidden dimensions outperform those with 4096 on the new metrics but appear equivalent on L0 and loss-recovered. Overall, p-annealing narrows the gap to Gated SAEs on interpretability-relevant metrics while retaining the simpler Standard architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of sparse dictionary learning for interpretability (Bricken et al., Rajamanoharan et al.) and non-convex sparse recovery from compressive sensing (Zheng et al.). It departs from the standard L1-penalty SAE training paradigm by introducing p-annealing, which progressively shifts the sparsity penalty from L1 toward L0, and from the Gated SAE architecture by showing that a simpler Standard SAE with p-annealing can match its performance at lower compute cost. The evaluation framework departs from prior reliance on proxy metrics (L0, loss recovered) and manual inspection by introducing supervised board-state metrics grounded in the chess and Othello domains, positioning the work between fully synthetic toy settings and natural language.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The coverage and board reconstruction metrics offer a more objective, quantitative signal of SAE quality than existing proxies, and the finding that they reveal quality differences (e.g., from larger expansion factors) invisible to standard metrics validates their utility. However, the metrics are confined to board games with researcher-specified BSPs, and generalizing this evaluation paradigm to natural language remains an open challenge. The p-annealing technique is a practical, composable improvement that practitioners can adopt immediately, but its benefits are demonstrated only on small 8-layer models with 512-dimensional hidden states, leaving open whether the gains persist at scale.
