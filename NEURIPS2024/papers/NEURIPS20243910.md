## Problem

Standard PAC learning assumes IID data and a fixed optimal hypothesis, but in deployed systems the data distribution and the learner's goals evolve over time, making the optimal hypothesis time-dependent. No first-principles theoretical framework existed for learning when the optimal predictor must change across future time steps, and existing approaches (distribution shift, continual learning, online learning) either minimize past error or assume known task boundaries, leaving them unable to prospectively predict arbitrarily far into the future.

## Input / Output

The learner receives a stream of (x_t, y_t) pairs up to time t and outputs a single neural network that, given any future time t' > t, produces a prediction by taking as input a sinusoidal time embedding φ(t') concatenated with the datum x_{t'} (a 1D/2D vector for synthetic tasks or a 28×28 / 32×32 image for MNIST/CIFAR-10). Unlike standard classifiers that return one fixed predictor, the output is effectively an infinite sequence of time-indexed predictors parameterized by one network, so the same weights yield different predictions at different future times.

## Method

Prospective ERM minimizes the expected cumulative future loss — the limsup of the average loss over all future time steps — rather than the instantaneous or past loss. The hypothesis class is a sequence of predictors h = (h_1, h_2, …) ⊂ (Y^X)^N, and the learner selects the sequence that minimizes an empirical estimate of the future limsup over the training data. In practice, this is implemented by feeding a sinusoidal time embedding φ(s) = (sin(ω_1 s), …, cos(ω_{d/2} s)) alongside the input x_s into an MLP or CNN, so a single network acts as a time-varying predictor h: N × X → Y. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that under a consistency condition on the hypothesis class and a uniform concentration condition on the limsup, Prospective ERM is a strong prospective learner whose risk converges to the prospective Bayes risk, and shows (Proposition 1) that standard time-agnostic ERM can fail to even weakly learn on non-stationary processes.

## Results

The paper evaluates prospective risk (expected cumulative future loss) over time rather than a single accuracy metric. On Scenario 2 tasks (independent but non-identically distributed data), Prospective ERM converges to Bayes risk (≈0) on synthetic data, MNIST, and CIFAR-10, while all baselines remain well above the chance level (0.5 for synthetic, 0.742 for MNIST/CIFAR-10). On Scenario 3 tasks (neither independent nor identically distributed, governed by a hierarchical hidden Markov model with no stationary distribution), Prospective ERM again approaches Bayes risk on all three datasets, whereas time-agnostic baselines fail to achieve low prospective risk. Experiments use 50,000 samples per sequence with prospective risk computed over the remaining time steps (up to 30,000 steps ahead).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Prospective ERM against three task-agnostic online/continual learning baselines: Follow-the-Leader [52], Online SGD, and Bayesian gradient descent [53]. All three baselines output a single time-agnostic hypothesis (the same predictor for all future times), which is fundamentally incompatible with prospective learning when the optimal hypothesis changes over time. On Scenario 2 (periodic task switching), Follow-the-Leader performs comparably to or slightly better than Online SGD and Bayesian GD, but all three exhibit risk spikes at task boundaries and fail to converge to Bayes risk. On Scenario 3 (hierarchical HMM with no stationary distribution), the failure is more pronounced: because no single time-agnostic predictor can capture the non-stationary dynamics, all baselines remain near or above chance level (0.742 for MNIST/CIFAR-10), while Prospective ERM—by incorporating a sinusoidal time embedding as an additional network input—converges to Bayes risk. The paper notes that in practice, tuning online learning methods (SGD, Bayesian GD) is difficult, and Follow-the-Leader's simpler batch-style retraining at each time step often matches or exceeds them.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces Prospective Learning as a new theoretical framework that explicitly departs from the IID assumption central to PAC learning, instead modeling data as an unknown stochastic process where the optimal hypothesis is time-dependent. The proof of the main consistency theorem (Theorem 1) builds directly on Hanneke's universal learning framework for general stochastic processes [38], while Scenario 4 draws on Q-learning [83] for settings where predictions influence future data. The paper carefully distinguishes itself from distribution shift, meta-learning, continual/lifelong learning, online learning, forecasting, and reinforcement learning (Appendix A), arguing that all of these either assume a fixed or finite number of optimal hypotheses, minimize past rather than future risk, or require knowledge of task boundaries. The practical implementation—encoding time via sinusoidal embeddings as an additional network input—borrows the positional encoding idea from Transformers [55].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a fundamental gap: deployed ML systems are brittle to distribution drift because the standard PAC/ERM framework ignores time entirely, and the paper formally demonstrates (Proposition 1) that time-agnostic ERM can fail to even weakly learn in non-stationary settings. The open questions are substantial: characterizing which stochastic processes are prospectively learnable, whether strong and weak prospective learnability are equivalent, and scaling the framework to real-world non-stationary data beyond the constructed synthetic/MNIST/CIFAR-10 tasks used here. The finding that current LLMs (Transformer architectures trained with auto-regressive losses) fail to learn prospectively (Appendix H) suggests a concrete limitation of a widely deployed architecture class, though the practical path to deploying prospective learners at scale remains unclear.
