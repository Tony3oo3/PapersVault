## Problem

The conditions under which transfer learning succeeds in a general cross-domain cross-task setting—where both feature distributions and label sets differ between tasks—remain poorly understood. Prior analytical bounds either assume identical features (cross-task only) or identical labels (cross-domain only), and existing score-based transferability estimation methods lack a theoretical grounding. Additionally, for large pre-trained models like CLIP or GPT, the source training task is unknown, making source-task-based analyses inapplicable.

## Input / Output

The method consumes encoder features (z-vectors) from a reference task with their labels, and encoder features from a target task where labels are optional. When target labels are unavailable, pseudo-labels are generated by applying the learned label-mapping matrix B to the reference classifier's predictions on target features. The output is a scalar task-relatedness score that serves as a provable upper bound on the cross-entropy loss after fine-tuning, computed in 3–4 minutes versus roughly a day for full end-to-end fine-tuning, enabling efficient model selection from a zoo of pre-trained encoders.

## Method

The paper proposes a chain of three transformations—class-prior reweighting (C), label-set mapping (B), and invertible linear feature transformation (A)—applied to the reference task distribution to approximate the target distribution. This yields Theorem 3, which upper-bounds target-task transferability as the sum of three interpretable terms: a re-weighted reference loss, a label mismatch term (conditional entropy between label distributions), and a distribution mismatch term (type-1 Wasserstein distance between the transformed reference and target joint distributions). An optimization problem (Eq. 4) jointly learns A, B, and C via mini-batch SGD with an optimal-transport coupling, and for computational efficiency the Wasserstein term is replaced by a mean-and-covariance matching objective (Eq. 5). The key design choice is anchoring the analysis to a reference task (e.g., ImageNet) rather than the unknown source task, and fixing B to a random permutation when the reference and target tasks share the same number of classes, which zeroes the label mismatch term and reduces the problem to learning only the feature transformation A.

## Results

Task-relatedness achieves an average Pearson correlation magnitude of 0.82 with end-to-end fine-tuning accuracy across Pets, DTD, and Aircraft target tasks (−0.77, −0.97, −0.72 respectively), outperforming most SbTE baselines in consistency. The method also achieves a small gap to actual transferability (cross-entropy loss) across architectures (ResNet-18/50/101/152, ViT, DistilRoBERTa) and pre-training methods (supervised, adversarial, SimCLR, MoCo, SwAV, MAE). Computationally, learning the transformations takes only 3–4 minutes versus nearly a day for full fine-tuning, and task-relatedness remains highly correlated with fine-tuning accuracy even when using pseudo-labels instead of true target labels (e.g., −0.76 vs −0.77 on Pets, −0.91 vs −0.97 on DTD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Task-relatedness (Ours) achieves the most consistent correlation across all three target tasks, with magnitudes of 0.72–0.97, whereas several baselines show severe instability: NCE drops to −0.19 on DTD, LogMe flips to −0.60 on Aircraft, and H-Score drops to −0.80 on Aircraft (all in the wrong direction for their respective metrics). Leep achieves the highest average correlation (0.90) but requires labeled target data and cannot be applied in the unsupervised setting. PACTran, the only other negative-correlation metric, achieves an average of only −0.52 and degrades sharply on Aircraft (0.11). OT-NCE and OTCE (which combine Wasserstein distance with conditional entropy) achieve moderate averages (0.76) but are less consistent than Ours. Crucially, task-relatedness is the only metric that maintains high correlation (−0.76 on Pets, −0.91 on DTD) even when computed with pseudo-labels instead of true target labels, enabling unsupervised transferability estimation—a capability none of the compared baselines offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the domain adaptation analysis lineage (Ben-David et al., Mansour et al.) and the transferability bound of Tran et al. [56] from restrictive settings (same features or same labels) to the general cross-domain cross-task setting where both feature distributions and label sets differ. The key conceptual departure is analyzing transferability relative to a *reference* task rather than the source task, which is practical since the pre-training data of large models (CLIP, GPT) is unknown. The three-transformation model (class-prior, label-set, feature-space) decomposes the bound into interpretable terms, and the use of Wasserstein distance for the distribution mismatch term connects to optimal transport-based domain adaptation work, while the conditional entropy term generalizes the NCE score of Tran et al. Unlike prior SbTE metrics (LogMe, Leep, OTCE, PACTran), this provides a provable upper bound rather than an empirically correlated score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical upper bound on transferability in a general cross-domain cross-task setting, giving practitioners an interpretable diagnostic for *why* transfer succeeds or fails (prior mismatch, label mismatch, distribution mismatch). The practical finding that task-relatedness can be estimated without target labels (via pseudo-labels from the reference classifier) opens the door to unsupervised transferability estimation, which most SbTE methods cannot handle. Limitations include the reliance on cross-entropy loss (not 0-1 loss), the difficulty of computing Wasserstein distance in high-dimensional spaces (mitigated by the mean/covariance approximation), and the Lipschitz constraint that restricts the hypothesis class. Open questions include whether the bound tightens with non-linear feature transformations and whether the framework extends to non-classification tasks.
