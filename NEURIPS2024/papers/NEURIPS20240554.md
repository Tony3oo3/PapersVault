## Problem

Traditional community detection methods like Infomap rely on custom heuristic search algorithms to optimise objective functions, making them difficult to combine with node features or integrate into end-to-end deep learning pipelines. Conversely, recent GNN-based graph clustering methods lack principled model selection and tend to overfit, reporting far more communities than the data supports. The paper bridges this gap by making the map equation—a well-established information-theoretic objective grounded in the minimum description length principle—differentiable and optimisable via gradient descent with arbitrary neural network architectures.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph's adjacency matrix A (n×n) and an optional node feature matrix X (n×d); when no features are available, the adjacency matrix itself serves as the feature input. It produces a soft cluster assignment matrix S (n×s) via a GNN followed by softmax, from which hard communities are extracted by assigning each node to its strongest cluster. The number of communities is inferred data-drivenly up to a user-specified maximum s, rather than being fixed a priori, and the codelength L serves as both the training loss and a model-selection criterion.

## Method

Neuromap expresses the map equation in differentiable tensor form by introducing a soft cluster assignment matrix S = softmax(GNN_θ(A, X)) and computing the cluster-level flow C = S^T F S, where F is the node-level flow matrix derived from smart teleportation and the graph's transition matrix. The codelength L(A, S) is then assembled in closed form from C's diagonal, row sums, and column sums, enabling end-to-end gradient-based optimisation of the GNN parameters θ. Because the map equation follows the MDL principle, it inherently balances model complexity against data fit, automatically selecting the number of communities without explicit regularisation or cross-validation. The formulation is architecture-agnostic—any GNN (GCN, GIN, SAGE) or even a simple MLP can be used—and a small constant ε is added to S before backpropagation to ensure differentiability of the logarithmic terms.

## Results

On ten real-world networks (s=√n), Neuromap achieves the best or tied-best AMI in 7 out of 10 datasets, with highlights including 78.1% AMI on Coauthor CS (MLP), 65.0% on Amazon Photo (GCN), and 46.1% on Cora (GIN). On 320 synthetic LFR networks (s=n), Neuromap with SAGE achieves the highest average AMI across all mixing parameters, outperforming Infomap and all GNN-based baselines except NOCD with GIN/GCN on directed networks. A key differentiator is that Neuromap detects close to the ground-truth number of communities (e.g., ~7 on Cora, ~5 on Coauthor Physics) while baselines like DMoN, MinCut, and Ortho routinely report 100–400+ communities, indicating severe overfitting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Neuromap is compared against five GNN-based clustering methods (DMoN, NOCD, DiffPool, MinCut, Ortho) and the traditional Infomap algorithm. Against NOCD (the strongest baseline), Neuromap wins significantly on Coauthor CS (78.1 vs. 73.9, p=4.4e-10) and PubMed (24.6 vs. 23.7, p=0.05), ties on Cora, PC, Photo, Wiki CS, and ogb-arxiv, but loses on CiteSeer (23.1 vs. 34.8), Physics (56.5 vs. 62.4 via DiffPool), and Cora ML (39.1 vs. 46.5). Against DMoN, MinCut, and Ortho, Neuromap consistently outperforms across nearly all datasets, with the gap widening on larger networks (e.g., ogb-arxiv: 43.6 vs. 38.4/33.9/34.3). Against Infomap, Neuromap significantly outperforms on 9 of 10 datasets (p<0.05), demonstrating that incorporating node features via GNNs substantially improves community quality while also reducing the number of detected communities from hundreds (Infomap) to near ground-truth levels. On synthetic LFR networks, Neuromap (SAGE) outperforms all baselines in AMI across most mixing parameters, while baselines like MinCut fail entirely on directed networks for μ>0.3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: information-theoretic community detection (the map equation and its Infomap optimizer) and deep graph clustering/pooling with GNNs. While recent works have begun incorporating network-science objectives like modularity, minimum cuts, and the Bernoulli-Poisson model into GNN loss functions, the map equation had not been adapted for differentiable optimization. The key technical contribution is a tensor-form reformulation of the map equation with soft cluster assignments, making it compatible with any neural network architecture and enabling end-to-end gradient-based training without custom search heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MDL principle embedded in the map equation provides automatic, data-driven model selection for the number of communities without explicit regularization, addressing a practical weakness of GNN-based clustering methods that tend to over-partition when not artificially constrained. The paper's empirical finding that DMoN, MinCut, and Ortho report far more communities than ground truth when s is set generously (a condition hidden in prior evaluations that fixed s to small constants) is a useful caution for practitioners. Open questions include extending to higher-order and multi-level community structures, integrating the method into graph pooling pipelines, and understanding why simpler architectures (linear, MLP) sometimes match GNNs, suggesting the flow-based loss captures global structure that message-passing may not add.
