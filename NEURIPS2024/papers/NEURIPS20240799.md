## Problem

Inorganic retrosynthesis planning is significantly underexplored compared to its organic counterpart due to the computational infeasibility of atomic-structure-based methods and the absence of generalized synthesis mechanisms. The existing retrieval-based approach (He et al.) directly copies precursor labels from retrieved reference materials, which constrains the model to known recipes and prevents discovery of novel synthetic pathways. Additionally, prior methods neglect the well-established thermodynamic domain expertise—namely, that more negative Gibbs free energy (ΔG) between target and precursors indicates higher synthesis feasibility—when selecting which reference materials to consult.

## Input / Output

The method takes the chemical composition of a target inorganic material, encoded as a fully connected composition graph whose nodes are the constituent elements (initialized with Matscholar embeddings) and whose edges form a complete graph, along with a knowledge base of previously synthesized materials and their precursor sets. The output is a binary label vector of length l (the number of predefined precursors), where each entry indicates whether that precursor is required for synthesizing the target. Retrieval selects K=3 reference materials per target via two complementary scoring mechanisms: cosine similarity on composition-vector embeddings (MPC) and computed ΔG values (NRE), with the NRE retriever restricted to precursors sharing elements with the target plus common elements (C, H, O, N).

## Method

Retrieval-Retro replaces the explicit use of reference-material precursor labels with an implicit extraction pipeline built on two attention stages. First, a self-attention layer (parameter-free, using the enhanced reference representations directly as Q/K/V) refines each reference material's representation by considering inter-reference relationships, after each reference is modulated by concatenation with the target representation. Second, a cross-attention layer (also parameter-free) lets the target representation attend over the enhanced reference set, extracting precursor-relevant information without ever seeing the reference precursors as explicit labels. Two complementary retrievers feed this pipeline: the MPC retriever (a masked-precursor-completion model trained with cross-attention to reconstruct precursor vectors) captures precursor co-occurrence patterns, while the NRE retriever (a GNN pre-trained on 80K DFT formation energies from Materials Project and fine-tuned on 1,637 experimental SGTE entries) retrieves materials whose precursor sets yield the most negative ΔG relative to the target. The final prediction concatenates the target, MPC-conditioned, and NRE-conditioned representations through an MLP classifier trained with binary cross-entropy loss.

## Results

On the more challenging Year Split (training on pre-2014 papers, testing on 2017–2020), Retrieval-Retro achieves 61.16% Top-1, 67.18% Top-5, and 69.45% Top-10 exact-match accuracy, along with 60.97% Macro Recall. On the Random Split, it reaches 79.81% Top-1, 84.46% Top-5, and 85.70% Top-10 accuracy with 74.61% Macro Recall. The method consistently outperforms all baselines across both splits, with the largest margins observed in the Year Split setting, which better reflects real-world material discovery conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Retrieval-Retro outperforms all four external baselines across both evaluation splits. Against He et al. [12], the closest prior retrieval-based method, Retrieval-Retro gains 16.13 points in Top-1 accuracy on the Year Split (61.16% vs. 45.03%), demonstrating that implicit precursor extraction via attention layers substantially surpasses the explicit precursor conditioning used by He et al. Against ElemwiseRetro [18], which formulates precursor prediction as multi-class classification over curated templates, Retrieval-Retro leads by 7.71 points in Top-1 (Year Split) and 2.58 points in Top-1 (Random Split). Against the composition-based representation learners Roost [10] and CrabNet [41], Retrieval-Retro achieves 6.78 and 4.01 point improvements in Top-1 accuracy on the Year Split, respectively, confirming that retrieval-augmented implicit extraction provides a meaningful advantage over purely composition-driven encoders. The gap is most pronounced in the Year Split, where the model must generalize to entirely unseen synthesis recipes, highlighting Retrieval-Retro's strength in discovering novel precursor sets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the retrieval-based inorganic retrosynthesis framework of He et al. [12], which retrieves reference materials via a Masked Precursor Completion (MPC) retriever and explicitly incorporates their precursor sets into prediction. The key departure is replacing this explicit precursor conditioning with implicit extraction through self-attention (to enhance reference representations) and cross-attention (to extract precursor-relevant information), giving the model flexibility to go beyond known recipes. Additionally, the paper introduces a Neural Reaction Energy (NRE) retriever that incorporates thermodynamic domain expertise (Gibbs free energy) into material retrieval, adopting the pre-train-on-DFT-then-fine-tune-on-experimental-data strategy from Jha et al. [16] for composition-based formation energy prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to discover novel synthesis recipes with entirely new precursors is critical for accelerating materials discovery and reducing synthesis costs, yet prior retrieval-based methods are constrained to the precursor sets of retrieved references. The finding that explicit precursor use actually degrades performance on novel recipes (new case) while implicit extraction consistently improves both subset and new cases provides a principled design guideline for retrieval-augmented retrosynthesis. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-step synthesis planning, whether incorporating synthesis conditions (temperature, pressure, time) would further improve predictions, and how the method scales as the knowledge base of synthesis recipes grows.
