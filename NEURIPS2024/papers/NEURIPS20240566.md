## Problem

Traditional fixed-confidence best arm identification algorithms (Track-and-Stop, Top-Two) are designed for the frequentist setting where the bandit model is fixed in advance. This paper shows that when the bandit model is drawn from a known prior (Bayesian setting), these algorithms have infinite expected stopping time because they over-invest in the rare but extremely hard narrow-gap instances. The paper establishes that the Bayesian FC-BAI problem is fundamentally different from the frequentist one and requires a new algorithmic approach.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a confidence level δ, a known Gaussian prior H = (H₁,…,H_k) on the arm means (each H_i = N(m_i, ξ_i²)), and known arm variances σ_i². It sequentially selects arms, observes scalar Gaussian rewards, and outputs a recommended arm J along with a random stopping time τ. Unusually, the correctness guarantee is Bayesian (marginalized over the prior: PoE ≤ δ) rather than frequentist (uniform over all instances), and the indifference-zone threshold Δ₀ = δ/(4L(H)) is automatically derived from the prior geometry rather than user-specified.

## Method

The algorithm is a successive elimination procedure with an early-stopping indifference criterion. In each round, all arms in the active set are pulled once, and UCB/LCB confidence bounds (with a 1/N_i(t)·log(1/N_i(t)) width) are computed; arms whose UCB falls below the maximum LCB are eliminated. The key innovation is the stopping rule: if the gap between the best UCB and best LCB among remaining arms drops below Δ₀ = δ/(4L(H)), the algorithm halts and uniformly samples a recommendation from the active set, preventing over-commitment on narrow-gap instances. The theoretical core is the Volume Lemma, which shows that the prior mass of instances with suboptimality gap ≤ Δ is proportional to L(H)·Δ for small Δ, yielding a lower bound of Ω(L(H)²/δ) and an upper bound of O(L(H)²/δ · log(L(H)/δ)) on the expected stopping time.

## Results

The paper establishes a lower bound of Ω(L(H)²/δ) on the expected stopping time for any Bayesian δ-correct algorithm, and provides an algorithm (Successive Elimination with Early-Stopping) achieving an upper bound of O(σ_max² · L(H)²/δ · log(L(H)/δ)), matching up to a logarithmic factor. Empirically, on a 2-armed Gaussian bandit with standard Gaussian prior (δ=0.1, N=1000 runs), Algorithm 1 achieves an average stopping time of 1.06×10⁴ and maximum of 2.35×10⁵, compared to 1.56×10⁵ (avg) and 1.09×10⁸ (max) for TTTS, and 1.95×10⁵ (avg) and 1.13×10⁸ (max) for TTUCB. The theoretical result (Theorem 2) shows that any frequentist δ-correct algorithm has infinite expected stopping time in the Bayesian setting, explaining the extreme max stopping times observed for top-two baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its Bayesian elimination algorithm against two frequentist top-two baselines: Top-Two Thompson Sampling (TTTS, Russo 2016) and Top-Two UCB (TTUCB, Jourdan & Degenne 2022). Algorithm 1 achieves a ~15× reduction in average stopping time over TTTS (1.06×10⁴ vs 1.56×10⁵) and ~18× over TTUCB (1.06×10⁴ vs 1.95×10⁵). The maximum stopping time improvement is even more dramatic: ~464× over TTTS (2.35×10⁵ vs 1.09×10⁸) and ~481× over TTUCB (2.35×10⁵ vs 1.13×10⁸), reflecting the theoretical result that frequentist algorithms have infinite expected stopping time in the Bayesian setting. The trade-off is a slightly higher error rate: Algorithm 1 reports 1.5% misidentification versus 0.5% for TTTS and 0% for TTUCB, though all remain well within the δ=0.1 budget. The paper also notes that Track-and-Stop would exhibit similar divergence behavior, as its expected stopping time is at least half that of TTTS/TTUCB for small δ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fixed-confidence best arm identification (FC-BAI) problem from the well-studied frequentist setting to the Bayesian setting, where the bandit model is drawn from a known prior. It builds on the information-theoretic lower bound techniques of Kaufmann et al. (2016a) and the successive elimination framework of Even-Dar et al. (2006), but departs fundamentally by showing that the frequentist-optimal algorithms (Track-and-Stop, Top-Two) are not merely suboptimal but have infinite expected stopping time under Bayesian marginalization. The key new ingredient is the Volume Lemma, which characterizes how the prior mass concentrates near small suboptimality gaps, and an indifference-zone-style early stopping criterion derived from the prior rather than a user-specified ε.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that all frequentist δ-correct algorithms diverge in expectation under a Bayesian prior is a clean negative result that clarifies a fundamental distinction between the two statistical paradigms for sequential decision-making. The matching lower and upper bounds (up to a log factor) establish the correct Bayesian sample complexity order Ω(L(H)²/δ), which is structurally different from the frequentist Ω(log(1/δ)/Δ²). Open questions include closing the logarithmic gap, extending beyond Gaussian bandits to general exponential families, handling misspecified priors, and adapting the indifference-zone strategy to other algorithm families like Track-and-Stop or Top-Two in the Bayesian setting.
