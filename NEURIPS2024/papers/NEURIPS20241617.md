## Problem

Current AI alignment methods (SFT, RLHF) rely on human demonstrations or judgments, inherently upper-bounding system capabilities at human level. This paper tackles the question of how to improve AI on hard reasoning tasks (e.g., level 4–5 MATH) using only human supervision on easier tasks (e.g., level 1–3), a setting the authors term easy-to-hard generalization. Prior work shows that SFT or ICL on easy tasks alone fails to transfer to hard tasks, leaving a gap in scalable oversight methods that do not require human labels on the harder problems.

## Input / Output

The generator (Llemma-7b/34b for math, Code Llama-7b/34b for coding) takes a problem question as input and produces a chain-of-thought solution ending in a final answer. In the easy-to-hard setup, training data consists only of easy problems (MATH levels 1–3 or APPS Introductory) with full solutions and process labels, while hard problems (levels 4–5 or Interview/Competition) appear only as questions during evaluation. The reward models output per-step correctness probabilities (PRM), a single final-answer score (ORM), or both simultaneously (OPRM), which are then aggregated via min (for best-of-n) or product (for weighted voting) into a solution-level score.

## Method

The paper's core insight is that evaluation generalizes from easy to hard better than generation does, so a reward model trained solely on easy tasks can effectively score solutions to hard tasks. The authors introduce the Outcome & Process Reward Model (OPRM), which jointly predicts step-level correctness (like a PRM) and final-answer correctness (like an ORM) by training on mixed ORM and PRM data. The easy-trained evaluator is then used to improve the generator in two ways: (1) re-ranking via weighted voting or best-of-n over sampled solutions, and (2) reinforcement learning (PPO, iterative DPO, or ReST-EM) where the evaluator provides step-by-step reward signals. Notably, a 7b PPO model trained with human supervision only on level 1–3 problems achieves 34.0% on MATH500, surpassing the previous RL SOTA (33.0%) that used supervision across all levels, and a 34b reranking@1024 setup reaches 52.5%.

## Results

On MATH500, the paper's 7b RL model trained with human supervision only on easy (Level 1-3) problems using PRM rewards achieves 34.0% accuracy, outperforming the previous RL SOTA (33.0%) that was trained across all difficulty levels. The 34b model with OPRM reranking at 1024 samples reaches 52.5% accuracy on MATH500. On the coding domain (APPS), an ORM trained only on easy (Introductory) problems improves Best-of-16 accuracy from 15.7% (greedy) to 18.0% (7b) and 20.1% to 26.6% (34b) on the full test set, with the largest gains on hard (Competition) problems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's headline result (34.0% on MATH500 with MetaMath-7b + easy-only PRM rewards) outperforms the previous RL SOTA from Math-Shepherd [75] (33.0%), which required training across all difficulty levels (L1-5). Among RL algorithms, PPO and Iterative DPO significantly outperform ReST-EM (34.0% vs. 31.6% on MetaMath; 29.4% vs. 22.4% on PRM800K), with PPO showing less sensitivity to SFT initialization quality. On PRM800K, PPO with all questions but easy-only PRM rewards (29.4%) beats PPO with easy questions only (27.4%), suggesting that exposure to hard problem statements during RL helps even when rewards come solely from the easy-trained evaluator. Notably, easy-only training with PRM rewards matches or exceeds full-data training (e.g., Iterative DPO easy: 34.0% vs. DPO all: 29.2% on MetaMath), demonstrating that the easy-to-hard evaluator provides more effective supervision than direct final-answer labels on hard tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the easy-to-hard generalization literature (Hase et al. 2024, Swayamdipta et al. 2020) by introducing an evaluator-mediated approach: rather than hoping SFT/ICL on easy tasks directly transfers to hard tasks, it trains process- and outcome-supervised reward models on easy data and uses them to re-rank or RL-train generators on hard problems. It builds on the PRM/ORM framework of Lightman et al. and Uesato et al., but departs from their IID setting by explicitly studying distribution shift across difficulty levels. The scalable oversight framing (Bowman et al., Leike et al.) distinguishes this from weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al.), where supervision is unreliable rather than absent on hard tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, tested pathway for improving AI performance on tasks beyond human supervision capability, addressing a core challenge in scalable oversight. The counterintuitive finding that easy-only RL training outperforms full-dataset training suggests that reliable supervision on simpler sub-problems is more valuable than noisy supervision on harder ones—a principle that could reshape how alignment data is curated. Limitations include focus on bounded domains (MATH, APPS) with clear difficulty taxonomies, relatively small model sizes (7b–34b), and the open question of whether this approach transfers to tasks without natural difficulty levels or where the easy-to-hard gap is much larger.
