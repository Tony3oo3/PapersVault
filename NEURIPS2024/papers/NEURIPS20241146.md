## Problem

Empirical studies show that attention layers can implement qualitatively different algorithmic mechanisms (positional vs. semantic), but no tight theoretical characterization exists for the conditions under which a given mechanism emerges during training. Previous theoretical analyses of attention either used non-tight bounds that cannot capture sharp transitions, or considered frozen/linear settings that preclude emergent phenomena. This paper provides the first rigorous identification of a phase transition between positional and semantic learning in a solvable dot-product attention model.

## Input / Output

The model consumes sequences of L i.i.d. Gaussian tokens (each d-dimensional with position-dependent covariance Σ_ℓ) augmented by fixed positional encodings p_ℓ, and is trained via empirical risk minimization against a teacher target that mixes tokens through a data-dependent attention matrix with tunable semantic/positional composition ω. The output is a trained tied low-rank weight matrix Q̂ characterized by finite-dimensional summary statistics (overlap θ with teacher weights, overlap m with positional embeddings), from which closed-form expressions for test MSE and training loss are derived. The analysis operates in the joint limit d, n → ∞ with α = n/d fixed, sentence length L and ranks r_s, r_t held O(1).

## Method

The paper analyzes a single tied low-rank dot-product attention layer (Q = K, value = identity) trained by ERM with ℓ₂ regularization, and maps the non-convex optimization to a Generalized Approximate Message Passing (GAMP) algorithm whose fixed points correspond to critical points of the empirical loss. In the high-dimensional limit, the state evolution equations reduce to a finite-dimensional system of self-consistent equations (Result 4.2) involving summary statistics {q_ℓ, V_ℓ, m_ℓ, θ_ℓ} and a low-dimensional Moreau-envelope minimization over the attention score matrix. For rank-one dot-product attention with a mixed positional/semantic teacher, the system admits two stable fixed points: a positional solution (θ=0, m>0) and a semantic solution (θ>0, m=0), and the global minimum switches between them at a critical sample complexity α_c(ω), constituting a sharp phase transition. The dot-product attention is further compared to a purely positional dense linear baseline, showing it outperforms the baseline only after crossing the semantic threshold α_l ≥ α_c.

## Results

The paper provides a tight closed-form asymptotic characterization (Result 4.2) of the global minimum of the non-convex empirical loss for a tied low-rank dot-product attention layer, revealing a sharp phase transition in sample complexity α = n/d between a positional mechanism (θ=0, m>0) and a semantic mechanism (θ>0, m=0). The critical threshold α_c(ω) grows with the positionality parameter ω of the target, meaning more data is needed to learn semantic attention when the task is more positional. In the concrete rank-1, L=2 setup (ω=0.3, σ²=0.25), the phase transition is verified experimentally at d=1000 with gradient descent (T=5000 epochs, η=0.15, 24 instances), showing good agreement between theory and practice. The dot-product attention outperforms a purely positional linear baseline only in the semantic regime, above a second threshold α_l ≥ α_c.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the dot-product attention (13) and a dense linear positional baseline f_W(x) = W·x (15), which can only implement positional mechanisms. In the positional regime (α < α_c), where the dot-product attention relies on a positional mechanism (θ=0, m>0), it is outperformed by the linear baseline (ε_g > ε_g^lin). In the semantic regime (α > α_c), the dot-product attention learns the semantic mechanism and eventually outperforms the linear baseline above a threshold α_l ≥ α_c, highlighting the architectural advantage of the dot-product parametrization for targets with semantic content. The theoretical predictions from Result 4.2 and Result 5.1 are validated against full-batch gradient descent experiments (d=1000, 24 instances), showing close agreement in training loss, overlap statistics (θ, m), and test MSE. Additionally, in the histogram counting task (Appendix A), both the positional and semantic local minima achieve >99.8% test accuracy, demonstrating that qualitatively different algorithmic mechanisms can coexist in the loss landscape of a practical transformer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the statistical physics framework for characterizing phase transitions in neural networks—previously established for feed-forward architectures (Sompolinsky, Györgyi, Barbier et al.)—to the setting of self-attention layers. Technically, it builds on the Generalized Approximate Message Passing (GAMP) framework of Rangan et al. and the high-dimensional GLM asymptotics of Loureiro et al., mapping the non-convex attention learning problem to a solvable fixed-point system. It departs from prior attention theory (Jelassi et al., Fu et al., Sahiner et al.) which either used non-tight convergence bounds, fixed queries/keys, or linearized the attention, none of which could capture sharp transitions between qualitatively different mechanisms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical demonstration that the emergence of semantic attention in a dot-product layer is a sharp phase transition in sample complexity rather than a smooth improvement, providing a concrete mechanism for the 'emergent abilities' observed empirically in language models. The finding that the more expressive dot-product architecture is actually outperformed by a simple linear positional baseline below the critical sample complexity highlights that architectural expressivity alone is insufficient without adequate data. Key limitations include the tied low-rank weights, single head, Gaussian 1-gram data, and the high-dimensional asymptotic setting; open questions include whether gradient descent from random initialization reliably finds the global minimum, and whether the transition persists in more realistic multi-head, multi-layer, and correlated-data settings.
