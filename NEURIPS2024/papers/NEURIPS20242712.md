## Problem

Grid cells in the medial entorhinal cortex are known to map not only physical space but also abstract cognitive domains (auditory frequency, visual feature spaces, conceptual spaces), yet the mechanism by which the brain extracts a general, state-independent notion of velocity from high-dimensional sensory inputs in these non-spatial environments remains unexplained. Existing cognitive mapping models (TEM, successor representations, CSCG) build new state representations and transition structures for each domain from scratch, failing to explain how the prestructured grid cell scaffold can be efficiently reused across vastly different modalities. The prerequisite challenge is extracting self-consistent, low-dimensional velocity signals from arbitrary abstract spaces without prior knowledge of the coordinate system or the intrinsic dimensionality of transitions.

## Input / Output

The model consumes pairs of consecutive high-dimensional states (16×16 or 32×12 pixel images for visual domains, 1×100 vectors for the audio frequency-modulation domain) sampled from procedurally generated trajectories, and is trained on triplets (i_t, i_{t+1}, i_{t+2}) where the first two frames yield a velocity used to predict the unseen third frame. The primary output is a low-dimensional velocity vector (1D, 2D, or 3D, matching the intrinsic transition dimensionality of the domain) that is state-independent and geometrically self-consistent. When these velocities are integrated through a synthetic continuous-attractor grid cell module, the system produces hexagonal grid firing fields, demonstrating that the same grid cell population can be reused across all five tested domains while preserving cell-cell correlation structure.

## Method

The architecture is an encoder-decoder pair of MLPs: the encoder f maps two consecutive states to a low-dimensional velocity latent, and the decoder g applies that velocity to the current state to predict the next unseen state. Training is entirely self-supervised via two critical loss terms: a next-state prediction loss (ensuring the velocity is decodable and independent of the specific states traversed) and a loop-closure loss (requiring that the sum of estimated velocities along any closed-loop trajectory equals zero, a computation performed by a downstream neural integrator analogous to the grid cell circuit). Two auxiliary losses—shortcut estimation (testing decoder generalization over multi-step displacements) and isotropy (constraining uniform scaling near zero velocity)—further refine the solution space. A key theoretical result proves that the loop-closure loss alone forces the velocity mapping to be a linear function of ground-truth velocities, thereby inducing a global metric structure compatible with grid cell path integration. The framework requires no a priori knowledge of the velocity dimensionality; when the latent space is over-parameterized, the model automatically collapses onto the correct low-dimensional subspace (PCA captures >97% variance in the true number of dimensions), and it outperforms PCA, Isomap, UMAP, autoencoders, and MCNet by orders of magnitude on all five domains.

## Results

The paper evaluates velocity estimation quality using a normalized mean-squared error (after best-fit linear alignment to ground-truth velocities) across five procedurally generated abstract cognitive domains. The proposed self-supervised model achieves errors of 0.02–0.07 across all tasks, dramatically outperforming all baselines. For example, on the 2D Stretchy Blob task the model reaches 0.05±0.01 versus 0.42 (Isomap), 0.63 (PCA), 0.79 (UMAP), 1.95 (MCNet), and ~1590 (Autoencoder). Even on the simplest 1D Frequency Modulation task, where one might expect dimensionality reduction to succeed, the model achieves 0.02±0.02 while all baselines plateau near 2.00.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all four baselines across every task, with errors roughly 3–30× lower than the best competing method. MCNet (Villegas et al., 2017), a deep motion/content decomposition network designed for video prediction, consistently produces errors near 2.0 regardless of task dimensionality, indicating it fails to extract a coherent low-dimensional velocity signal. The VAE-based autoencoder (Kingma & Welling, 2013) performs worst on the Stretchy tasks (errors of ~1590 and ~266) due to its stochastic bottleneck, but recovers to ~2.0 on Moving Blobs and Freq. Modulation. Among classical dimensionality reduction methods, Isomap (Tenenbaum et al., 2000) and UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018) are the closest competitors, achieving 0.21–0.66 on the 2D/3D Stretchy tasks, yet they still lag the proposed model by a factor of 3–10 and completely fail on the 1D Frequency Modulation task (error ≈ 2.00). PCA, while not in the formal comparison set, also struggles (0.21–1.97), confirming that global distance-preserving embeddings cannot recover the local tangent-space velocity structure that the self-supervised loop-closure constraint captures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior cognitive mapping models (TEM, successor representations, CSCG) that jointly learn state representations and transition structures, instead proposing a velocity-extraction-first decomposition: first infer a state-independent, low-dimensional velocity signal, then feed it to a prestructured continuous attractor grid cell circuit (Burak & Fiete, 2009) for path integration. It also distinguishes itself from standard dimensionality reduction (PCA, Isomap, UMAP) by learning the tangent-space velocity structure between successive states rather than preserving pairwise distances, and from motion-prediction networks (MCNet) by enforcing a geometric loop-closure constraint that provably induces linearity in the estimated velocity space. The self-supervised formulation—requiring no ground-truth coordinates, no known dimensionality, and no explicit action labels—repurposes the grid cell integrator as both a training signal (loop-closure loss) and a downstream decoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of how a single grid cell module can flexibly map multiple abstract cognitive domains, making the testable prediction that cell-cell co-activity patterns should be preserved across spatial and non-spatial tasks. Key limitations include the assumption of commuting (Euclidean) velocity vectors, the reliance on auxiliary losses (isotropy, shortcut) with weaker biological grounding, and validation exclusively on small synthetic domains rather than naturalistic stimuli. Open questions include whether the framework scales to high-dimensional naturalistic data, how non-Euclidean spaces (e.g., family trees) can be handled beyond embedding tricks, and which brain regions actually generate the extracted velocity signals. For ML, the loop-closure constraint offers a general self-supervised principle for learning metric-consistent displacement operators, potentially applicable to transfer learning and data-efficient navigation in robotics.
