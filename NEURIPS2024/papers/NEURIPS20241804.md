## Problem

Whether causal language modeling (next-token prediction) with Transformers can genuinely learn complex search and reasoning, or merely performs brittle pattern matching, remains debated. This paper tackles the question by studying a precise synthetic setting—solving Sudoku and Zebra puzzles—where the model must both search over cells to decide which to fill next and apply multi-step logical strategies to determine values, a task that frontier LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5 Pro) fail at with near-random accuracy under 4-shot CoT prompting.

## Input / Output

For Sudoku, the input is a sequence of (row, col, value) triplets encoding the initially filled cells, and the output is the sequence of (row, col, value) triplets for all unfilled cells; each cell is represented by exactly three tokens. For Zebra puzzles, the input consists of typed relational clues (7 types: is-equal, is-not-equal, immediate-left, neighbour, ends-in, left-of, in-between) over symbolic entity/attribute indices, and the output is the full entity-attribute assignment grid. The loss is applied only to the solution portion of the sequence, not the given cells, and the order in which solution cells appear during training is the critical design variable the paper manipulates.

## Method

The core contribution is showing that training a 42M-parameter GPT-2 (8 layers, 8 heads, dim 576) with next-token prediction on a 'solver-decomposed reasoning order'—where cells are sequenced by a 7-strategy solver (lone single, hidden single, naked pair/triplet, locked candidate, XY wing, unique rectangle) that fills the easiest cells first—transforms a task the model cannot learn (7.2% complete accuracy with fixed order, 1% with random order) into one it solves at 87.18% (94.21% with beam width 5). The position tokens in the sequence incentivize the model to learn *which* cell to fill next (search), while the value tokens incentivize learning *how* to fill it (strategy application), effectively decomposing the CSP into two sub-tasks without requiring explicit search traces in the training data. A probing analysis further reveals that the model's logits implicitly encode the solver's candidate set for every empty cell (≥93% overlap), demonstrating that human-like abstract reasoning structures emerge in the weights without any direct supervision on candidate sets.

## Results

On the 100K-puzzle Sudoku test set, a 42M-parameter GPT-2 trained with solver-decomposed reasoning order achieves 94.23% cell accuracy and 87.18% complete puzzle accuracy with greedy decoding, improving to 98.03% cell accuracy and 94.21% complete puzzle accuracy with beam search (width 5). On Zebra puzzles (320K training, 15K test), the same approach yields 96.26% evaluation accuracy and 92.04% complete puzzle accuracy with beam width 5. In contrast, fixed-order training achieves only 58.64% cell / 7.2% complete accuracy, and random-order training achieves ~52% cell / ~1% complete accuracy, demonstrating that the solver-decomposed order is essential for learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary external comparison is against the Recurrent Relational Network (RRN) of Palm et al. [2018], which handcrafts a recurrent architecture to match Sudoku's constraint structure and performs multi-round message passing between cells. On the RRN's own 18,000-puzzle test set, the proposed model (causal LM + solver-decomposed order + beam search width 5) achieves 95.43% complete puzzle accuracy versus RRN's 96.6%, a gap of roughly 1.2 percentage points, while requiring no architecture or loss-function customization. The paper also reports that frontier LLMs (GPT-4o and Gemini-1.5 Pro) fail entirely on 4-shot chain-of-thought Sudoku solving, achieving 0% complete puzzle accuracy and only 9.5–10.2% per-cell accuracy (near random). Internally, the solver-decomposed reasoning order training is the critical factor: it lifts complete puzzle accuracy from 7.2% (fixed order) and ~1% (random order) to 87.18% (greedy) and 94.21% (beam 5), a >12× improvement over the best non-decomposed baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of synthetic-task studies on Transformers (Othello, context-free grammars, modular arithmetic, sorting) to a substantially harder constraint-satisfaction setting where both search over cell positions and multi-step logical deduction are required. It departs from [LSM+24], which argues that full search traces must be included in training data for planning tasks, by showing that a much simpler 'solver-decomposed reasoning order' (just the sequence of cell positions, not the reasoning steps) suffices for next-token prediction to learn the task. It also contrasts with [BN24], which posits that next-token prediction is inherently flawed for search, by demonstrating that the objective works when the training data provides the right high-level decomposition. The probing analysis connects to the linear-representation literature [NLW23, PCV23, JRR+24] but applies it to a reasoning-specific concept (candidate sets) rather than linguistic features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that the right form of training data decomposition (solver order as a lightweight Chain-of-Thought) can unlock search-and-reasoning capabilities in a small 42M-parameter Transformer without requiring expensive search-trace generation, specialized architectures, or reinforcement learning. This suggests that pre-trained LLMs may already contain a strong reasoning engine that is simply not being properly activated by current prompting or fine-tuning strategies. Key limitations include the synthetic, no-backtracking setting, the absence of creative strategy invention, and the small model scale; open questions include whether the approach generalizes to problems requiring backtracking, whether models can discover novel strategies, and whether the candidate-set representation persists at larger scales or in more open-ended reasoning tasks.
