## Problem

In hierarchical federated learning (HFL), model drift occurs at multiple levels and timescales: client-level drift from local updates on heterogeneous data, and group-level drift from FL across clients within each group. Existing HFL algorithms either assume i.i.d. data or produce convergence bounds that degrade as data heterogeneity increases, and prior gradient-correction methods (SCAFFOLD, FedDyn, ProxSkip) are not easily extendable to the multi-timescale communication architecture of HFL. The paper asks how to provably tame multi-timescale model drift in non-i.i.d. HFL without requiring frequent model aggregations.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a hierarchical topology of N group aggregators, each coordinating n_j clients with local non-i.i.d. datasets, using two aggregation periods: H local iterations per group aggregation and E group aggregations per global aggregation. Beyond the standard model parameters exchanged in hierarchical FedAvg, MTGC requires only marginal additional communication: initializing the client-group correction variable z at the start of each global round and broadcasting the group-global correction variable y to clients after each global aggregation, yielding a per-aggregation communication overhead of (E+1)/E times that of HFedAvg. The output is a single trained global model, with convergence characterized at a virtual sequence of averaged models across the hierarchy.

## Method

MTGC introduces two coupled control variables updated at different timescales to correct gradient biases at each level of the hierarchy: a client-group correction z_i^{t,e} (updated after every H local iterations at group aggregation) that steers each client's gradient toward its group's gradient, and a group-global correction y_j^t (updated after every E group aggregations at global aggregation) that steers each group's gradient toward the global gradient. The local update at client i in group j becomes x_{i,h+1} = x_{i,h} − γ(∇F_i(x_{i,h}) + z_i^{t,e} + y_j^t), so both correction terms are applied at every local iteration despite being refreshed at different frequencies. The convergence analysis under general non-convex settings shows the bound is immune to the extent of intra- and inter-group data heterogeneity (no bounded gradient dissimilarity assumption needed), achieves linear speedup in H, E, and the number of clients, and recovers SCAFFOLD's rate when N=1 and E=1. The key analytical challenge is handling the coupling between z and y through their updates at different timescales, addressed via a novel Lyapunov function that tracks the interplay between client-level and group-level model drifts.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, MTGC achieves the largest speedups in the most challenging group non-i.i.d. & client non-i.i.d. setting: with E=30, H=20, MTGC requires only 19.6 global rounds to reach 80% testing accuracy, compared to >500 rounds for HFedAvg (>25.5× speedup). In the group non-i.i.d. & client i.i.d. setting, MTGC reaches 80% accuracy in 13.0 rounds versus 344.7 for HFedAvg (26.5× speedup). The speedup grows with increasing aggregation periods: for group i.i.d. & client non-i.i.d., MTGC's advantage over HFedAvg increases from 3.2× (E=10, H=20) to 5.1× (E=30, H=20). MTGC also achieves the highest testing accuracy across all four datasets (EMNIST-L, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100) in the group non-i.i.d. & client non-i.i.d. setting, with the gap being most pronounced on CIFAR-100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MTGC consistently outperforms all four baselines across every data distribution setting and aggregation period. Against HFedAvg (Wang et al., AAAI 2022), the primary baseline, MTGC achieves 3.2×–26.5× speedup in global rounds to reach 80% accuracy, with the advantage growing as data heterogeneity and aggregation periods increase; HFedAvg fails to reach 80% within 500 rounds in the most heterogeneous setting. Against SCAFFOLD (Karimireddy et al., ICML 2020) extended to HFL, MTGC achieves higher testing accuracy in all four datasets, particularly on CIFAR-100 where the gap is most visible. Against FedProx (Li et al., MLSys 2020) extended to HFL, MTGC also achieves superior accuracy, though the gap is smaller than with SCAFFOLD. FedDyn (Acar et al., ICLR 2020) performs worst among all baselines, exhibiting significant variance and instability, which the authors attribute to the hierarchical topology disrupting FedDyn's dynamic regularization structure. The ablation-style comparison between Local Correction (SCAFFOLD within groups) and Group Correction (SCAFFOLD across groups) confirms that each correction level addresses its corresponding heterogeneity source, and MTGC's combination of both yields the most stable and accurate performance across all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

MTGC extends the gradient correction mechanism of SCAFFOLD (which corrects client gradients toward the global gradient in star-topology FL) to the hierarchical setting by decomposing it into two coupled correction terms operating at different timescales: a client-group correction updated at each group aggregation and a group-global correction updated at each global aggregation. It builds on the hierarchical FedAvg framework (Wang et al., AAAI 2022) but departs from all prior HFL methods by avoiding bounded gradient dissimilarity assumptions entirely, whereas existing HFL convergence analyses (e.g., [47]) produce bounds that degrade as data heterogeneity increases. The paper also contrasts with single-level correction approaches (local-only or group-only SCAFFOLD variants) and shows that neither suffices when both intra- and inter-group non-i.i.d. are present.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HFL is the natural architecture for edge/fog computing and software-defined networks where clients cannot all communicate with a central server, yet prior algorithms either assumed i.i.d. data or suffered convergence degradation under heterogeneity. The heterogeneity-immune convergence bound and the demonstrated speedups (up to 26.5× over HFedAvg in the most heterogeneous setting) make MTGC a practical option for deployment in genuinely non-i.i.d. hierarchical systems. A key limitation is that the convergence analysis covers only the two-level case, while the three-level extension is validated empirically but lacks a formal guarantee; extending the Lyapunov-based analysis to arbitrary depth remains an open problem. The finding that FedDyn—a strong star-topology method—performs worst in HFL also cautions against naively porting conventional FL algorithms to hierarchical topologies.
