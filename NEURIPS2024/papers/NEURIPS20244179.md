## Problem

Vision Transformers achieve state-of-the-art performance on many vision tasks yet exhibit surprising failures on tasks requiring computation over visual relations between objects. Prior interpretability efforts have characterized low-level visual features but have not examined the higher-level algorithms ViTs use for abstract visual reasoning. This paper asks: how do ViTs internally attempt to implement relational operations like same-different judgment, and what internal mechanisms enable or prevent generalizable relational reasoning?

## Input / Output

Inputs are 224×224 RGB images containing simple geometric objects (16 shapes × 16 colors) that are patch-aligned to ViT token boundaries (single patch for B/32, four patches for B/16 and B/14), with Gaussian noise added to tokens to prevent pixel-level shortcuts. The discrimination task presents two objects and requires a binary same/different label; the RMTS task presents four objects in two pairs (a fixed display pair in the top-left and a variable sample pair) and requires judging whether both pairs exhibit the same intermediate same/different relation. A photorealistic Blender-rendered 3D evaluation set tests zero-shot transfer to realistic lighting, backgrounds, occlusion, and rotation.

## Method

The paper applies mechanistic interpretability techniques to CLIP- and DINOv2-pretrained ViTs fine-tuned on same-different tasks, revealing a two-stage processing pipeline: a perceptual stage (early layers) where local attention heads form disentangled shape and color representations in separate linear subspaces, followed by a relational stage (later layers) where global attention heads compare object representations. Disentanglement is verified via Distributed Alignment Search (DAS) counterfactual interventions that swap shape or color information between objects; abstraction of the relational computation is tested by patching novel vectors (additions, interpolations, samples) into the identified subspaces. For RMTS, linear probes and directional interventions identify abstract same/different representations invariant to perceptual content. The authors further introduce a disentanglement loss (token-level probes enforcing separate shape/color subspaces) and a pipeline loss (attention regularization encouraging within-object → within-pair → between-pair attention at specific layers), demonstrating that from-scratch ViTs require both losses to solve RMTS, while either alone suffices for discrimination.

## Results

CLIP- and DINOv2-pretrained ViTs achieve near-perfect accuracy on both the discrimination task (99.3% and 99.5% test) and the RMTS task (98.3% and 98.2% test), while smaller-scale pretrained models (ImageNet, DINO, MAE) and from-scratch models degrade substantially, with from-scratch ViTs reaching only chance (50.1%) on RMTS. On a zero-shot photorealistic evaluation, CLIP uniquely generalizes to 93.9% accuracy while all other models remain at chance (~48–53%). Mechanistically, CLIP and DINOv2 exhibit a clear two-stage processing pipeline (perceptual → relational) in their attention patterns, whereas DINO, MAE, and from-scratch models show no such separation, and disentanglement of shape/color subspaces correlates with downstream generalization performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP (400M pretraining) and DINOv2 (142M) are the only models that achieve near-perfect accuracy on both the discrimination and RMTS tasks (≥98% test), and both exhibit the two-stage perceptual→relational attention pipeline. On the photorealistic zero-shot evaluation, CLIP dramatically outperforms all other models (93.9% vs. 48–53%), suggesting that the CLIP objective or its specific pretraining data confers additional robustness beyond scale alone. ImageNet-pretrained ViTs (14.2M) show loose two-stage processing and moderate RMTS performance (89.3%), while DINO and MAE (1.28M each) fail to exhibit any clear stage separation and score 87.7% and 93.4% on RMTS respectively. From-scratch ViTs collapse to chance on RMTS (50.1%) and only reach 80.5% on discrimination, confirming that both the perceptual and relational stages must be intact for relational reasoning. The paper concludes that pretraining scale, rather than the specific training objective, is the primary driver of the two-stage processing architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior behavioral evaluations of same-different generalization (Kim et al., 2018; Tartaglini et al., 2023) by adopting mechanistic interpretability techniques originally developed for NLP transformers—Distributed Alignment Search, attention pattern analysis, and linear probing/interventions—to characterize the internal algorithms of ViTs rather than just their input-output behavior. It extends the same-different discrimination task (well-studied in ML) with a novel relational match-to-sample (RMTS) formulation drawn from cognitive science, requiring explicit representation of the abstract relation. The work also connects to the long-standing Fodor-Pylyshyn debate on whether neural networks can implement syntactic/relational operations, providing mechanistic evidence rather than purely behavioral evidence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-stage (perceptual → relational) processing pipeline provides a concrete diagnostic framework: failures in relational reasoning can be attributed to either insufficiently disentangled object representations or failed relational computation, and each can be targeted with specific auxiliary losses. The correlation between disentanglement and compositional/OOD generalization offers a principled explanation for why some pretrained models (CLIP, DINOv2) succeed where others fail. Key limitations include the restriction to the simplest possible relation (same-different on two objects), primarily synthetic stimuli, and ViT-B/16 and B/32 architectures; open questions include whether the pipeline generalizes to more complex relations, whether pretraining scale (vs. objective) is the true driver, and whether the observed abstraction is sufficient for real-world compositional visual reasoning.
