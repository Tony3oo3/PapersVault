## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models produce unnatural faces and hands in human-centric generation due to two factors: insufficient high-quality human-centric training priors, and the high variability of facial features and hand gestures that makes them especially difficult to render faithfully. Existing approaches either lack dedicated close-up data for these parts or require additional conditioning inputs (skeletons, camera parameters) that reduce practical usability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text prompt (processed by the CLIP text encoder, ≤77 tokens) and produces a 512×512 RGB image (1024×1024 for SDXL) without requiring any auxiliary inputs such as body skeletons, SMPL-H parameters, or camera poses. This distinguishes it from concurrent human-generation methods like HyperHuman and HanDiffuser that demand structured conditioning signals beyond text.

## Method

MoLE is a three-stage pipeline built on Stable Diffusion: (1) fine-tune the UNet and text encoder on a curated ~1M-image human-centric dataset to inject sufficient human prior; (2) train two independent LoRA modules (rank 256) as specialized experts—one on ~6.4k close-up face images, the other on ~7k close-up hand images—exploiting the observed 'low-rank refinement' phenomenon where a LoRA trained on close-ups of a body part refines that part at inference; (3) integrate both experts via a Soft MoE mechanism that combines a local per-token gating (sigmoid over a learnable linear projection of each token) with a global scalar gating (sigmoid over an adaptive average pool), allowing both experts to be simultaneously active with content-adaptive weights. The final output is the base UNet linear layer output plus the weighted sum of both expert outputs, with only the gating layers trained in Stage 3 while the base model and experts remain frozen.

## Results

On COCO Human Prompts, MoLE(SDXL) achieves 21.36% HPS and 98.52% IR, outperforming SDXL (20.84% HPS, 73.34% IR) by a large margin on IR. On DiffusionDB Human Prompts, MoLE(SDXL) reaches 22.35% HPS and 105.25% IR, again substantially exceeding SDXL (21.51% HPS, 87.88% IR). MoLE(SDv1.5) improves over the SDv1.5 baseline on both benchmarks (e.g., 20.27% vs 19.91% HPS and 33.75% vs 28.34% IR on COCO), while also surpassing VQ-Diffusion and Versatile Diffusion on all metrics. The method generalizes to SD v2.1 (20.45% HPS vs 20.17%) and PixArt-α (21.79% HPS vs 21.33%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoLE(SDXL) decisively outperforms SDXL on both benchmarks, with the largest gap on IR (98.52% vs 73.34% on COCO; 105.25% vs 87.88% on DiffusionDB), while HPS gains are more modest (~0.5 points). Against the older architectures VQ-Diffusion and Versatile Diffusion, MoLE wins on every metric by wide margins, with those baselines even producing negative IR scores. MoLE(SDv1.5) consistently beats the SDv1.5 baseline on both HPS and IR across both benchmarks. In user studies (Appendix A.5), 58% of 50 participants prefer MoLE over HyperHuman on hand quality, while MoLE is slightly behind HanDiffuser (48% vs 52%), though MoLE requires no additional inputs (skeleton, SMPL-H, camera parameters) unlike both competitors. On the broader metrics CLIP-T and FID (Table 4), MoLE improves over SDv1.5 (27.33 vs 26.87 CLIP-T; 64.37 vs 69.82 FID) but is marginally worse on Aesthetic Score (5.18 vs 5.19), which the authors attribute to SDv1.5's fine-tuning on a high-aesthetic dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoLE sits at the intersection of three established threads: LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning, Mixture-of-Experts routing, and latent diffusion text-to-image generation. Its key conceptual departure is repurposing LoRA modules trained on specialized close-up datasets (face, hand) as MoE experts, rather than the conventional use of MoE in diffusion (e.g., ERNIE-ViLG and eDiff-i divide denoising stages among experts). The soft assignment mechanism draws from Soft MoE, but the paper extends it with a novel local (per-token) assignment alongside the standard global (per-image) assignment, motivated by the observation that face and hand occupy different spatial regions within a single image. The collected human-centric dataset, particularly the high-quality close-up hand subset, fills a gap the authors identify as absent in prior human-centric generation work like CosmicMan.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a persistent and visible failure mode in text-to-image generation (unnatural hands and faces) with a resource-friendly recipe (single A100) that generalizes across SD v1.5, v2.1, XL, and PixArt, making it practically accessible. The curated dataset—especially the 7k close-up hand images—is a standalone contribution that the authors argue is missing from prior literature. However, significant limitations remain: the method struggles with multi-person scenes, only ~25% of generated images reach high quality (vs. 10% for SDXL), and it inherits racial biases from training data. Open questions include whether the low-rank refinement phenomenon generalizes to other body parts or object categories, and whether scaling the close-up datasets or adding more experts would close the remaining quality gap.
