## Problem

Transformers trained on masked language modeling exhibit sudden, sharp drops in training loss after extended plateaus, yet the mechanistic cause of this 'algorithmic transition' remains poorly understood. This paper addresses the gap by formulating low-rank matrix completion as a controlled MLM analogue, where the mathematical structure of the task makes it possible to identify precisely what the model computes before and after the transition, and which components drive the sudden drop.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241746/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is an n×n low-rank matrix (primarily 7×7, rank-2) with a fraction p_mask=0.3 of entries replaced by a MASK token; the matrix is flattened to an n²-token sequence where real values are discretized to 2-decimal tokens over [-10,10]. The output is the full n×n matrix with predicted values at every position, trained with MSE loss over all entries. Notably, no explicit row/column delimiters are provided—the model must infer 2D structure purely from learned positional embeddings.

## Method

A 4-layer, 8-head BERT is trained in an online setting (256 fresh matrices per step, 50k steps, Adam at 1e-4) on 7×7 rank-2 matrices, and the paper's contribution is a detailed interpretability analysis of the model before and after the sudden loss drop at ~step 15000. Pre-transition, the model simply copies observed entries and outputs 0 at masked positions, with attention heads contributing negligibly (confirmed via uniform ablation and model switching). Post-transition, the model learns structured attention patterns—row heads, column heads, and identity heads—that causally compute missing entries (verified via activation patching), and it outperforms nuclear norm minimization, ruling out that classical algorithm. Component-wise training dynamics reveal that MLPs and token embeddings converge without plateaus, while attention layers and positional embeddings exhibit the plateau-then-drop pattern, identifying the learning of correct attention structure as the primary driver of the abrupt loss decrease.

## Results

The 4-layer, 8-head BERT model trained on 7×7 rank-2 matrices (p_mask=0.3) achieves a final MSE of approximately 4×10⁻³, outperforming nuclear norm minimization (best L ≈ 0.012) by roughly 3×. The training loss exhibits a plateau followed by a sudden drop at approximately step 15,000, marking an algorithmic transition from copying the input to accurately computing missing entries. Post-transition, the model's L_mask drops from ~0.22 (copying phase) to ~0.013, while L_obs remains low throughout (~10⁻⁴).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole quantitative baseline is nuclear norm minimization (Candès & Recht, 2009), the standard convex relaxation for low-rank matrix completion. BERT clearly outperforms nuclear norm minimization on MSE: the trained model achieves L ≈ 0.004 versus the best nuclear norm solution of L ≈ 0.012 (at λ=0.001), a roughly 3× improvement. Crucially, the BERT solution has a *larger* nuclear norm than the nuclear norm minimizer's solution, confirming that the model is not implicitly recovering the convex relaxation. The authors further verify this by testing against the regularized variant (adding λ‖U‖_* to the MSE objective) across multiple λ values (0.0005–0.005), and BERT still outperforms all of them on MSE. This rules out the hypothesis that the Transformer is simply learning to implement nuclear norm minimization internally.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the observation of sudden loss drops in transformer training (Chen et al. on MLM breakthroughs, the grokking literature), (2) transformers solving mathematical/linear-algebraic tasks (Charton, Lee et al.), and (3) mechanistic interpretability tools (activation patching, attention analysis, linear probing). It carves new ground by transplanting the interpretability toolkit from NLP into a controlled mathematical setting (low-rank matrix completion framed as MLM), enabling causal analysis of what the model actually computes before and after the sudden drop. The authors explicitly distinguish their setup from in-context learning and from prior work on transformer arithmetic, emphasizing that the sudden drop here is a function of training steps rather than input length or number of observed entries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare controlled setting where the 'sudden drop' in transformer training can be dissected mechanistically, revealing a clean algorithmic transition (copying → computing) and showing that different components (token embeddings, positional embeddings, attention heads, MLPs) converge at markedly different times. The finding that the trained BERT outperforms nuclear norm minimization on MSE while having a larger nuclear norm suggests the model learns a genuinely different algorithm, raising the open question of what that algorithm is. Limitations include small matrix sizes (≤15×15), no error bars, and a single architecture family, so whether these interpretability findings generalize to larger or more complex tasks remains unclear. The work also highlights a broader implication for AI safety: if capabilities emerge through discrete algorithmic transitions rather than gradual improvement, tracking predefined progress measures may be insufficient for predicting when a model will suddenly acquire new abilities.
