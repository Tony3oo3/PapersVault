## Problem

When LLMs are used in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), retrieved documents may contain incorrect or harmful information, yet it remains unclear whether models can reliably arbitrate between their own parametric knowledge and external context. Prior work only tested one direction of conflict (context wrong, model right), leaving open whether models can also correct their own errors when given correct context, and how this behavior scales with the degree of perturbation and the model's confidence in its prior.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a question with a retrieved context document whose answer has been systematically perturbed (10 multiplicative levels for numerical answers, 10 absolute year shifts, or 3 categorical tiers for names/locations). The model is queried twice—once without context to obtain a prior response and its token log-probabilities, and once with the perturbed context—yielding a contextual response. From these, the paper derives accuracy, context bias (adopting wrong context over correct prior), prior bias (ignoring correct context in favor of wrong prior), and context preference rate binned by prior token probability.

## Method

The paper introduces ClashEval, a 1,294-question benchmark across six domains (drug dosages, sports records, news, dates, names, locations) where each question is paired with a relevant document whose answer is perturbed across a controlled range of deviations. Six LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5, Llama-3-8B, Gemini 1.5 Flash, Claude Opus, Claude Sonnet) are evaluated under standard, strict, and loose RAG prompts, and their context preference rates are regressed against both the perturbation magnitude and the mean token probability of the prior response. The key empirical finding is a consistent negative correlation: models are less likely to adopt context as it deviates further from truth or as their prior confidence increases. Building on this, the authors propose a token probability correction that compares the mean token probabilities of the prior and contextual responses, and a calibrated variant that compares percentiles instead of raw scores, improving overall accuracy by ~14% and reducing context bias by ~20% at the cost of modestly increased prior bias.

## Results

On the ClashEval benchmark (1,294 questions across 6 domains), Claude Opus achieves the best overall performance with 74.3% accuracy, 15.7% context bias, and 2.1% prior bias. GPT-4o, despite being the top-ranked model on LMSYS Chatbot Arena, exhibits the second-highest context bias (30.4%), overriding its own correct prior knowledge over 60% of the time when presented with incorrect retrieved content. A simple calibrated token probability correction method improves GPT-4o's accuracy from 61.5% to 75.4% while reducing context bias from 30.4% to 10.7%, at the cost of increasing prior bias from 2.1% to 8.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Claude Opus outperforms GPT-4o on all three metrics: 74.3% vs. 61.5% accuracy, 15.7% vs. 30.4% context bias, and comparable prior bias (2.1% vs. 2.1%), making it the most robust model at arbitrating between prior knowledge and retrieved context. GPT-4o underperforms Gemini 1.5 Flash on accuracy (61.5% vs. 62.4%) and context bias (30.4% vs. 24.5%), despite being the top-ranked model on general-purpose benchmarks, suggesting that general LLM performance does not translate to RAG robustness. GPT-3.5 achieves the worst accuracy (53.9%) and highest context bias (31.3%), while Llama-3-8B, though having lower context bias than GPT-4o (26.4% vs. 30.4%), suffers from a high 'neither' rate (26.3%) that drags its accuracy down to 50.0%. The calibrated token probability correction narrows the gap substantially: GPT-4o jumps to 75.4% accuracy (surpassing Claude Opus's 74.3%) with 10.7% context bias, though at the cost of elevated prior bias (8.5%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge-conflict framework established by Longpre et al. (2021) and Xie et al. (2023), which studied LLMs' tendency to favor parametric priors over contextual information. The key departure is making the conflict bidirectional: prior work primarily measured whether models reject incorrect context, whereas ClashEval also measures whether models adopt correct context when their own prior is wrong, preventing the trivial strategy of always ignoring context. Additionally, the paper introduces a quantitative axis absent from prior work—systematically varying the degree of perturbation in retrieved documents and correlating context preference with token-level confidence scores—thereby moving from qualitative observations to measurable dose-response relationships.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a practically critical vulnerability in deployed RAG systems: even the top-performing model (GPT-4o) overrides its own correct knowledge with incorrect retrieved content over 60% of the time, and the simple token-probability correction yields a 14% accuracy gain, suggesting that calibration-aware routing is an underexploited lever. Limitations include coverage of only six fact-based domains, enriched error rates not representative of real-world retrieval, and applicability of the correction method only to models exposing logprobs. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to multi-step reasoning tasks, whether the context bias persists as models scale, and how to design retrieval pipelines that explicitly account for model confidence rather than treating all retrieved documents as equally trustworthy.
