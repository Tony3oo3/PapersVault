## Problem

Existing computer-aided synthesis planning (CASP) algorithms assume that reaching any purchasable building block is sufficient, failing to address the common real-world constraint where a specific starting material must appear in the route (e.g., natural products for semi-synthesis or waste feedstocks for sustainable chemistry). This paper formulates starting material-constrained synthesis planning and proposes a bidirectional search algorithm that explicitly enforces the constraint rather than treating it as an afterthought.

## Input / Output

The method takes a target molecule and one or more specified starting material molecules (as SMILES strings) along with a building block set of ~23 million purchasable compounds from eMolecules. It outputs a valid synthetic route as a directed acyclic graph of reactions where at least one leaf node maps to the specified starting material, all other non-building-block molecules are synthesized, and the target is the final product. The search operates on an AND-OR graph (molecules as OR nodes, reactions as AND nodes) with a maximum molecule depth of 11 (top-down) or 6 (bottom-up) and a budget of 500 total expansions.

## Method

DESP instantiates two AND-OR search graphs — a top-down retrosynthetic graph G_R rooted at the target and a bottom-up forward graph G_F rooted at the starting material — and alternates expansions between them (ratio λ=2). A key component is the synthetic distance network D, a goal-conditioned cost function trained offline via pathway enumeration over the USPTO-Full reaction corpus (~1M reactions) that estimates the minimum reaction cost between any two molecules, enabling both search directions to be guided toward convergence. The bottom-up forward expansion uses two MLPs (f_t for template selection and f_b for building block retrieval via k-NN over Morgan fingerprints) conditioned on the opposing molecule, adapting Gao et al.'s amortized tree generation. Two variants are implemented: F2E (each direction conditioned on the opposing root) and F2F (each direction conditioned on the closest opposing node by D). The top-down selection policy combines Retro*'s V_t cost with the minimum synthetic distance D_t across frontier nodes, while the bottom-up selection follows standard Retro* logic, and a modified MSE loss with injected negative examples handles the skewed cost distribution during D training.

## Results

On the starting material-constrained synthesis planning task, DESP-F2E achieves the highest solve rates across all three benchmarks: 39.5% on USPTO-190 (500 expansions), 96.0% on Pistachio Reachable (300 expansions), and 50.0% on Pistachio Hard (500 expansions), while also requiring the fewest average expansions (340, 41, and 300 respectively). DESP-F2F achieves comparable solve rates (39.5%, 97.3%, 48.0%) with slightly fewer average expansions (336, 38, 293). Both DESP variants find shorter routes on average than Retro* (e.g., 5.56 vs. 5.56 reactions on USPTO-190, 4.67 vs. 4.81 on Pistachio Hard), and DESP-F2E outperforms Retro*+D (the ablation adding synthetic distance without bidirectional search) on all benchmarks, confirming the complementary contribution of both components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DESP outperforms all baselines on every benchmark. Against Retro* (Chen et al., 2020), the A*-like search that DESP builds upon, DESP-F2E improves solve rate by 3.7 points on USPTO-190 (39.5% vs. 35.8%), 3.3 points on Pistachio Reachable (96.0% vs. 92.7%), and 13.0 points on Pistachio Hard (50.0% vs. 37.0%), while reducing average expansions by 11–42 nodes. Against MCTS (Segler et al., 2018), DESP-F2E achieves substantially higher solve rates (39.5% vs. 35.3% on USPTO-190; 50.0% vs. 32.0% on Pistachio Hard) with fewer expansions. Against GRASP (Yu et al., 2022), the only other method explicitly designed for goal-conditioned retrosynthesis, DESP-F2E improves solve rate by 15.8 points on USPTO-190 (39.5% vs. 23.7%) and 21.0 points on Pistachio Hard (50.0% vs. 29.0%), with GRASP performing worse than even BFS on the harder benchmarks. The ablation Retro*+D (adding synthetic distance without bidirectional search) confirms both components contribute: it improves over Retro* but still trails DESP by 2.1–8.0 points in solve rate, indicating that bidirectional search provides additional gains beyond better node evaluation alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

DESP extends the Retro* A*-like AND-OR graph search framework (Chen et al., 2020) by introducing a bidirectional search scheme that interleaves top-down retrosynthetic expansions with bottom-up forward expansions, drawing on the conditional forward synthesis generation approach of Gao et al. (2022). The key conceptual departure from prior CASP work is the introduction of a 'synthetic distance' network that estimates the cost between two specific molecules rather than between a molecule and arbitrary building blocks, enabling goal-conditioned search. While GRASP (Yu et al., 2022) and LHASA (Johnson et al., 1992) previously addressed starting material constraints via RL with hindsight replay and expert rules respectively, DESP is the first to combine a learned goal-conditioned cost function with a formal bidirectional AND-OR search, and the first to release dedicated benchmarks for this constrained setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practically important gap: real-world synthesis planning (semi-synthesis from natural products, sustainable feedstock utilization) routinely requires specific starting materials, yet mainstream CASP algorithms ignore this constraint. The new Pistachio-derived benchmarks also address known redundancies in USPTO-190, providing the community with more rigorous evaluation. Key limitations include the inability of the bottom-up search to handle convergent syntheses, GPU dependency for k-NN over ~23M building blocks, and the finding that DESP-F2F produces longer routes despite fewer expansions. Open questions include whether DESP's performance will scale with improvements to one-step models, and whether sub-goal or divide-and-conquer strategies from hierarchical planning could further improve efficiency.
