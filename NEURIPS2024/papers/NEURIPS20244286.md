## Problem

Diffusion-based video super-resolution produces perceptually realistic frames but suffers from temporal inconsistency due to stochastic sampling fluctuations. Traditional pixel-level inter-frame alignment (optical flow, deformable convolution) breaks down on diffusion-processed frames because iterative denoising disrupts pixel correspondences, making it impossible to reliably track sub-pixel information across adjacent frames.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244286/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-resolution video sequence (N frames of C×H×W, degraded via 4× bicubic downsampling) and a predefined vocabulary for open-vocabulary segmentation, producing high-resolution frames of C×sH×sW. Processing is parallelized over non-overlapping clips of m frames (5 in practice), and the diffusion process operates in the 2D discrete wavelet transform domain rather than pixel space, with HR/LR frames recursively decomposed into four frequency sub-bands concatenated along the channel dimension. A frozen pre-trained OpenSeeD segmentation model serves as the semantic distiller, so all learnable parameters reside in the pixel condenser.

## Method

SeeClear reformulates the forward diffusion as a 'Blurring ResShift' process that combines patch-level Gaussian blurring (via DCT diagonal matrices) with progressive residual shifting between HR and LR frames in the frequency domain, constructing a Markov chain from HR to LR rather than to pure noise. The Pixel Condenser is an attention-based U-Net operating on DWT sub-bands, where window-based self-attention models intra-sub-band correlations and channel-wise self-attention models inter-sub-band correlations, replacing the autoencoder to avoid high-frequency loss. The Instance-Centric Alignment Module (InCAM) uses semantic tokens from the frozen distiller to spatially modulate features and perform multi-frame self-attention in semantic space, bypassing unreliable pixel-level alignment. The Channel-wise Texture Aggregation Memory (CaTeGory) clusters decoder features into semantic-category groups along the channel dimension, providing a learnable long-term texture prior that is queried by clip-wise semantics to maintain consistency across video clips. Training proceeds in three stages (pixel condenser, CaTeGory, joint fine-tuning) with Charbonnier loss on 4×4090 GPUs.

## Results

On REDS4, the extended SeeClear (SeeClear*) achieves the best LPIPS of 0.1548 and a PSNR of 31.32 dB, while the base SeeClear attains an LPIPS of 0.1843 with a PSNR of 28.92 dB. On Vid4, SeeClear* reaches an LPIPS of 0.2054 and PSNR of 27.80 dB. The extended version delivers a 10.8% relative LPIPS improvement over the strongest diffusion-based competitor SATeCo (0.1735) on REDS4, while maintaining a competitive PSNR (31.32 vs. 31.62 dB). In terms of efficiency, SeeClear uses 229.23M parameters with 15 time steps and 1.142 s inference per 512×512 frame, substantially fewer parameters than StableVSR (712M) and MGLD-VSR (1.5B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against regression-based VSR methods (BasicVSR, VRT, IconVSR), SeeClear* achieves comparable PSNR (31.32 vs. 31.42–31.67 dB) while substantially outperforming on LPIPS (0.1548 vs. 0.1939–0.2023 on REDS4), indicating superior perceptual quality. Against the strongest diffusion-based VSR competitor SATeCo, SeeClear* wins on LPIPS by a 10.8% margin on REDS4 (0.1548 vs. 0.1735) and 10.3% on Vid4 (0.2054 vs. 0.2291), though SATeCo retains a slight PSNR edge (31.62 vs. 31.32 dB). Compared to single-frame diffusion SR methods (StableSR, ResShift), SeeClear dominates on all three metrics, with PSNR gains of 3.56 dB over StableSR and 0.56 dB over ResShift on REDS4. In efficiency comparisons (Table 5), SeeClear (229.23M params, 15 steps, 1.142 s) is markedly lighter and faster than StableVSR (712M, 50 steps, 28.57 s) and MGLD-VSR (1.5B, 50 steps, 1.113 s), while matching or exceeding their perceptual quality. The base (non-extended) SeeClear trades ~2.7 dB PSNR for a 6.6% LPIPS gain over the baseline without semantic conditioning, confirming that the dual semantic control (InCAM + CaTeGory) is the primary driver of perceptual improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

SeeClear sits at the intersection of three research threads: diffusion-based super-resolution (building directly on ResShift's residual-shifting schedule and Blurring Diffusion's heat-dissipation forward process), video SR with temporal alignment (departing from the pixel-level optical-flow/deformable-convolution paradigm of BasicVSR, EDVR, and VRT), and semantic-assisted restoration (extending the instance-centric alignment idea from the authors' own Semantic Lens [34] and the broader SFT/SeD/CoSeR/SeeSR line). The key conceptual departure is replacing pixel-space inter-frame alignment with alignment in a semantic token space, motivated by the observation that diffusion's iterative corruption destroys the intensity structure that flow-based methods rely on. The DWT-based frequency decomposition replaces the autoencoder latent space used by StableSR and LDM, and the dual semantic memory (InCAM for clip-level, CaTeGory for cross-clip) is a novel architectural contribution not present in prior diffusion VSR work like Upscale-A-Video or MGLD-VSR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine weakness of diffusion-based VSR—stochastic frame-to-frame inconsistency—by grounding generation in distilled semantic priors rather than relying on pixel-level alignment that breaks under diffusion noise. The 10.8% LPIPS improvement over the strongest competitor (SATeCo) in the extended version, combined with competitive PSNR, suggests semantic conditioning is a viable path to closing the perceptual gap between generative and regression-based VSR. However, the method is limited to synthetic bicubic degradation on relatively small datasets (REDS, Vimeo-90K), and the authors acknowledge it does not fully exploit the generative capacity available from large-scale pretraining. Open questions include whether the semantic-space alignment strategy transfers to real-world degradation, whether trainable wavelet transforms (lifting scheme) could further improve the DWT-based pipeline, and how the CaTeGory memory scales to very long video sequences beyond the 5-frame clip window.
