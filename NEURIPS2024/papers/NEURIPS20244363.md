## Problem

Existing video QA benchmarks predominantly cover simplistic actions that can be identified from a single frame (static appearance bias), leaving a critical gap in evaluating models on fine-grained, domain-specialized action recognition. Within a single domain such as sports, actions can appear nearly identical in isolation but differ in subtle temporal movement sequences, requiring models to track specific individuals and discern nuanced motion across many frames. This paper asks whether current vision-language foundation models can truly recognize such domain-specific actions, or whether they still rely on static visual cues.

## Input / Output

Each instance pairs a short video clip (average 6.07 seconds at 32.18 FPS) with a natural-language question that identifies a specific individual by distinguishing attributes (e.g., jersey number, clothing color) and asks which of four or five choices best describes that person's action within a specified temporal context (before/after certain events). The output is a single multiple-choice letter. For models that cannot process video directly, frames are uniformly sampled at varying rates (1–32 frames) and fed as image inputs concatenated with the question text; for Gemini's video mode, the raw video file is passed, though the authors discovered it always samples only the middle frame per second (1 FPS).

## Method

The core contribution is a scalable, multi-stage data collection pipeline: GPT4 is prompted via an expansion-and-squeeze strategy to compile 10.5k actions across 150 sports; YouTube metadata (~4.5M videos) is filtered using Elasticsearch lexical search and CLIP similarity; Whisper transcription combined with GPT4-text localizes likely action segments within long tutorial videos; and multi-round crowd-sourcing validates action presence and provides temporal localization with actor-identifying attributes. The resulting benchmark contains 934 video-question pairs spanning 580 unique actions across 56 sports (1896 total actions in choices). Evaluation reveals that GPT-4o achieves 45.52% accuracy at 16 frames (best model) versus 61.64% for non-expert humans given action descriptions, with random chance at ~21%. A key empirical finding is that higher frame sampling rates significantly improve GPT-4o's performance (33.08% at 1 frame → 42.95% at 16 frames), while Gemini's fixed 1 FPS sampling and most open models (performing near random chance) fail to capture the necessary temporal detail.

## Results

On ActionAtlas (934 videos, 56 sports, 580 unique actions), the best proprietary model GPT-4o achieves 42.95% top-1 accuracy with 16 uniformly sampled frames, rising to 45.52% with chain-of-thought reasoning and choice descriptions. The best open-weight model, Qwen2-VL-7B, reaches only 30.24% (16 frames), while most other open models (mPLUG-Owl, Video-LLaMA, VideoChat2, LLaVA-Next-Video) perform at or near the 20.91% random-chance level. Non-expert crowd workers, given action descriptions, achieve 61.64% accuracy, revealing a substantial gap between current VLMs and human visual recognition of fine-grained domain-specific actions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o clearly outperforms all other evaluated models, reaching 42.95% with 16 frames versus 35.59% for Gemini 1.5 Pro (all frames) and 34.25% for GPT-4 Turbo (8 frames). Among open models, Qwen2-VL-7B is the only one to meaningfully exceed random chance (30.24% vs. 20.91%), while mPLUG-Owl (19.49%), Video-LLaMA (22.71%), VideoChat2 (21.27%), and LLaVA-Next-Video (22.90%) all perform at or below random. Gemini 1.5 Pro in video mode (1 fps default) scores 32.37%, and converting videos to 1 FPS to expose all frames yields only a marginal, non-significant gain to 35.59%, suggesting the model was not trained to exploit higher temporal density. The 16.12-point gap between the best model (GPT-4o, 45.52%) and non-expert humans (61.64%) underscores that current VLMs still struggle substantially with fine-grained action discrimination within specialized domains, even when action descriptions are provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VideoQA benchmark tradition (NextQA, ActivityNet-QA, VATEX) and fine-grained action recognition datasets (Finegym, Diving48, Multisports) by combining them into a single multiple-choice QA format that demands both temporal understanding and individual tracking within specialized domains. It departs from prior action recognition work that relied on expert-curated action lists or single-source knowledge bases by leveraging GPT4's domain knowledge for action compilation and Whisper+GPT4-text for segment localization in long videos. The collection pipeline repurposes existing tools (Elasticsearch lexical search, CLIP filtering, Mechanical Turk) in a novel multi-stage configuration, but the evaluation targets are standard VLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini, Qwen2-VL) rather than a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial gap between state-of-the-art VLMs (GPT-4o at 45.52%) and non-expert humans with action descriptions (61.64%), localizing the bottleneck to visual recognition of fine motions rather than action-name knowledge. The finding that higher frame sampling rates significantly improve performance (GPT-4o: 33%→43% from 1→16 frames) while CoT reasoning consistently degrades results provides actionable guidance for video model design. Open questions include whether better spatiotemporal tokenizers can maintain accuracy at higher sampling rates without proportional token increases, and whether the 4.5M collected YouTube videos can serve as pretraining data to close the human-AI gap. Limitations include restriction to sports, small dataset size (934 videos), and absence of an action taxonomy.
