## Problem

Existing DNA language model (DNALM) benchmarks rely on surrogate classification tasks, compositionally uncontrolled negatives, and weak baselines, failing to assess whether DNALMs actually learn useful representations of regulatory DNA for key downstream applications like motif discovery, cell-type-specific activity prediction, and counterfactual variant effect prediction. DART-Eval addresses this by providing a rigorous, multi-task evaluation suite that directly targets these biologically relevant use cases.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241980/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs span multiple resolutions: 350 bp regulatory element sequences (with dinucleotide-shuffled compositionally-matched negatives), TF motifs inserted into 100 neutral backgrounds, 2 kb genomic context sequences for chromatin accessibility regression, and 2,114 bp variant allele pair sequences for counterfactual scoring. Outputs are structured per task: paired/absolute accuracy for element discrimination, per-motif accuracy across 1,443 TFs, five-way cell-type classification (AUROC/AUPRC), Spearman/Pearson correlation for quantitative accessibility, and AUROC/AUPRC/Pearson r for variant effect prediction. All evaluations are conducted in three settings—zero-shot (likelihood or embedding distance), probed (frozen embeddings + CNN head), and fine-tuned (LoRA adapters)—enabling direct comparison of how much task-specific training is needed.

## Method

DART-Eval introduces five benchmark tasks of increasing difficulty: (1) regulatory element vs. background discrimination, (2) sensitivity to 1,443 known TF binding motifs, (3) cell-type-specific regulatory sequence classification across five cell lines, (4) quantitative chromatin accessibility regression from 2 kb sequence, and (5) counterfactual prediction of regulatory variant effects using caQTL/dsQTL datasets. Six contemporary DNALMs (Caduceus, DNABERT-2, GENA-LM, HyenaDNA, Mistral-DNA, Nucleotide Transformer) are evaluated in zero-shot, probed, and LoRA fine-tuned settings against ab initio baselines including ChromBPNet (6.6M parameter dilated CNN) and a small probing-head-like CNN (68K parameters). Key design choices include dinucleotide-shuffled negatives to control for GC-content confounders, LD-aware curation of causal variants restricted to accessible peak regions, and chromosome-based train/validation/test splits to prevent data leakage. The framework reveals that embedding-free methods (likelihoods, fine-tuning) consistently outperform embedding-based approaches, and that simple ab initio supervised models match or exceed much larger fine-tuned DNALMs, particularly on counterfactual variant prediction tasks.

## Results

Across five regulatory DNA tasks of increasing difficulty, DNALMs show inconsistent performance relative to ab initio baselines. On the easiest task (regulatory element identification), all DNALMs achieve 0.745–0.971 zero-shot accuracy, and fine-tuned models reach ~0.97 paired accuracy, but a 68K-parameter ab initio CNN matches probed DNALMs (0.932 paired accuracy). On chromatin accessibility regression (Spearman r), fine-tuned DNALMs (e.g., NT: 0.515–0.737) roughly match ChromBPNet (0.540–0.754), while probed models fall well behind. On the hardest task—counterfactual variant effect prediction—ChromBPNet substantially outperforms all DNALMs, achieving AUROC 0.772 (African caQTLs) and 0.892 (Yoruban dsQTLs) versus a best DNALM of 0.666 and 0.670, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six DNALMs (Caduceus, DNABERT-2, GENA-LM, HyenaDNA, Mistral-DNA, Nucleotide Transformer) against two ab initio baselines (a 68K-parameter probing-head-like CNN and the 6.6M-parameter ChromBPNet). On the simplest task (cCRE vs. shuffled background), all DNALMs succeed zero-shot, but the small ab initio CNN matches probed DNALMs (0.932 vs. 0.959 paired accuracy for GENA-LM). On cell-type-specific classification, the ChromBPNet-like ab initio model (0.667 overall accuracy) matches the best fine-tuned DNALM (Caduceus, 0.671) and substantially outperforms all probed DNALMs (best: HyenaDNA at 0.587). On chromatin accessibility regression, fine-tuned DNALMs (NT: Spearman r 0.515–0.737) roughly match ChromBPNet (0.540–0.754), but probed DNALMs fall far behind. The most striking gap appears on counterfactual variant effect prediction: ChromBPNet achieves AUROC 0.772 (African) and 0.892 (Yoruban), while the best fine-tuned DNALM (Caduceus) reaches only 0.666 and 0.670—a gap of 10–22 AUROC points. Mistral-DNA (1.6B parameters) is consistently the weakest DNALM across all tasks, while Nucleotide Transformer (500M) is generally the strongest DNALM in fine-tuned settings, yet still loses to the much smaller ChromBPNet on the hardest task.

## Relation to Prior Work

DART-Eval positions itself as a corrective to the existing DNALM evaluation landscape, which the authors argue suffers from three systemic flaws: reliance on surrogate classification tasks rather than biologically meaningful applications, dataset designs lacking compositional controls, and comparisons against weak or outdated baselines. It builds on the general paradigm of evaluating self-supervised sequence models (analogous to protein LM benchmarks) but departs by introducing compositionally-matched negatives via dinucleotide shuffling, counterfactual variant effect prediction, and state-of-the-art ab initio baselines (ChromBPNet) that prior benchmarks omitted. The benchmark also extends motif-level evaluation from the 3–10 TFs analyzed in prior work to 1,443 individual motifs from HOCOMOCO v12, and adds regression tasks for quantitative chromatin accessibility that no prior DNALM benchmark included.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that 6.6M-parameter ab initio CNNs match or exceed 1.6B-parameter fine-tuned DNALMs on most regulatory DNA tasks, and that DNALMs fail particularly badly on counterfactual variant prediction despite matching observational performance—challenges the prevailing assumption that large-scale pre-training is necessary for regulatory genomics. This has direct implications for resource allocation in the field: if simpler supervised models suffice, the enormous compute invested in DNALMs may be better directed toward data curation (balanced sampling of regulatory elements) or annotation-aware training. Open questions include whether DNALMs can be made useful for long-range regulatory interactions, distal enhancer–promoter communication, and cross-species generalization, none of which DART-Eval currently covers.
