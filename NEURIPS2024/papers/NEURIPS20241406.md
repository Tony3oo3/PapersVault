## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks lack fine-grained, continuous difficulty annotations for individual problems, relying instead on coarse categorical labels or pairwise comparisons that fail to capture the full difficulty distribution. This gap prevents systematic profiling of how LLMs generalize from easy to hard tasks across a broad spectrum of complexity, which is essential for understanding model capabilities and guiding training strategies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241406/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes problem text in six domains (math competitions, competitive programming, chess puzzles, and reasoning QAs) paired with performance statistics: human solve rates from official competition reports, submission records with contestant rating histories from Codeforces/Lichess, and sample-wise binary correctness from 5,000+ LLMs on the Open LLM Leaderboard. Outputs are continuous difficulty scores normalized to [0,1] with per-problem uncertainty estimates (from Bayesian IRT or Glicko-2 rating deviation), enabling smooth performance curves and two-dimensional generalization heatmaps (training difficulty × evaluation difficulty) rather than discrete bin comparisons.

## Method

The paper applies two complementary statistical models to estimate per-problem difficulty: a 1PL-with-guessing IRT model (fitted via MCMC/variational Bayes) for datasets where participant ability is assumed static (E2H-AMC, E2H-GSM8K, E2H-ARC, E2H-Winogrande), and the Glicko-2 rating system (which incorporates rating deviation and time-based volatility) for dynamic competitive environments (E2H-Codeforces, E2H-Lichess), where each problem attempt is treated as a discrete game between a contestant and the problem-as-opponent. The key design choice is sourcing difficulty from abundant real-world performance data—official item statistics, submission logs with Elo/Glicko ratings, and thousands of LLM evaluation results—rather than domain-specific heuristics or small expert panels. For easy-to-hard generalization profiling, the authors perform SFT on difficulty-binned training subsets (a=7 bins plus b=1 random baseline), interpolate evaluation curves with an RBF kernel, and subtract the random-baseline 'background performance' to isolate the generalization gain as a function of both training and evaluation difficulty.

## Results

The IRT-based difficulty estimation achieves 94.2% matching accuracy with human preferences on E2H-GSM8K (0.026 avg. per-pair discrepancy), 73.7% on E2H-ARC, and 73.4% on E2H-Winogrande. In contrast, GPT4-Turbo's difficulty rankings achieve only 83.6%, 61.6%, and 59.7% matching accuracy on the same datasets, with substantially higher discrepancies (0.057, 0.137, 0.204). Across all six SoTA LLMs (GPT4-Turbo, Claude3-Opus, Gemini1.5-Pro, Llama3-70B, Mixtral-8x22B, Qwen1.5-110B), performance shows monotonic decline as difficulty increases, validating the difficulty labels. The newly curated datasets (E2H-AMC, E2H-Codeforces, E2H-Lichess) are substantially more challenging than the pre-existing ones, with all models showing significantly lower scores on the hard quantile.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between IRT-estimated difficulty ratings and two alternative difficulty signals: human expert preferences (50 participants, majority vote over 100 pairs per dataset) and GPT4-Turbo rankings (2,000 pairs per dataset with 3 majority votes). IRT consistently outperforms GPT4-Turbo in alignment with human judgment across all three LLM-sourced datasets: on E2H-GSM8K, IRT achieves 94.2% matching accuracy vs. GPT4's 83.6%; on E2H-ARC, 73.7% vs. 61.6%; on E2H-Winogrande, 73.4% vs. 59.7%. The per-pair discrepancy gap is even more pronounced on E2H-Winogrande (0.113 for IRT vs. 0.204 for GPT4), suggesting GPT4 struggles with commonsense reasoning difficulty assessment. The paper attributes the lower alignment on ARC and Winogrande (vs. GSM8K) to the intrinsic vagueness of difficulty in knowledge-based and one-step reasoning tasks, where even human participants reported difficulty ranking problem pairs. Spearman correlations between IRT and GPT4 rankings are moderate on GSM8K (0.612) but weak on ARC (0.218) and Winogrande (0.164), further confirming that LLM-as-judge is not a reliable proxy for human difficulty perception in these domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes two well-established statistical frameworks—Item Response Theory (IRT) from psychometrics and the Glicko-2 rating system from competitive gaming—to assign continuous, numerical difficulty scores to LLM benchmark problems, departing from the categorical or pairwise difficulty labels used in prior benchmarks like MATH, GPQA, and APPS. The IRT formulation follows Rodriguez et al. (2021), who first applied IRT to NLP evaluation, while the Glicko-2 application to problem difficulty (treating each problem as a 'player') is a novel repurposing of the rating system. The easy-to-hard generalization study extends the line of work by Schwarzschild et al. and Hase et al., who studied generalization from easy to hard examples, but is the first to do so with continuous difficulty ratings across multiple domains and LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Easy2Hard-Bench provides the first standardized, continuous difficulty labels across six diverse domains, enabling fine-grained profiling of where LLMs break down and how they generalize across difficulty spectra—something categorical labels cannot support. The preliminary finding that SFT on harder problems yields poor or negative generalization gains (a 'poor scaling law') raises an open question about whether current training paradigms are fundamentally ill-suited for easy-to-hard generalization. Limitations include the small human verification pool (50 participants), the restriction to zero/few-shot evaluation without chain-of-thought, and the preliminary nature of the generalization experiments (small models, SFT only), leaving the broader question of how to train LLMs that scale gracefully with difficulty unresolved.
