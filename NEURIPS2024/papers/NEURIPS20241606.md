## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks either evaluate general language understanding or narrow cybersecurity sub-tasks (e.g., secure code generation, memorization of security facts), leaving no standardized way to assess LLMs on the practical, applied tasks that CTI analysts actually perform—such as attributing threats to actors, scoring vulnerability severity, and mapping attack behaviors to frameworks. This gap makes it impossible to objectively determine whether LLMs can be trusted in operational CTI workflows.

## Input / Output

Each of the five tasks consumes a different text input: a multiple-choice question (CTI-MCQ), a CVE description from NVD (CTI-RCM and CTI-VSP), a malware/threat behavior description (CTI-ATE), or a threat report with actor names replaced by [PLACEHOLDER] tokens (CTI-TAA). Outputs are strictly structured: a single letter, a CWE ID, a CVSS v3.1 vector string (e.g., CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H), comma-separated MITRE technique IDs, or a named threat actor. All evaluations are zero-shot with temperature=0 and top_p=1, and the CTI-ATE prompt includes the full list of Enterprise technique IDs as in-context reference.

## Method

CTIBench constructs five tasks targeting distinct cognitive capabilities—memorization (CTI-MCQ), understanding (CTI-RCM), problem-solving (CTI-VSP, CTI-ATE), and abductive reasoning (CTI-TAA). The MCQ dataset (2,500 questions) is generated by GPT-4o from authoritative sources (MITRE ATT&CK, CWE, NIST, GDPR, STIX/TAXII) and manually validated for correctness and single-answer uniqueness. Practical tasks deliberately use 2024 data beyond the evaluated models' knowledge cutoffs to test generalization rather than recall. Evaluation metrics are task-specific: accuracy for MCQ and RCM, mean absolute deviation on CVSS scores (computed via the Python cvss library to isolate reasoning from arithmetic), Micro-F1 for multi-label technique extraction, and a BFS-based graph traversal over alias/related-actor links for threat attribution scoring. Five models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Gemini-1.5, LLaMA3-70B, LLaMA3-8B) are compared, with additional ablations on description length, knowledge-cutoff timing, and explicit reasoning prompts.

## Results

GPT-4 achieves the highest accuracy on 4 of 5 CTIBench tasks: CTI-MCQ (71.0%), CTI-RCM (72.0%), CTI-ATE (Macro-F1 0.6388), and CTI-TAA (52% correct / 86% plausible accuracy). Gemini-1.5 leads on CTI-VSP with the lowest Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD = 1.09), outperforming GPT-4 (MAD = 1.31). LLAMA3-70B is competitive with Gemini-1.5 on CTI-MCQ (65.7% vs. 65.4%) and CTI-ATE (0.4720 vs. 0.4612), while LLAMA3-8B trails significantly on reasoning-heavy tasks (CTI-RCM 44.7%, CTI-ATE 0.1562).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 dominates four out of five tasks, achieving 71.0% on CTI-MCQ (vs. 65.4% Gemini-1.5, 65.7% LLAMA3-70B), 72.0% on CTI-RCM (vs. 66.6% Gemini-1.5), 0.6388 Macro-F1 on CTI-ATE (vs. 0.4720 LLAMA3-70B), and 86% plausible accuracy on CTI-TAA (vs. 80% LLAMA3-70B). Gemini-1.5 is the sole winner on CTI-VSP with MAD 1.09, beating GPT-4 (1.31) and LLAMA3-70B (1.83), suggesting stronger numerical reasoning for CVSS scoring. LLAMA3-70B, despite being open-source, matches GPT-4 on CTI-TAA correct accuracy (52%) and slightly edges Gemini-1.5 on CTI-MCQ (65.7% vs. 65.4%) and CTI-ATE (0.4720 vs. 0.4612). GPT-3.5 consistently underperforms the larger models, scoring 54.1% on CTI-MCQ and 0.3108 on CTI-ATE, though it remains ahead of LLAMA3-8B on every task. LLAMA3-8B is the clear weakest model, particularly on CTI-RCM (44.7% vs. 65–72% for others) and CTI-ATE (0.1562), indicating that smaller models struggle with nuanced vulnerability reasoning and multi-label technique extraction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by positioning CTI-specific LLM evaluation as a gap not addressed by either general-purpose benchmarks (GLUE, MMLU, HELM) or existing cybersecurity benchmarks (SECURE, CyberSecEval, SevenLLM, SecEval, CyberBench), which the authors argue are too narrow (single industry), too focused on memorization/information extraction, or limited to code generation. Rather than building on a specific prior method, CTIBench repurposes well-established cybersecurity activities—CVE-to-CWE mapping, CVSS scoring, ATT&CK technique extraction, and threat actor attribution—as structured LLM evaluation tasks, drawing on authoritative sources (NIST, MITRE, NVD, CWE) for ground truth. The benchmark explicitly targets four cognitive capabilities (memorization, understanding, problem-solving, reasoning) in a way that prior cybersecurity LLM evaluations have not systematically addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CTIBench provides the first comprehensive, multi-task benchmark for assessing LLM reliability in the broad CTI domain, which is critical given that hallucinated or inaccurate threat intelligence could have real-world consequences for defenders. Key open questions remain: the CTI-TAA dataset is small (50 reports), evaluation is English-only, and the paper acknowledges that the task subset may not capture the full breadth of CTI operations. The finding that models consistently overestimate CVSS severity and that explicit reasoning prompts do not reliably improve MCQ performance raises practical calibration concerns for deploying LLMs in security operations. Future work on multilingual CTI, broader task coverage, and misuse potential (e.g., generating convincing false threat reports) are identified as important directions.
