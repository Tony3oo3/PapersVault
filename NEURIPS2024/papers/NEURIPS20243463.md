## Problem

Standard analysis conventions in closed-loop optogenetics studies—between-group comparisons and within-group laser/no-laser contrasts—cannot test 'micro' longitudinal effects tied to specific treatment sequence patterns (e.g., dose-response, effect dissipation, synergistic consecutive stimulation), and in closed-loop designs they introduce bias through treatment-confounder feedback. No formal causal inference framework existed to estimate these local causal contrasts in the presence of positivity violations inherent to dynamic (closed-loop) treatment policies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes sequential trial-level data (covariates X_t, binary treatment A_t, outcome Y_t) along with known-by-design propensity scores π_t(a;H_t) and availability indicators I_t that encode when treatment is prohibited by the experimental protocol. It outputs HR-MSM regression parameters β whose linear combinations yield interpretable sequential excursion effects (blip, dissipation, dose-response) with asymptotically valid confidence intervals; in the application, propensity scores are plugged in directly rather than estimated, and the working model is a logistic regression on policy-sequence indicators.

## Method

The paper extends history-restricted marginal structural models (HR-MSMs) from static to dynamic treatment regimes by defining counterfactual outcomes under sequences of time-specific deterministic policies d_{Δ,t} = (d_{t-Δ+1}, …, d_t) that are compatible with availability restrictions (i.e., d_t ≡ 0 when I_t = 0, d_t ≡ I_t otherwise). Identification follows from a g-formula argument under consistency, bounded positivity, and sequential randomization, yielding a product IPW estimating equation for the MSM parameters β_0 defined as L2-projection parameters onto a working mean model m. The estimator solves the empirical IPW estimating equation via root-finding, and asymptotic normality is established under Donsker and non-singularity conditions, enabling Wald confidence intervals. For small-sample settings common in optogenetics (n as low as 6 animals), the authors show that HC3-adjusted sandwich variance estimators achieve nominal 95% coverage, while the large-sample sandwich estimator is consistent. The framework handles arbitrary Δ ≥ 1, accommodating treatment sequences longer than one timepoint—a capability absent from existing excursion effect methods restricted to Δ = 1.

## Results

In simulations mimicking closed-loop optogenetics designs with positivity violations, the IPW estimator is unbiased (relative bias → 0 as n or T grows) and achieves nominal 95% CI coverage: HC3-adjusted CIs reach ≥94% coverage even at n=6, T=50, while the large-sample (LS) sandwich estimator reaches 95% at n≥30 or T≥500. On the Markowitz et al. 2023 dopamine optogenetics dataset (n=28 treatment, n=12 control; T≈3612 trials), the standard macro-longitudinal GEE interaction term yields no significant effects for any of the six target poses, whereas the proposed sequential excursion effect HR-MSM (Δ=3) identifies significantly negative laser×group interaction effects for 5 out of 6 poses. A dose-response HR-MSM (Δ=5) further reveals a monotonic increase in target pose odds with each additional stimulation opportunity in the treatment group, significant for 4 of 6 poses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed sequential excursion effect HR-MSM against two baselines on the Markowitz et al. 2023 dopamine optogenetics dataset. Against the standard macro-longitudinal GEE analysis (the approach used in the original Nature paper), the proposed method decisively wins: the GEE interaction term (γ₃) yields no significant effects for any of the six target poses, while the proposed Δ=3 HR-MSM identifies significantly negative laser×group interaction effects for 5 out of 6 poses. Against the availability-conditional estimand of Boruvka et al. 2018 (restricted to Δ=1 policies), the proposed method again wins: the availability-conditional laser×group interaction is significant for only 1 pose, whereas the proposed sequential excursion effect (Δ=3) is significant for 5 poses. In the dose-response analysis (Δ=5), the proposed HR-MSM reveals a clear monotonic dose-response curve (significant for 4/6 poses), while the availability-conditional blip effect (Δ=1) identifies no significant effects for any pose. The authors note that the original study's authors confirmed these findings in personal communication, agreeing that the proposed methods reveal causal effects difficult to uncover without the sequential excursion effect framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends history-restricted marginal structural models (HR-MSMs) from Neugebauer et al. [21], which model mean counterfactuals for proximal treatment sequences in sequentially randomized experiments, to the setting of dynamic treatment regimes with positivity violations. It simultaneously generalizes the excursion effect framework from Boruvka et al. [5]—which estimates causal contrasts only for single-timepoint (Δ=1) interventions in mobile health—to arbitrary sequence lengths (Δ>1) under availability restrictions. The paper departs from prior optogenetics methodology, which relies on between-group GEE comparisons or instrumental variable approaches targeting neural activity, by introducing the first counterfactual-based causal framework for behavioral outcomes in closed-loop optogenetic designs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework reveals that standard macro longitudinal analyses in closed-loop optogenetics can completely obscure treatment effects due to treatment-confounder feedback, where opposing causal effects on intermediate behavior and outcome cancel in observed means—a phenomenon formalized in Appendix B. The application to the Markowitz et al. Nature dataset demonstrates this concretely: no individual pose shows a significant effect under standard GEE, yet the proposed method identifies significant effects for five of six poses. Open questions include extending inference to non-Donsker working models (e.g., deep networks), handling very large Δ computationally, and whether the framework generalizes to more complex multi-valued or continuous treatments. The method also reduces the need for negative-control groups when off-target effects are negligible, potentially lowering animal use.
