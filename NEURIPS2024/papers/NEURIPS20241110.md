## Problem

Standard CNNs rely on local convolutional kernels, which underperform when information must be transported across large spatial distances (e.g., moving a feature from one corner of an image to the opposite corner). Existing spatio-temporal prediction architectures (CNN+RNN hybrids) treat this as a black-box sequence modeling problem, lacking both the ability to model long-range advection efficiently and physical interpretability of the learned dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241110/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The network consumes a short history of j past frames (j ranges from 2 to 10 depending on the dataset) at resolutions from 64×64 (Moving MNIST) to 154×512 RGB (KITTI) or 128×128 grayscale (SWE, CloudCast). The input is first embedded into a higher-dimensional latent space via an MLP, evolved through m ADR layers, then projected back to the original channel count. An optional UNet denoiser is applied to the final output. Prediction horizons range from 1 to 50 future steps, with the displacement field at each layer computed from the evolving latent representation rather than the raw input.

## Method

ADRNet reformulates spatio-temporal prediction as a discretized Reaction-Advection-Diffusion PDE solved via operator splitting, yielding a network where each layer composes three operations: a reaction step (1×1 convolution / MLP for pointwise channel mixing), a diffusion step (5-point Laplacian implemented as group convolution, optionally merged with reaction into a single 3×3 conv), and an advection step (the key novelty). The advection step uses a semi-Lagrangian push operator: a residual convolutional network predicts a per-pixel displacement field from the current latent state, and features are then transported to their displaced locations via bilinear interpolation (PyTorch's grid_sample), enabling non-local, mass-preserving information transfer in a single step. This contrasts with standard convolutions, which require many stacked layers or pooling/upsampling to achieve equivalent receptive fields. The full architecture is trained end-to-end with supervised loss, and the authors show via ablation that the advection operator outperforms dilated convolutions for long-range transport tasks.

## Results

On PDEBench-SWE, ADRNet achieves an NRMSE of 1.3×10⁻⁴, outperforming the next-best method (MPP-AVIT-L, 2.2×10⁻³) by nearly an order of magnitude. On CloudCast, ADRNet reaches SSIM 0.83 and PSNR 38.17 dB, substantially exceeding AE-ConvLSTM (SSIM 0.66, PSNR 8.06). For long-range SWE prediction (10→50 steps), ADRNet maintains nRMSE of 7.8×10⁻⁷ versus FNO's 1.3×10⁻⁶. On Moving MNIST, ADRNet reports MSE 16.1 and MAE 50.3, close to the best method IAM4VP (15.3/49.2), while on KITTI it achieves the lowest LPIPS (7.54 at t+1, 9.26 at t+3) among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On PDEBench-SWE, ADRNet (NRMSE 1.3×10⁻⁴) decisively outperforms all seven baselines, including MPP-AVIT-L (2.2×10⁻³), FNO (4.4×10⁻³), and UNet (8.3×10⁻²), with errors near machine precision. On CloudCast, ADRNet (SSIM 0.83, PSNR 38.17) dominates AE-ConvLSTM (0.66/8.06), MD-GAN (0.60/7.83), TVL1 (0.58/7.50), and Persistent (0.55/7.41) by large margins. On Moving MNIST, ADRNet (MSE 16.1) slightly trails IAM4VP (15.3) but beats SwinLSTM (17.7), TAU (19.8), CrevNet (22.3), SimVP (23.8), and PhyDNet (24.4). On KITTI, ADRNet achieves the best LPIPS (7.54/9.26) but is slightly behind DMVFN (10.74/19.27) on MS-SSIM at t+1 (85.86 vs. 88.53), though it leads at t+3 (83.62 vs. 78.01). On Navier-Stokes, ADRNet (nMSE 0.0168) ranks second behind MPP-AVIT-L (0.0142) but far ahead of FNO (0.243) and UNet (1.67). On KTH Action and TaxiBJ, ADRNet underperforms the top methods (TAU, SwinLSTM, IAM4VP), consistent with the authors' noted limitation that the method lacks generative power for video synthesis tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the advection-diffusion-reaction (ADR) framework previously applied to graph neural networks (ADR-GNN, Eliasof et al. 2023) to convolutional neural networks on regular grids, introducing a semi-Lagrangian push operator as a new learnable layer. The push operator draws directly from semi-Lagrangian advection methods in computational fluid dynamics (Lentine et al. 2011), repurposing them as a sparse, non-local linear operation within a CNN. The broader PDE-motivated network design lineage (Ruthotto & Haber 2018, Haber et al. 2019) provides the conceptual bridge between operator splitting for PDEs and layered network architectures, but the specific combination of a learned displacement field with a semi-Lagrangian interpolation step as a first-class CNN layer is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and addresses a fundamental limitation of CNNs: their inability to transport information across large spatial distances in a single operation, which is critical for advection-dominated physical processes. The dramatic performance gaps on scientific datasets (e.g., ~17× lower NRMSE on SWE, ~30 dB higher PSNR on CloudCast vs. best competitors) suggest that physics-inspired inductive biases can outperform purely data-driven architectures when the underlying dynamics are well-structured. However, the method's advantage diminishes on tasks requiring generative power (KTH Action, TaxiBJ), leaving open the question of how to combine the ADR framework with generative components for open-world video prediction. The push operator also offers a new, interpretable primitive for the CNN toolkit that could be useful beyond the specific ADR formulation.
