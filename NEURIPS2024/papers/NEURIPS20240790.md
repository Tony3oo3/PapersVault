## Problem

Existing LLM evaluation benchmarks either assess generalist abilities on non-professional texts or repurpose traditional NLP datasets from a computer-centric perspective, failing to capture the complexity of judicial cognition, legal reasoning, and ethical judgment required in real legal practice. No standardized, comprehensive Chinese legal benchmark existed that organized tasks by cognitive ability levels, covered legal ethics, and provided unified evaluation metrics across diverse legal tasks.

## Input / Output

Each task presents a standardized instruction prompt followed by a legal query (case description, legal text, or exam question) and four multiple-choice options (or an open-ended prompt for Generation tasks). In few-shot mode, three task-specific examples are prepended to the input. Outputs are parsed as letter selections for accuracy scoring or free-text for Rouge-L scoring, with all 23 tasks unified into a single JSON format. The longest task (Similar Case Identification) averages 4,502 characters, exceeding the context window of some evaluated models, requiring middle-truncation of inputs.

## Method

The core contribution is the LexAbility Taxonomy, a six-level hierarchy (Memorization → Understanding → Logic Inference → Discrimination → Generation → Ethic) inspired by Bloom's taxonomy and real-world legal workflows, which organizes 23 tasks and 14,150 questions into a coherent evaluation framework. Data is sourced from three channels: reformatted existing datasets (CAIL 2018–2022, JEC-QA, LeCaRD), curated questions from the National Uniform Legal Profession Qualification Examination, and 6,250 newly annotated questions crafted by 18 legal experts (all bar-exam qualified, with PhD-level cross-checking at a 90% approval threshold). Evaluation is conducted in both zero-shot and few-shot (3 examples) settings at temperature 0, using Accuracy for all multiple-choice tasks and Rouge-L for the four Generation tasks, with 38 models (29 general LLMs and 9 legal-specific LLMs) benchmarked under identical protocols.

## Results

On the LexEval benchmark (23 tasks, 14,150 questions across 6 legal cognitive ability levels), GPT-4 achieves the highest zero-shot average score of 52.4%, followed by Qwen-14B-Chat (48.6%) and Qwen-7B-Chat (44.8%). Even the best model scores below 40% on most Memorization tasks (e.g., GPT-4: 34.0% on Legal Concept, 14.0% on Legal Evolution) and below 40% on Discrimination tasks. Under few-shot settings, GPT-4 improves slightly to 53.7% average, while some open-source models (e.g., Qwen-14B-Chat) show unstable or degraded performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 leads all 38 evaluated models with a 52.4% zero-shot average, outperforming the best open-source model Qwen-14B-Chat (48.6%) by 3.8 points and ChatGPT (39.6%) by 12.8 points. Surprisingly, legal-specific LLMs underperform general LLMs: ChatLaw-33B (30.0%) and Fuzi-Mingcha (30.2%) rank 10th and 9th respectively, trailing even mid-tier general models like ChatGLM3 (35.8%) and Baichuan-13B-Chat (36.4%). Among open-source models, larger parameter counts and chat-tuned variants consistently outperform their base counterparts (e.g., Qwen-14B-Chat > Qwen-7B-Chat; Baichuan-13B-Chat > Baichuan-13B-base), suggesting instruction-following alignment is critical. Across ability levels, all models struggle most with Memorization (Legal Evolution: 6.7–19.7%) and Discrimination (Similar Case Identification: 3.6–37.0%), while Understanding tasks (e.g., Reading Comprehension: 58–95%) and Logic Inference (Cause Prediction: 54.9–80.3%) show relatively stronger performance. Few-shot prompting yields only marginal and inconsistent gains, with GPT-4 improving from 52.4% to 53.7% while Qwen-14B-Chat drops from 48.6% to 49.9% on some sub-tasks, indicating in-context learning is not an effective strategy for injecting legal knowledge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing a Legal Cognitive Ability Taxonomy (six levels from Memorization to Ethic) inspired by Bloom's taxonomy, which no prior Chinese legal benchmark had attempted. It departs from existing legal benchmarks (LawBench, LAiW) that repurpose traditional NLP datasets from a computer-centric perspective, and from general benchmarks (C-Eval, CMMLU) that include only a small legal subset without structured ability organization. The paper also extends beyond prior work by incorporating ethics-specific tasks (bias, morality, privacy) and exam-based questions from the National Uniform Legal Profession Qualification Examination, dimensions largely absent from earlier evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the largest Chinese legal benchmark (14,150 questions, 23 tasks), LexEval provides a standardized reference point that the community lacked, and its finding that even GPT-4 achieves only 52.4% average accuracy underscores that current LLMs are far from deployment-ready in legal practice. The observation that legal-specific LLMs do not consistently outperform general LLMs challenges the assumption that domain-specific pre-training is sufficient and points toward better training objectives. Key open questions include how to improve memorization of legal evolution, how to extend the benchmark to Case Law systems, and whether Rouge-L adequately captures generation quality in legal contexts.
