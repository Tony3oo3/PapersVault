## Problem

Estimating the nadir objective vector is essential for normalizing the objective space and guiding search in multi-objective optimization, yet existing methods are fundamentally limited: exact methods are restricted to discrete MOPs with integer objective values, while heuristic methods (population-based or extreme-point-based) fail when the feasible objective region is irregular, concave, or contains a weakly Pareto-optimal boundary. No general method with theoretical guarantees exists for continuous black-box MOPs with arbitrary PF shapes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a black-box MOP (n-dimensional decision space Ω ⊂ ℝⁿ with m objective functions) and a user-defined trade-off parameter μ > 0 that bounds the acceptable exchange rate between objectives; μ determines the cone-domination parameter α = m/((m−1)μ + m). The output is a single m-dimensional estimated nadir objective vector ẑⁿᵃᵈ, extracted as the component-wise maximum over the final solution set. The algorithm operates purely through function evaluations with a fixed budget (FE_max), requiring no gradient information or analytical structure of the objectives.

## Method

BDNE introduces a boundary subproblem that scalarizes the MOP using a boundary weight vector wⁱ (with wᵢⁱ = 0) applied to a cone-domination-transformed objective vector, guaranteeing Pareto-optimality of the solution under mild conditions (Theorem 1). For each objective i, a bilevel optimization problem (BLOP) is formulated: the upper level searches over boundary weight vectors in ℝ^{m−2} to maximize the i-th objective value (penalized by a flat-landscape distance term), while the lower level solves the corresponding boundary subproblem over the original decision space. The key insight is that the critical point of objective i is the unique solution to a specific boundary subproblem (Theorem 2), so the bilevel structure identifies it by comparing optimal solutions across weight vectors. In practice, m CMA-ES instances handle the upper levels while a shared MOEA (EC-NSGA-II) collaboratively solves all lower-level subproblems using a rank-based selection over the boundary subproblem values, with reference points iteratively updated to approximate zⁱᵈᵉ and zⁿᵃᵈ.

## Results

BDNE achieves an average rank of 1.07 across 28 instances (3, 5, and 8 objectives), winning on 27/28 instances with the error metric E = √Σ((z_i^nad − ẑ_i^nad)/(z_i^nad − z_i^ide))². On the proposed TN1–TN4 problems with irregular feasible objective regions, BDNE attains errors below 3% (e.g., 0.000172 on TN2-3obj, 0.0268 on TN4-8obj), while ECR-NSGA-II errors range from 1.35 to 85.1 and DNPE errors range from 0.17 to 0.77. On standard benchmarks (DTLZ2, mDTLZ2, IMOP4, IMOP6), BDNE achieves near-machine-precision errors (e.g., 1.81e-10 on DTLZ2-3obj, 4.16e-17 on IMOP4). The only instance where BDNE does not rank first is CRE5-3-1 (5-obj), where the approximate true PF limits all methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BDNE is compared against two primary baselines in the main experiments: ECR-NSGA-II (SF1-based, [54]) and DNPE (EP6-based, [34]), plus nine additional heuristic methods (SF2–SF4, EP1–EP5) in the appendix. ECR-NSGA-II is the weakest baseline, losing on 27/28 instances with errors up to 85.1 on ML-DMP (5-obj) due to dominance-resistant solutions on the WPB. DNPE ranks second overall (wins on 2/28: ML-DMP-3obj and CRE5-3-1-5obj) and is competitive on problems with simple convex PFs (DTLZ2, mDTLZ2, IMOP6) but degrades severely on irregular or concave PFs (TN2, TN4, DTLZ5, IMOP4). Among the nine appendix baselines, EP1 (pay-off table, [29]) is the closest competitor, winning on 9 instances but with small gaps (Δ < 0.03), while BDNE dominates EP1 by margins exceeding 1.5 on 5/8-objective DTLZ2 and 12.8 on 5-objective ML-DMP. All SF-based methods (SF2–SF4) and remaining EP-based methods (EP2–EP5) lose to BDNE on 25–28 instances, confirming that BDNE's bilevel boundary decomposition approach provides a consistent and substantial advantage over both straightforward and extreme-point-based heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

BDNE builds on the cone domination concept from Ramirez-Atencia et al. (GECCO 2017) and the modified weighted Tchebycheff metric from Kaliszewski (1987), repurposing them into a novel boundary subproblem formulation that scalarizes the MOP along the Pareto front boundary. It departs from prior exact methods (Kirlik & Sayin 2015) by replacing exhaustive discrete search with a bilevel optimization framework applicable to continuous black-box problems, and from heuristic methods (ECR-NSGA-II, DNPE, pay-off table variants) by providing theoretical guarantees via a connection between cone optimality and proper Pareto optimality (Geoffrion 1968). The bilevel structure—upper level searching boundary weight vectors via CMA-ES, lower level solving boundary subproblems via an MOEA—is a new architectural choice not present in prior nadir estimation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The nadir objective vector underpins normalization, search guidance, and preference-based decision-making across virtually all MOP solvers, yet existing estimation methods either require discrete search spaces or fail on irregular/concave Pareto fronts and weakly Pareto-optimal boundaries. BDNE's theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1–6) clarify exactly when the method recovers the true nadir vector, and the user-defined trade-off parameter μ offers a principled way to incorporate decision-maker preferences. Key limitations include the longer runtime compared to heuristic baselines (roughly 2–3×), the requirement that critical points be properly Pareto-optimal (or that μ be set sufficiently large), and the fact that BDNE is currently an independent pre-estimation step rather than being tightly integrated into iterative MOEA loops—integration is flagged as future work with only pilot results shown.
