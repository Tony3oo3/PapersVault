## Problem

While modern generative models can manipulate abstract concepts in their training data, it remains unclear what determines which concepts a model learns, the order of that learning, and when the model acquires the capability to flexibly compose concepts for out-of-distribution generation. This paper asks whether there is a structured, predictable geometry to concept learning dynamics and whether models possess latent compositional capabilities that are not yet accessible through standard input prompting.

## Input / Output

The model takes a continuous conditioning vector h (e.g., RGB values for color, a scalar for size) specifying a combination of discrete concept values and produces 32×32 RGB images via a variational diffusion model. The primary analytical output is not the image itself but the model's trajectory in a multidimensional concept space, where each axis corresponds to one concept and a point represents a concept class. Evaluation uses U-Net-based classifier probes trained on the data-generating process to independently measure per-concept accuracy of generated samples, enabling quantitative tracking of learning dynamics without human annotation.

## Method

The paper introduces a concept space framework in which each axis of an abstract coordinate system corresponds to an independently distributed concept in the data-generating process, and defines concept signal as the sensitivity of the data-generating process to changes in each concept variable. Using controlled synthetic datasets of 2D objects (varying color, size, shape) and a variational diffusion model (U-Net with learned noise schedule), the authors show that concept signal dictates the speed and order of concept learning, and that learning trajectories in concept space exhibit a characteristic sudden turn from concept memorization to out-of-distribution generalization. A minimal energy-based toy model (Eq. 1) reproduces the observed clockwise/counterclockwise trajectory geometry and decomposes learning into two phases: (P1) acquisition of a hidden capability, where latent interventions (linear projection in activation space or overprompting in input space) can elicit correct OOD outputs before naive prompting succeeds, and (P2) alignment of the input space to the learned concept representations. The authors further show that underspecification in conditioning (masked prompts) shifts target directions in concept space and delays OOD generalization, while the hidden capability still emerges at a consistent point detectable via alternative prompting protocols.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that alternative prompting protocols (linear latent intervention and overprompting) elicit OOD compositional generalization capabilities approximately 2,000 gradient steps earlier than naive input prompting (e.g., ~6,000 vs. ~8,000 steps for the green-curve model in Fig. 6), and this transition point is consistent across seeds. Concept signal directly controls learning speed: stronger pixel-level separation between concept values (e.g., higher RGB contrast for color, larger size difference) yields faster concept acquisition. On CelebA, latent interventions reveal OOD generalization capability (Female, With Hat) at ~5×10⁵ gradient steps while naive prompting still fails, confirming the hidden-capability hypothesis on realistic data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new generative method to benchmark against competing architectures; instead, it studies learning dynamics of a fixed variational diffusion model under controlled conditions. The key 'comparisons' are between prompting protocols: naive input prompting, linear latent intervention in activation space, and overprompting in input space. All three protocols elicit the OOD capability at approximately the same gradient step (~6,000), but naive prompting alone only succeeds in some seeds after ~8,000 steps, while latent intervention and overprompting succeed consistently across all five seeds (Fig. 6, Fig. 17). The paper also compares learning dynamics under varying concept signal strengths, showing that higher signal accelerates the corresponding concept's learning and shifts the memorization-to-generalization transition. On CelebA (Fig. 7), latent intervention achieves near-100% accuracy for the OOD class (Female, With Hat) while naive prompting remains below 50% at 2M gradient steps, demonstrating a clear gap between competence and performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on compositional generalization in diffusion models initiated by Okawa et al. [30], which first observed multiplicative emergence of compositional abilities in synthetic settings. It draws on the disentangled representation learning literature (Locatello et al., Kim & Mnih) for its formal notion of concepts as latent factors, and on cognitive science's competence-versus-performance distinction to frame its central hypothesis. The key departure is the introduction of the 'concept space' as a formal analytical coordinate system and 'concept signal' as a measurable quantity governing learning speed, together with a phenomenological energy-function toy model that captures the geometry of learning trajectories—tools absent from prior work on concept learning or grokking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-phase decomposition—where a model acquires the latent capability to manipulate concepts (verifiable via interventions) before naive prompting can elicit it—provides a concrete mechanism for the 'hidden capabilities' phenomenon observed anecdotally in large generative models. If the hypothesis generalizes, it has direct implications for AI safety evaluation: standard prompting-based benchmarks may systematically underestimate a model's true competence. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic data with 2–3 binary concepts, the assumption of linearly embedded concepts, and the fact that CelebA experiments do not reach full OOD generalization within the compute budget, leaving the broader claim as a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated result.
