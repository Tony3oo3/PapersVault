## Problem

Existing long-tailed learning benchmarks are dominated by image classification and lack a systematic framework to characterize the extent of long-tailedness, account for data complexity across modalities (tabular, sequential, grid, relational), or evaluate algorithm performance across heterogeneous tasks. This makes it difficult to determine which algorithms are appropriate for a given dataset and task, and whether an algorithm addresses data imbalance, extreme category counts, or both.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242326/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 17 real-world datasets spanning four modalities: tabular features (Glass, Abalone, Housing), sequential text (EURLEX-4K, AmazonCat-13K, Wiki10-31K), grid images (ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, iNaturalist, CIFAR-LT, LVIS), and relational graphs (Cora-Full, Wiki, Email, Amazon-Clothing, Amazon-Electronics). Outputs are task-specific—class labels, multi-label rankings, segmentation masks, node labels, or continuous regression values—and are scored using 10 metrics including accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, recall, MAP, MAE, MSE, Pearson correlation, and running time. A novel Pareto-LT Ratio metric is introduced alongside Imbalance Factor and Gini Coefficient to jointly capture both distribution skewness and the extreme number of categories.

## Method

HEROLT is a benchmarking framework organized around three angles: (A1) characterizing long-tailedness via three complementary metrics (IF for head-to-tail ratio, Gini for overall imbalance, and the proposed Pareto-LT Ratio for category-extremity), (A2) spanning four data modalities with 17 datasets, and (A3) covering six heterogeneous tasks (object recognition, multi-label text classification, image classification, instance segmentation, node classification, regression). The framework evaluates 18 state-of-the-art algorithms—spanning data augmentation (SMOTE, GraphSMOTE), meta-learning (OLTR, BALMS), decoupled training (Decoupling, MiSLAS), contrastive learning (PaCo), causal inference (TDE), and GNN-based methods (Tail-GNN, LTE4G)—under unified hyperparameter settings with 10 independent runs. Across 315 experiments, the key finding is that no single algorithm or technique family (meta-learning, mixup, contrastive learning, decoupling) consistently dominates, and the three-angle taxonomy proves more informative for algorithm selection than technique-based categorization.

## Results

Across 315 experiments spanning 18 algorithms, 17 datasets, 6 tasks, and 4 data modalities, no single algorithm statistically outperforms all others across every task and domain. On natural long-tailed image classification, PaCo achieves the best overall accuracy of 58.3% on ImageNet-LT (surpassing the runner-up by 16.37%) and MiSLAS reaches 71.6% on iNatural 2018. On node classification, Tail-GNN attains 63.8% accuracy on Cora-Full and 69.2% on Amazon-Clothing, outperforming GraphSMOTE, ImGAGN, and LTE4G. On multi-label text classification, XR-Transformer and X-Transformer are comparably strong (e.g., 88.2% Acc@1 on EURLEX-4K), while XR-Linear is >100× faster but consistently weaker.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark reveals that no single technique (meta-learning, decoupled training, mixup, or contrastive learning) consistently dominates across all tasks and datasets. On image classification, PaCo (contrastive learning) and MiSLAS (decoupled + mixup) are the strongest: PaCo leads on ImageNet-LT (58.3% vs. 50.1% for TDE) and Places-LT (41.4% vs. 40.5% for MiSLAS), while MiSLAS leads on iNatural 2018 (71.6% vs. 69.7% for BBN). On node classification, Tail-GNN outperforms GraphSMOTE by 3.3% accuracy on Cora-Full (63.8% vs. 60.5%) and by 2.9% on Amazon-Clothing (69.2% vs. 66.3%), but suffers OOM on the largest dataset (Amazon-Electronics), where LTE4G (55.8%) and GraphSMOTE (56.2%) remain viable. On multi-label text classification, XR-Transformer and X-Transformer are nearly tied (88.2% vs. 87.0% Acc@1 on EURLEX-4K), both substantially outperforming XR-Linear (82.1%), though XR-Linear is >100× faster. On tabular object recognition, SMOTE (upsampling) beats NearMiss (downsampling) by 85.43% in balanced accuracy on Abalone (19.1% vs. 10.3%), and on regression, SmoteR outperforms SMOGN across all MAE/MSE/Pearson/GM metrics on the Housing dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior long-tailed learning surveys (Fang et al. 2023, Fu et al. 2022, Zhang et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2022) that focused almost exclusively on visual recognition and categorized methods by technique (re-sampling, cost-sensitive loss, decoupled training). Rather than proposing a new algorithm, HEROLT synthesizes 18 existing state-of-the-art methods across 6 tasks and 4 data modalities under a unified evaluation protocol, introducing the Pareto-LT Ratio as a new metric that captures both data imbalance and extreme category count—two properties that prior metrics (Imbalance Factor, Gini Coefficient) conflate or ignore. The three benchmarking angles (long-tailedness characterization, data complexity, task heterogeneity) reframe the field's organizing principle from 'which technique works best' to 'which algorithm fits which scenario.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no single algorithm or technique (meta-learning, contrastive learning, decoupled training, mixup) statistically dominates across all tasks and domains—challenges the implicit assumption in the literature that newer, more sophisticated methods should universally outperform simpler baselines, and shifts the practical question toward scenario-dependent algorithm selection. The Pareto-LT Ratio provides a principled way to diagnose whether a dataset's challenge is primarily imbalance-driven or category-count-driven, enabling more informed method selection. Open questions highlighted include the lack of theoretical tools for analyzing generalization in long-tailed models, the need for multi-modal and multi-task long-tailed learning frameworks, and the underexplored application to domains like antibiotic resistance gene prediction. Limitations include small algorithm pools for some tasks (only 2 methods for regression and object recognition) and scalability issues (e.g., Tail-GNN OOM on larger graphs).
