## Problem

Existing deepfake detection benchmarks rely on a narrow set of forgery methods (e.g., only 2 swapping and 2 reenactment methods in FF++), often use outdated techniques from several years ago, and evaluate detectors on a single forgery type in isolation. This 'golden compass' protocol masks a critical generalization gap: detectors that appear state-of-the-art on one dataset fail to handle the diverse, modern deepfakes encountered in the real world, where non-blending methods (SimSwap, FaceDancer), audio-driven reenactment (SadTalker), and diffusion-based synthesis (Stable Diffusion, PixArt-α) are prevalent.

## Input / Output

The DF40 dataset provides over 0.1M fake video clips (for face-swapping and face-reenactment) and over 1M fake images (for entire face synthesis and face editing), all paired with corresponding real data from FF++ (c23) and Celeb-DF. Detectors consume RGB face images or video clips and produce a binary real/fake prediction, with performance measured as AUC. The four evaluation protocols systematically vary forgery type (FS, FR, EFS, FE) and data domain (FF, CDF, unknown) to stress-test generalization, including a 'one-verse-all' protocol where a model trained on a single method must detect all 39 others.

## Method

DF40 implements 40 distinct deepfake generation techniques spanning four categories: 10 face-swapping (from classical FSGAN/FaceSwap to disentangled methods like SimSwap and BlendFace, plus the popular DeepFaceLab), 13 face-reenactment (image-driven, audio-driven, and text-driven methods including HeyGen), 12 entire face synthesis (GAN-based like StyleGAN3 and diffusion-based like DiT, PixArt-α, and Stable Diffusion 2.1), and 5 face editing (StyleGAN-based and diffusion-based). EFS generators are fine-tuned on FF++/CDF real data to ensure domain consistency. The paper then conducts over 2,000 evaluations using 8 representative detectors (Xception, CLIP-base/large, SRM, SPSL, RECCE, RFM, SBI, CFDA) under 4 standardized protocols, revealing 7 key findings: asymmetric performance drops between forgery types (fake region matters), SoTA detectors offering no consistent advantage over the Xception baseline, CLIP's pre-training providing the strongest generalization, and the combined effect of domain and forgery method shifts causing the largest performance degradation.

## Results

Trained on DF40(FF) (combined FS+FR+EFS from FF++ domain), CLIP-large achieves the highest average AUC of 0.898 across 12 test sets (including unknown forgeries like DeepFaceLab, HeyGen, MidJourney-6, and face-editing methods), compared to 0.814 for CLIP-base and 0.644 for Xception. On the non-face GenImage benchmark, CLIP-large reaches 0.746 AUC while Xception only achieves 0.535 (near chance). Across all four evaluation protocols (cross-forgery, cross-domain, unknown-domain, and One-Verse-All), the four SoTA detectors (SRM, SPSL, RECCE, RFM) show no consistent advantage over the plain Xception baseline, with average AUC differences typically under 2 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP-large dominates all comparisons, outperforming Xception by 25.4 points in average AUC (0.898 vs. 0.644) and CLIP-base by 8.4 points (0.898 vs. 0.814) when trained on DF40(FF). The four SoTA detectors (SRM, SPSL, RECCE, RFM) all use Xception as backbone and achieve results within ~1–2 AUC points of the plain Xception baseline across all four protocols, indicating their architectural additions provide no meaningful generalization benefit on diverse forgeries. The blending-based SBI detector (trained only on pseudo-fakes without real deepfake data) achieves 0.514–0.734 AUC depending on protocol, competitive on same-domain tasks but degrading sharply on unknown forgeries. Notably, CFDA (ECCV 2024), the latest SoTA that leads on prior datasets like FF++ and CDF, performs comparably or worse than simpler baselines on DF40, suggesting its advantage was dataset-specific rather than a genuine generalization improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DeepfakeBench [90] standardized evaluation framework but fundamentally departs from prior deepfake detection datasets (FF++, CDF, DFDC, ForgeryNet) that typically contain only 1–15 forgery methods, are dominated by blending-based face-swapping, and rely on outdated generators. DF40 introduces 40 techniques spanning four categories (face-swapping, face-reenactment, entire face synthesis, face editing) and incorporates 2024-era AIGC methods (PixArt-α, DiT, Stable Diffusion) alongside real-world software (DeepFaceLab, HeyGen, MidJourney), addressing what the authors identify as the 'primary culprit'—dataset limitations—rather than proposing a new detection architecture. The four evaluation protocols (cross-forgery, cross-domain, unknown-domain, one-verse-all) extend the prior single-type train/test paradigm into a more realistic open-set setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark is likely to become the de facto standard for evaluating next-generation deepfake detectors, given its scale (1M+ images, 0.1M+ video clips), diversity (10× FF++), and inclusion of the latest generators. The finding that well-designed SoTA detectors (SRM, SPSL, RECCE, RFM) fail to consistently outperform the plain Xception baseline, and that the latest CFDA (ECCV'24) performs comparably or worse on DF40, challenges the field's assumption that architectural sophistication alone drives generalization. Open questions remain about the role of blending data, how to jointly learn from many heterogeneous forgeries without overfitting, whether type-level (FS/FR/EFS/FE) classification is more useful than instance-level, and how to build truly domain-invariant detectors. A stated limitation is the lack of deep video-level temporal analysis.
