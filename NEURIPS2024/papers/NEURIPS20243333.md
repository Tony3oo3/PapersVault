## Problem

Machine unlearning is widely adopted to satisfy data-deletion requests, yet the privacy risks it introduces have been largely overlooked. The common assumption is that simple models (e.g., linear regression) pose negligible privacy risk, but this paper shows that the unlearning operation itself—by exposing two model states that differ only in whether one sample was used—creates a powerful attack surface that enables near-perfect reconstruction of the deleted data point, even from the simplest model classes.

## Input / Output

The attacker receives the full parameter vectors of the model before deletion (β⁺) and after deletion (β⁻), a public dataset (X_pub, y_pub) drawn from the same distribution but disjoint from the private training set, and knowledge of the loss function and any fixed embedding function φ. The output is the reconstructed deleted sample (x̃, ỹ), evaluated by cosine similarity to the true deleted point. Notably, the attacker has no access to the private training set, the regularization strength λ, or the deleted sample's label; the public data is used solely to estimate the covariance matrix or Hessian needed to invert the parameter difference back into feature space.

## Method

The key insight is that the parameter difference β⁺ − β⁻, when multiplied by the regularized covariance matrix C (or, in the general case, the Hessian H), is proportional to the deleted sample's feature vector x. For linear regression this relationship is exact via the Sherman-Morrison formula, yielding a closed-form reconstruction x̃ = Ĉ(β⁺ − β⁻) normalized by the bias coordinate. For arbitrary loss functions and architectures, the paper uses a second-order Newton's method approximation to show that nH(β⁺ − β⁻) ≈ −∇ℓ(β⁺; x, y), reducing the problem to estimating the Hessian from public data and then recovering the sample from its gradient. When a fixed embedding φ is present, the attack first reconstructs the embedding z̃ and then solves an inverse problem to recover the original input. For multiclass classification, the deleted label is inferred by identifying the bias coordinate with the most negative gradient, exploiting the asymmetric structure of the cross-entropy loss.

## Results

On ACS Income regression, HRec achieves near-perfect reconstruction of deleted samples (cosine similarity ≈ 1) for both raw-feature ridge regression and ridge regression over 4096 random Fourier features, across three states (NY, CA, TX). On image datasets (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-10), HRec consistently dominates the Avg and MaxDiff baselines in cosine similarity CDF curves across three model architectures (linear cross-entropy, RBF ridge, RBF cross-entropy), with the advantage being most pronounced on the denser CIFAR-10 dataset. For binary classification (logistic regression, SVM) on ACS Income, HRec outperforms baselines across all settings, though reconstruction is slightly less perfect due to Newton's method approximation errors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HRec outperforms both the Avg baseline (mean of public samples) and the MaxDiff baseline (public sample with largest prediction shift) across every dataset and model architecture tested, with the gap widening on higher-dimensional data (CIFAR-10 over MNIST/Fashion-MNIST). On ACS Income tabular data, HRec achieves near-perfect reconstruction while both baselines remain far from the true sample. In the limited comparison with Updates-Leak (Salem et al., 2020) on CIFAR-10 with cross-entropy over 4096 random Fourier features, HRec achieves higher cosine similarity, though the authors note this is not a direct comparison since Updates-Leak attacks a single-gradient-step update rather than a full retraining. For binary classification with random Fourier features, HRec outperforms all baselines on logistic regression but only matches MaxDiff on SVM, suggesting the Newton approximation introduces more error for non-smooth losses. Overall, the paper demonstrates that even the simplest public-data baselines are far outperformed by the Hessian-based reconstruction, confirming that the parameter difference between pre- and post-deletion models is highly informative about the deleted sample.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper connects two previously separate literatures: gradient-based data reconstruction (Zhu et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2020) and influence functions / Newton's method approximations for model updates (Koh & Liang, 2017; Izzo et al., 2021). The key conceptual bridge is showing that the parameter difference between pre- and post-deletion models is (for linear models, exactly; for general models, approximately) proportional to the gradient of the deleted sample, thereby reducing the unlearning-reconstruction problem to the well-studied gradient-reconstruction problem. It departs from prior unlearning-privacy work (Chen et al., 2021) which only demonstrated membership inference vulnerabilities, and from Updates-Leak (Salem et al., 2020) which assumed black-box API access and attacked single-step updates rather than full retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the standard machine-unlearning guarantee (indistinguishability from retraining without the deleted point) is fundamentally insufficient for privacy: the very act of releasing both pre- and post-deletion models leaks the deleted sample with near-perfect accuracy, even for linear regression. This motivates stronger protections such as differential privacy on the sequence of released models (as advocated by Chourasia et al., 2022; Cohen et al., 2023). Open questions include whether the attack degrades gracefully for approximate (non-retraining) unlearning methods, how it scales to deep architectures where Hessian estimation becomes intractable, and whether practical mitigations exist that preserve unlearning utility without full differential privacy.
