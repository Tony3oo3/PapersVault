## Problem

Evolving domain generalization (EDG) aims to predict on future unseen domains whose distributions shift continuously over time, but existing EDG methods assume all source domains are simultaneously available. In the more realistic domain-incremental setting, where source domains arrive sequentially and historical data is inaccessible, the problem of generalizing to future domains remains largely open. The closest prior work, EvoS, models evolution at the feature level under a Gaussian distribution assumption and suffers from error accumulation across consecutive target domains.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method consumes unlabeled target-domain samples (no labels required) to compute a prototype matrix via soft-averaged predictions from stored historical classifiers, and uses the FIFO queue of past classifier weights (each a C×d_f matrix) as reference points. The conditional diffusion model generates multiple residual weight matrices (C×d_f) per reference point, which are added to the reference points and averaged into a single ensemble classifier. The final output is a set of class labels for the target domain, with the ensemble classifier serving as the prediction head on top of a frozen shared feature encoder.

## Method

W-Diff trains a conditional diffusion model (U-Net with hybrid cross-attention and input-side conditioning) to generate residual classifier weights ΔW = W_current − W_historical, conditioned on the concatenation of a historical reference-point weight matrix and the current domain's prototype matrix. During domain-incremental training, a FIFO queue stores the best-performing classifier weights from recent domains as reference points, while a second FIFO queue caches updated classifier weights and prototype matrices after a warm-up phase to form diffusion training pairs. A domain-shared feature encoder is simultaneously learned via a KL-divergence consistency loss that forces predictions from all stored classifiers to agree, restricting domain-specific adaptation to the classifier head. At inference, the diffusion model generates M_g residual weights per reference point (1000 denoising steps each), and the final classifier is the average over all reference points and generated samples, yielding robust domain-customized predictions without any labeled target data.

## Results

W-Diff achieves the best OOD worst accuracy on 5 out of 7 datasets in the domain-incremental setting. On RMNIST, it reaches 98.70% accuracy on D^{T+1} and 87.36% OOD worst (vs. EvoS at 98.64% and 87.04%); on fMoW, 68.80% and 46.51% (vs. EvoS at 67.18% and 45.86%); on 2-Moons, 1.5% error rate (vs. EvoS at 2.5%). On Yearbook, W-Diff (94.05% OOD worst) is slightly behind EvoS (94.78%), which the authors attribute to feature-level modeling being more suitable for that dataset. Overall, W-Diff surpasses all incremental baselines on six of seven datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The closest competitor is EvoS [45], which models feature-level evolution via self-attention under a Gaussian assumption; W-Diff outperforms it on RMNIST (+0.32% OOD worst), fMoW (+0.65% OOD worst), 2-Moons (1.5% vs 2.5% error), ONP (32.9% vs 33.1% error), Huffpost (+0.21% OOD worst), and Arxiv (+0.51% OOD worst), but trails on Yearbook (94.05% vs 94.78% OOD worst). DRAIN [2], which models whole-network evolution via LSTM, is consistently outperformed by W-Diff across all datasets (e.g., 46.51% vs 46.24% OOD worst on fMoW, 87.36% vs 85.75% on RMNIST). Continual learning baselines (EWC, SI, A-GEM, SGP) focus on retaining seen-task performance rather than future generalization and lag significantly on OOD worst metrics (e.g., A-GEM drops to 41.13% on fMoW vs W-Diff's 46.51%). Offline DG methods (IRM, CORAL, Mixup, LISA, GI, LSSAE) require simultaneous access to all source domains and are not directly comparable in the incremental setting, though GI achieves strong Yearbook results (95.73% OOD worst) by exploiting the full domain set. Self-supervised incremental baselines (SimCLR, SwAV) and simple fine-tuning (IncFinetune) are substantially weaker, confirming that explicit modeling of the evolution pattern is critical for future-domain generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

W-Diff repurposes the conditional diffusion model (building on DDPM [14] and LDM's U-Net [31]) from image generation to parameter-space generation, specifically modeling the evolution of classifier weights across sequentially arriving domains. It departs from EvoS [45], which captures evolution at the feature level under a Gaussian distribution assumption, by instead modeling the residual change in classifier weights between historical reference points and the current anchor point. The approach also contrasts with DRAIN [2], which uses an LSTM to model temporal dynamics of the full network, and with p-diff [42], which generates parameters unconditionally for in-distribution data. The key conceptual move is conditioning the diffusion process on both the historical classifier weights and the current domain's prototype matrix to enable domain-customized parameter generation without labeled target data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practically important gap: most EDG methods assume all source domains are simultaneously available, which is unrealistic when data distributions evolve over time and new domains arrive sequentially. The finding that modeling only the classifier head (rather than the full network) suffices for capturing evolutionary patterns makes the approach computationally tractable and scalable to larger backbones. Limitations include restriction to classification tasks, modeling only the linear classifier rather than deeper parameters, and potential degradation under significant distribution shifts. Open questions include extending to regression tasks, encoding deeper network parameters into latent spaces for diffusion, and understanding when parameter-level vs. feature-level evolution modeling is more appropriate (as Yearbook results suggest).
