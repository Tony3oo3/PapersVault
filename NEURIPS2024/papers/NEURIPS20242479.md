## Problem

Conditional independence tests such as the Conditional Randomization Test (CRT) and its Pearson chi-squared variant (PCR) assume that source and target populations share the same covariate distribution. When covariate shift is present—differences in the joint distribution of treatment X, confounders Z, and surrogate variables V between source and target—naively applying these tests on source data yields invalid Type-I error control for the target population. This paper addresses the gap by constructing a PCR test that remains valid under such distributional shifts while retaining high statistical power.

## Input / Output

The method consumes labeled source data (Y, X, Z, V) of size n, a density ratio e(X,Z,V) estimated from unlabeled data in both populations (semi-supervised setting), a user-specified test statistic T, an optional control variate function a, and hyperparameters K, L, and significance level α. It outputs a binary reject/accept decision for H₀: X ⊥ Y | Z in the target population along with a p-value computed against a weighted sum of chi-squared distribution (not a standard χ²), where the weights are estimated from the squared importance weights of the labeled source samples. Notably, Y is never observed in the target population; all inference about the target is mediated through the density ratio reweighting of source observations.

## Method

The csPCR test modifies the standard PCR procedure in two key ways. First, it replaces the per-label count of data points with the sum of importance weights wⱼ = e(Xⱼ, Zⱼ, Vⱼ) for each label, and adjusts the rejection threshold from a standard χ² quantile to the quantile of a weighted sum of chi-squared distributions whose weights are estimated from the squared importance weights. Under the null, each weighted label sum concentrates at n/L, and the Pearson chi-squared statistic tests deviation from this uniformity. Second, a power enhancement step introduces a control variate: for each label ℓ, the indicator 1{ℓⱼ=ℓ} is augmented by subtracting γ̂ℓ·a(Xⱼ,Zⱼ,Vⱼ), where γ̂ℓ is obtained from a weighted linear regression of the label indicator on the control variate with importance weights. This reduces the variance of the weighted label sums without altering their expectation, counteracting the variance inflation caused by large density ratios. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) establishes asymptotic Type-I error control at level α under a finite fourth-moment condition on the density ratio, and Theorem 2 proves that the control variate step asymptotically reduces variance relative to the un-augmented test.

## Results

On the DUHS COVID-19 dataset (N=3,057 patients), csPCR yields a p-value of 0.025 and csPCR(pe) yields 0.032, both statistically significant at α=0.05, while the IS benchmark produces a non-significant p-value of 0.663. In simulation studies, csPCR(pe) achieves substantially higher power than the IS method: at indirect effect β=1.4, power is 0.80 for csPCR(pe) versus 0.33 for IS; at direct effect γ=1, power is 0.86 for csPCR(pe) versus 0.40 for IS. All three methods maintain Type-I error control near 0.05 when the density ratio is well estimated, though csPCR variants show more stable control at small estimation sample sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the importance-resampling (IS) method of Thams et al. (2023), which performs resampling without replacement to approximate the target distribution. csPCR and csPCR(pe) decisively outperform IS on both power and practical utility: in the real-world COVID-19 application, both proposed methods detect a significant treatment effect (p < 0.05) while IS fails to reject the null (p = 0.663). In simulations, csPCR(pe) achieves roughly 2.4× the power of IS at β=1.4 (0.80 vs. 0.33) and 2.15× at γ=1 (0.86 vs. 0.40). The IS method's limitation stems from its need to resample a small subset (theoretically o(√n)) of source data, which severely reduces effective sample size; increasing the resample size to 400 causes IS to inflate Type-I error while still retaining ~0.4 lower power than csPCR. The paper also notes that under model misspecification (full nonlinear V|X,Z with θ→1), the power-enhanced csPCR(pe) advantage over plain csPCR disappears, though both still exceed IS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Pearson Chi-Squared Conditional Randomization (PCR) test [Javanmard & Mehrabi, 2021] to the covariate shift setting by incorporating density-ratio importance weights into the label-counting step, building on the model-X/CRT framework of Candes et al. [2018]. The closest prior work is Thams et al. [2023], which proposed a general importance-sampling-based CRT under distributional shifts but did not accommodate surrogate variables V whose distributional differences between source and target can invalidate naive source-population testing. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that even when P(Y|X,Z,V) is invariant across populations, the marginal P(Y|X,Z) can differ due to shifts in P(V|X,Z), making the problem non-trivial and requiring reweighting rather than simple resampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: leveraging labeled source data to test conditional independence in a target population when surrogate variables (e.g., hospital stay length, SAT scores) shift between populations. The control-variates power enhancement is a useful practical addition, though the paper itself notes it can backfire under model misspecification of V|X,Z (the θ→1 case). Open questions include developing a double-robust version, formal power analysis, and extending beyond the model-X assumption that requires known joint distributions of (X,Z,V). The single real-world application (DUHS COVID-19 data) and reliance on the model-X framework limit immediate generalizability, but the framework is directly applicable to semi-supervised health-outcome and economics settings.
