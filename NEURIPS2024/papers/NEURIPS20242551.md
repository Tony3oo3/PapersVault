## Problem

While increasing context length in RL policies improves the ability to model non-Markovian dependencies, no prior work has established how this choice affects the time needed to reliably evaluate a policy's performance. This paper identifies a previously unexplored downside: longer context lengths impose looser upper bounds on the mixing time of the induced Markov chain, making policy evaluation and downstream learning slower. The gap is both theoretical (no formal connection between context length and mixing time existed) and practical (practitioners scaling context lengths in Transformer-based agents have no guidance on the evaluation cost this entails).

## Input / Output

Policies consume a finite interaction history window h^(k) = {o_{t-k+1}, a_{t-k+1}, r_{t-k+1}, ..., o_t} of variable context length k, where k=1 corresponds to a memoryless policy. The key theoretical output is an upper bound on the ε-return mixing time t_ret^π(ε) — the minimum number of steps needed to estimate the true reward rate within precision ε from any starting state — expressed as O(1/β_k^{g_k} · log(1/ε)), where β_k and g_k are the minimum common probability mass and maximum strongly connected component size at context length k. Empirically, mixing times are estimated by rolling out the greedy policy for 10,000 steps and recording the last step where the running reward rate deviates from the full estimate by more than ε.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-theorem theoretical framework. Theorem 1 provides a tighter mixing time bound than Kearns and Koller (1999) by a factor of 8ℓ, showing t_mix^π(ε) ∈ O(1/β^g · log(1/ε)) where g is the maximum strongly connected component size in the Dynamic Bayesian Network induced by the policy, proved via successive coupling of components. Theorem 2 then establishes that increasing context length from k to k' ≥ k can only add causal edges in the DBN (increasing g) and decrease β, so the mixing time bound can only loosen: 1/β_{k'}^{g_{k'}} ≥ 1/β_k^{g_k}. Empirically, the authors verify this with tabular Q-learning over 1 billion steps on an RGB World POMDP, showing that Transformer models exhibit higher encountered mixing times than MLP or LSTM baselines at intermediate reward rates, and that Decision Transformers require context lengths (k≥50) far exceeding those of the behavior policies (k=1) that generated their training data, with a 27× increase in average mixing time (86,834 vs. 3,271 steps) for reliable evaluation.

## Results

In the toy MDP examples, policies conditioned on irrelevant variables exhibit up to 7.6× higher mixing times (340.3 vs. 44.9) compared to policies conditioned only on relevant variables, and policies on all variables in the independent subtasks example reach mixing times up to 968.1 vs. 267.1 for contextually structured policies. In online RL on the RGB World POMDP (1B steps, 100 seeds), longer context lengths consistently produce larger average mixing times at comparable reward rates, and Transformer architectures show notably higher encountered mixing times at intermediate reward rates than MLP or LSTM models of similar size (~1M parameters). For Decision Transformers on Minigrid Crossing, training accuracy requires context length k≥50 (vs. k_D=1 for behavior policies), and the average mixing time at ε=0.01 is 298.1 episodes (86,834 steps) for the DT with k=25 versus only 11.2 episodes (3,271 steps) for the behavior policies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper with empirical verification rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so there is no single method being benchmarked against named baselines. The empirical comparisons are architectural: tabular Q-learning, MLP, LSTM, and Transformer models are all evaluated on the same RGB World POMDP at matched parameter counts (~1M). All architectures converge to the same optimal policy and thus the same mixing time at the final reward rate, but Transformers exhibit higher average mixing times at intermediate reward rates, an effect amplified with increased model capacity. For the Decision Transformer experiments, the implicit comparison is between the DT (k=25) and the original behavior policies (k=1): the DT achieves comparable downstream performance but requires ~26× more interaction for reliable evaluation (298.1 vs. 11.2 episodes at ε=0.01). The paper also contrasts its tighter mixing time bound O(1/β^g · log(1/ε)) against the prior Kearns & Koller [15] bound O(8ℓ/β^g · log(1/ε)), removing the 8ℓ factor and making the dependence on strongly connected component structure explicit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper tightens the mixing time bound from Kearns and Koller (IJCAI 1999) by removing a factor of 8ℓ, and extends it to the partially observable setting by formally connecting context length to the strongly connected component structure of the induced Markov chain. It builds on the return mixing time framework of Kearns and Singh (2002) and the high-mixing-time analysis of Riemer et al. (NeurIPS 2022), while offering a complementary perspective to Dong et al. (2022), who bound mixing times by restricting the planning horizon rather than the context length. The empirical section positions the theory against Decision Transformers (Chen et al., 2021) and standard RL architectures (MLP, LSTM, Transformer) in POMDPs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental and previously unacknowledged tradeoff: longer context lengths improve a policy's ability to model non-Markovian dependencies but simultaneously loosen mixing time bounds, making reliable policy evaluation and downstream learning slower. This is particularly relevant as the field scales toward foundation models for RL, where Decision Transformers must use context lengths far exceeding those of the behavior policies they imitate (k≥50 vs. k_D=1 in experiments), dramatically increasing the interaction needed for trustworthy evaluation. Open questions include whether architectures with sparse or hierarchical attention can scale context without inflating mixing times, and how to design evaluation protocols for foundation models that account for this context-length-dependent evaluation cost. The empirical validation, while thorough (100 seeds, 1B steps), is limited to small toy environments, leaving open whether the effects persist in genuinely large-scale POMDPs.
