## Problem

In online display advertising, a demand-side platform must decide ex-ante how many campaigns to send to a second-price auction without observing bidder values (due to browser privacy constraints), yet standard bandit algorithms ignore the inherent structure of the resulting reward function. This paper formalizes the problem as an N-armed structured bandit where the expected reward r(n) is fully characterized by the unknown value distribution F and the number of competitors p, and shows that r is unimodal under standard distributional assumptions — a structure that existing unimodal bandit algorithms cannot exploit because of the restricted feedback (only winning bid values are observed).

## Input / Output

At each auction t, the algorithm receives the number of campaigns N, the number of competitors p, and the stream of historical winning bid values collected whenever the coalition won a previous auction. It outputs n_t ∈ [N], the number of bidders to send to auction t. Unusually, feedback is only available conditionally: the decision maker observes the realized winning bid value only if one of its own bidders won, and the reward estimate for arm n must be constructed from samples collected by a potentially different arm k via a power-inversion of the empirical CDF.

## Method

The paper introduces two algorithms built on a key technical ingredient: concentration bounds (Theorem 1) showing that a 'power estimate' of r(n) computed from m_k samples of arm k ∈ V(n) (the estimation neighborhood) achieves sub-Gaussian confidence intervals with constants independent of n. Local-Greedy (LG) adapts OSUB by maintaining a leader arm and playing greedily within its estimation neighborhood using these power estimates, with a linear sampling requirement to ensure concentration. Greedy-Grid (GG) layers a successive-elimination procedure over a logarithmic-size reference grid S (with |S| = O(log N)) to identify the bin containing the optimal arm, then switches to greedy play within that bin. Both algorithms achieve constant problem-dependent regret independent of T; GG additionally provides a problem-independent bound of Õ(√((log N + |B*|)T)), while LG avoids explicit confidence interval computation and empirically outperforms GG, UCB, EXP3, and OSUB.

## Results

Both proposed algorithms achieve constant problem-dependent regret independent of the time horizon T, a key improvement over standard MAB baselines. LG attains Õ_N(Σ_{n∈[N]} Δ_n/Δ²) where Δ is the worst local gap, while GG achieves the tighter Õ_N(Σ_{n∈B*} 1/Δ_n + Σ_{n∈S} Δ_n/Δ²) ∧ Õ(√((log(N)+|B*|)T)), combining problem-dependent and problem-independent guarantees. In the experimental benchmark (Appendix D), LG achieves the lowest expected regret among all tested algorithms, outperforming GG, UCB, EXP3, and OSUB on synthetic second-price auction data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to EXP3 (O(√(NT))) and UCB1 (O(Σ log(T)/Δ_n ∧ √(NT))), both LG and GG eliminate the dependence on T entirely in their problem-dependent bounds, achieving constant regret. Against OSUB, the closest structured-bandit baseline, LG improves the dependence from log(T)/Δ_{n*±1} terms to a sum over all gaps scaled by 1/Δ², and avoids the log log(T) factor. GG further refines the bound to depend only on gaps Δ_n in the optimal bin B* and the O(log N) grid arms S, rather than all N arms, and additionally provides a problem-independent √((log N + |B*|)T) guarantee. In the empirical benchmark, LG achieves the lowest expected regret across all tested distributions, outperforming GG (which is more conservative due to its successive-elimination phase), OSUB, UCB, and EXP3. The paper attributes LG's practical edge to its avoidance of explicit confidence interval computation, which makes it less conservative in exploration while still exploiting the unimodal structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two distinct lines of bandit research: the unimodal bandit framework of OSUB (Combes & Proutière, 2014), which LG adapts by replacing UCB with greedy power-estimates computed from a single leader arm, and the successive-elimination procedure of Even-Dar et al. (2006), which GG employs over a logarithmically-sized reference grid. The key departure from prior structured-bandit work is the estimation mechanism: rather than observing the reward of the pulled arm directly, the learner only sees a sample from F^{n+p} (the order-statistic distribution), requiring novel concentration bounds (Theorem 1) that control how estimation error propagates when inverting between different powers of F. The authors explicitly position the problem as novel in the structured-bandits literature, arising from the observability restrictions imposed by browser privacy-enhancing systems that prevent ex-post value observation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The constant problem-dependent regret (independent of T) for both LG and GG, combined with GG's problem-independent guarantee, represents a meaningful improvement over standard UCB/EXP3 for this auction setting, with practical relevance to DSP operators choosing campaign participation under privacy constraints. The gap between theory and practice—GG has strictly better regret bounds yet LG outperforms it empirically because it avoids explicit confidence-interval computation—highlights an open question about whether a single algorithm can match both. The paper also identifies a natural extension to multiple simultaneous auctions (allocating N campaigns across A auctions) as a key open problem, and notes that the unimodality assumption, while holding for common distributions (Beta, truncated exponential, Complementary Beta), can fail for adversarial discrete distributions.
