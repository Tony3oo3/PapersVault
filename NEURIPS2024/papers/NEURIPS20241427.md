## Problem

Standard feature attribution methods (SHAP, IG) applied to predictive models trained to fit E[y|x] systematically misattribute the effects of unobservable confounders to correlated input features, producing attributions that are unfaithful to the data generation process. This paper identifies and formalizes this gap: when data fidelity is the goal, the confounder's direct effect on the target gets erroneously attached to observable features (e.g., ability's effect on income being attributed to education), leading to systematic overestimation of feature contributions.

## Input / Output

The method takes an explained input vector x*, a baseline input x', and a set of instrumental variables Ψ satisfying relevance, exogeneity, and exclusion restriction properties. It produces per-feature attribution scores (Shapley values or integrated gradients) that reflect each feature's true contribution to the target in the data generation process rather than the predictive model's output. The approach handles both continuous and discrete features, with the instrumental variables serving as proxies to re-estimate confounded features before attribution is computed on the resulting confounder-free model.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage training procedure that produces a confounder-free model: first, a neural network M_φ approximates the conditional distribution of confounded features Ẋ given observable features X̄ and instrumental variables Ψ; second, the target model f_θ is trained to fit E[ŷ|x̃, x̄] where ŷ = g(x̃, x̄) + E[ε], using re-estimated features sampled from M_φ in the gradient computation. The key insight is that E[ε] is a constant, so the model's gradients and Shapley utilities become identical to those of the true data generation function g, provably breaking the spurious association between confounders and features (Proposition 4). Standard SHAP or IG is then applied to this confounder-free model, yielding attributions that match the ground-truth data generation equation. The paper also provides theoretical characterizations of the exact attribution errors in standard methods (Propositions 1–3) and proposes a confidence-interval-based stratified sampling algorithm for efficient SHAP approximation.

## Results

On the Griliches76 dataset, IV-SHAP reduces the relative error of the education attribution ratio from 0.548–0.569 (SHAP) to 0.146–0.184, and IV-IG reduces it from 0.467–0.583 (IG) to 0.135–0.178, achieving up to a 67% relative improvement over the baselines. On the Angrist and Krueger dataset, IV-SHAP and IV-IG yield average attribution ratios of 0.0206 and 0.0207 respectively, compared to 0.0218 for both SHAP and IG, consistent with the known overestimation of educational returns when the ability confounder is unobserved. On synthetic datasets with known ground-truth attribution values, IV-SHAP and IV-IG produce substantially smaller absolute errors than SHAP and IG across all noise levels (ρ = 0.2–1.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares IV-SHAP against standard SHAP [27] and IV-IG against Integrated Gradients [38] on both synthetic and real-world datasets. On the Griliches76 dataset, IV-SHAP outperforms SHAP by a factor of roughly 3–4× in relative error (e.g., 0.146 vs. 0.552 in Year 5), and IV-IG outperforms IG by a similar margin (0.135 vs. 0.538 in Year 5). The improvement is consistent across all five education-reduction scenarios, with the largest gain (≈67%) observed in Year 1 for IV-SHAP over SHAP. On the Angrist and Krueger dataset, IV-SHAP and IV-IG produce slightly lower average attribution ratios (0.0206, 0.0207) than SHAP and IG (both 0.0218), aligning with the economic literature's finding that unobserved ability inflates estimated education returns. On synthetic datasets where ground-truth attribution values are known, both IV methods show substantially smaller absolute errors than their non-IV counterparts across all confounder noise levels, confirming the theoretical error bounds established in Propositions 1–3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges causal inference and feature attribution by applying the instrumental variable (IV) framework—well-established in econometrics and causal effect estimation (Angrist & Imbens, Hartford et al.)—to the problem of training confounder-free models for SHAP and Integrated Gradients. It departs from prior causal attribution methods (Causal Shapley, Asymmetric Shapley, Shapley Flow) that model causal relationships among observable input features but ignore unobservable confounders entirely. The two-stage training procedure (first estimate the conditional distribution of confounded features given the IV, then train the target model on re-estimated features) is directly inspired by Deep IV for counterfactual prediction, but here it serves the distinct purpose of decoupling confounder effects before attribution rather than estimating individual treatment effects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a systematic and previously unacknowledged source of error in widely-used attribution methods: unobservable confounders inflate the attribution of correlated features (e.g., overestimating education returns by absorbing ability's direct effect on income). The 67% relative error reduction on the Griliches76 dataset is practically significant for high-stakes domains like credit scoring and healthcare. Key limitations remain: the method requires valid instrumental variables satisfying relevance, exogeneity, and exclusion restrictions, which are often unavailable or hard to verify in practice; the theoretical analysis assumes linear confounder effects; and the real-dataset 'ground truth' is itself an approximation (using IQ/kww as proxies for ability). Open questions include developing confounder-robust attribution without IVs and extending the theory to nonlinear confounding.
