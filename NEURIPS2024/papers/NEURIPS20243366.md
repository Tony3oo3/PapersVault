## Problem

The Elo rating system, originally designed for dynamic chess players, is widely adopted to rank LLMs via pairwise human-feedback comparisons, yet its suitability for entities with static, time-agnostic capabilities has never been systematically examined. This paper investigates whether Elo satisfies two fundamental axioms—reliability (insensitivity to match ordering and hyperparameters) and transitivity (A beats B, B beats C implies A beats C)—when applied to LLM evaluation, revealing that both can fail, particularly when models have closely matched performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of binary win/loss outcomes from pairwise model comparisons (ties excluded), an initial Elo rating (1400) for each model, and a K-factor hyperparameter controlling update magnitude. The output is a per-model Elo score and an induced ranking, but critically, the final scores depend on the chronological ordering of the input sequence and the chosen K-factor, meaning the same multiset of outcomes can yield different rankings under different permutations. The paper evaluates this sensitivity across N_perms up to 10,000 reorderings and K-factors from 1 to 64, using both synthetic Bernoulli-generated data and real LMSYS Chatbot Arena annotations (≥300 non-tie comparisons per pair).

## Method

The paper constructs an axiomatic stress-testing framework rather than proposing a new rating algorithm. Synthetic human feedback is generated via Bernoulli processes with controlled win probabilities, and reliability is probed by permuting the match sequence N_perms times (1 to 10k) and sweeping the K-factor, while transitivity is tested across four scenarios with varying win-probability gaps (0.51 to 0.75) among three models. Real-world validation uses LMSYS Chatbot Arena data, sampling fixed-size subsets to match synthetic conditions. The key empirical finding is that Elo ratings are highly volatile for single permutations when win probabilities are near 0.5, stabilize with N_perms ≥ 100, and that transitivity breaks down in scenarios where intermediate models are closely matched. The paper distills these findings into concrete guidelines: use N_perms ≥ 100, select K-factor based on win-rate disparity (lower K for close matches, higher K for clear winners), and treat transitivity as an unguaranteed assumption.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that Elo ratings for LLMs are highly sensitive to match-up ordering and hyperparameters. For win probabilities P(A_win) ≥ 0.6, Elo ratings are stable even with a single sequence (N_perms=1), but for P(A_win) ≈ 0.5, ratings exhibit significant volatility with single sequences and only stabilize when N_perms ≥ 100. In the transitivity test (Table 1), violations of the expected A > B > C hierarchy occur in 3 out of 12 configurations (scenarios ② and ③ with N=1, K=1; scenario ③ with N=1, K=16), while N=100 with K=16 maintains correct ordering in all four scenarios. Real-world validation on LMSYS Chatbot Arena data confirms these findings: the closely matched GPT-4-0314 vs. GPT-4-0613 pair (win rates 0.51 vs. 0.49) shows ranking inversions at N_perms=1, whereas the more skewed Claude-1 vs. Claude-2.1 pair (0.59 vs. 0.41) remains stable across all configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it stress-tests the standard Elo rating system under varying configurations. The key comparison is between N_perms=1 (single sequence, as commonly used in practice) and N_perms=100 (averaged over 100 permutations), across K-factors of 1 and 16. With N_perms=1, K=1, transitivity is violated in scenarios ② and ③ (closely matched adjacent models), and with N_perms=1, K=16, scenario ③ still fails. Only the N=100, K=16 configuration maintains correct transitive ordering across all four scenarios. On real-world LMSYS Chatbot Arena data, the GPT-4-0314 vs. GPT-4-0613 pair (win rates 0.51/0.49) exhibits ranking inversions at N_perms=1 across multiple K-factors, while the Claude-1 vs. Claude-2.1 pair (0.59/0.41) remains stable. The paper's practical recommendation is that N_perms ≥ 100 is necessary for reliable Elo-based LLM rankings, and that K-factor should be adapted to the expected performance gap rather than using a one-size-fits-all value as in chess.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the classical Elo rating system (Elo, 1978) to the LLM evaluation setting, building on its widespread adoption in leaderboards like Chatbot Arena and MT-Bench. It departs from prior Elo limitation studies (e.g., Bertrand et al. 2023, Vadori & Savani 2023) that focused on non-transitive cyclic games like rock-paper-scissors, instead showing that transitivity can fail even in the simpler LLM context where models have static, time-agnostic capabilities. The Bernoulli-process framing for synthetic data generation connects to standard probability theory, while the reliability analysis draws on the broader ML reproducibility literature (random seeds, hyperparameter sensitivity). Unlike Glicko or TrueSkill variants that address dynamic skill estimation, this work specifically interrogates whether the base Elo system is adequate for the static-skill LLM evaluation regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Elo scores currently underpin major LLM leaderboards and influence research funding and model selection decisions, yet this paper demonstrates that a single permutation of match order can invert rankings for closely matched models (e.g., 0.51 vs. 0.49 win rates), and that transitivity is not guaranteed without exhaustive pairwise data. The actionable guidelines (N_perms ≥ 100, context-dependent K-factor) are immediately applicable to existing evaluation pipelines. Key limitations include the exclusion of tie outcomes, restriction to win/loss dynamics, and validation on a limited set of model families (GPT-4, Claude), leaving open whether the findings generalize to broader model pools or whether more sophisticated systems like Glicko-2 or TrueSkill mitigate these issues.
