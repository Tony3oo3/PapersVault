## Problem

Neuron attribution has been well-established for text-only LLMs but remains largely unexplored for Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs). Extending existing methods to MLLMs faces three compounding challenges: semantic noise from extraneous elements in generated images, computational inefficiency of gradient- or causality-based scoring when applied to the more complex MLLM architecture, and the intermingling of modality-specific neurons (T-neurons vs. I-neurons) that produce conflicting attribution results for the same concept.

## Input / Output

NAM consumes a generated image (or text output) from an MLLM along with the target semantics of interest, and internally accesses neuron activations and FFN weight matrices (W_out^l) from a single forward pass. For image attribution, it additionally requires a segmentation mask (from EVA-02) isolating the target semantic region and a diffusion-model attribution score (from DiffuserInterpreter) mapping that region to dimensions of the last-layer representation h^L. The output is a ranked set of I-neurons (for image generation) or T-neurons (for text generation) with per-neuron attribution scores, which can further drive a training-free image editing operation by perturbing the corresponding W_out columns.

## Method

NAM is a two-step, activation-based attribution pipeline that avoids gradient computation entirely. Step 1 attributes the target semantic region in a generated image to the last-layer representation h^L via EVA-02 segmentation followed by DiffuserInterpreter, yielding a relevance vector R ∈ R^H. Step 2 decomposes h^L through the residual stream (Eq. 3) so that each neuron's direct contribution is a_k^l · (W_out^l)_k weighted by R, and combines this with a softmax-normalized activation score (capturing indirect attention-mediated influence) via a max function (Eq. 4) to produce the final I-neuron score. For text outputs, the same residual-stream decomposition is projected through the unembedding matrix W_u to yield per-vocabulary contributions, with an argmax semantic-specificity filter (Eq. 7) that zeroes out all but the dominant semantic per neuron. The key design choices—segmentation-based noise removal, activation-only scoring, and modality-decoupled pipelines—address the three challenges of semantic noise, inefficiency, and intermingled neurons simultaneously.

## Results

On the COCO dataset with GILL as the target MLLM, NAM achieves the highest semantic relevance scores across all metrics for both T-neurons and I-neurons. For T-neurons, NAM scores 0.637±0.047 on output CLIPScore, 0.640±0.039 on BERTScore, and 0.148±0.013 on BLEURT, outperforming the best activation-based baseline AcT (0.594, 0.624, 0.139). For I-neurons, NAM reaches 0.613±0.052 on output CLIPScore and 0.144±0.014 on BLEURT, surpassing AcU (0.601, 0.137). In the image editing task, NAM requires 40.2% less perturbation (ℓ₂ norm) than baselines on average to replace target semantics, and outperforms all baselines by 16.83% on cross-sample invariance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NAM outperforms all six baselines (CE, GraI, GraD, GraT, AcT, AcU) on the majority of semantic relevance metrics for both T-neurons and I-neurons. The closest competitors are the activation-based methods: AcT leads on MoverScore for T-neurons (0.653 vs. NAM's 0.657, a marginal gap) and AcU is competitive on output CLIPScore for T-neurons (0.624 vs. 0.637). Gradient-based methods (GraD, GraT, GraI) and the causality-based CE method consistently underperform, with BLEURT scores below 0.072 compared to NAM's 0.148. In the image editing task (Table 3), NAM requires the smallest perturbation magnitude (e.g., 0.255 for dog→cat vs. 0.288 for AcU, 0.321 for GraT), confirming that NAM-identified I-neurons are more precisely localized. For cross-sample invariance, NAM exceeds all baselines by an average of 16.83%, indicating superior robustness to sample-specific noise. The results are consistent across both GILL and NExT-GPT, with NAM maintaining top performance on NExT-GPT as well (e.g., 0.614†CLIPScore for T-neurons vs. 0.607 for AcT).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuron attribution paradigm from text-only LLMs to Multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), building directly on the residual stream decomposition framework established by Schwettmann et al. (ICCV Workshops 2023) and the knowledge-editing-through-neuron-perturbation approach of Meng et al. (NeurIPS 2022). The key conceptual departure is addressing three challenges unique to MLLMs: semantic noise in generated images (solved via EVA-02 segmentation), computational inefficiency of gradient-based scoring (replaced with activation-based scores), and the intermingling of text- and image-specific neurons (resolved through modality-decoupled attribution). Unlike prior work that attributed entire generated images to neurons, NAM isolates target semantic regions before attribution, and explicitly separates T-neurons from I-neurons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that T-neurons and I-neurons are typically distinct for the same concept (e.g., 'dog') challenges the implicit assumption that a single set of neurons encodes a concept across all output modalities, with implications for how MLLMs are edited, pruned, or analyzed. The practical demonstration of training-free image editing via minimal neuron perturbation (40.2% less than baselines) shows immediate utility for downstream MLLM applications. However, the scope is limited to two models (GILL, NExT-GPT) and two modalities (text, image), and the method depends on external tools (EVA-02, DiffuserInterpreter) that may not generalize to all MLLM architectures. Open questions include whether modality-specific neuron separation holds in more recent MLLMs (e.g., GPT-4V, Gemini) and whether the attribution can be made self-contained without external segmentation/attribution modules.
