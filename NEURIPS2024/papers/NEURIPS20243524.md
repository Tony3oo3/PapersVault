## Problem

Existing motion representations for video prediction face a fundamental trade-off: optical flow and image differences lack the capacity to model complex motion patterns (e.g., motion blur, perspective distortion, many-to-many correspondences), while motion matrices achieve high expressiveness at O(n²) space complexity. This paper seeks a motion representation that simultaneously captures rich multi-directional motion dynamics and remains memory-efficient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243524/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes T past RGB frames (4 frames at 512×512 for UCF Sports; 2 frames at 256×832 for KITTI or 512×1024 for Cityscapes) and predicts T' future frames (typically 1). Internally, frames are encoded into multi-scale patch features at a downsampled resolution (Hs×Ws), where each patch becomes a graph node. The final output is synthesized via multi-flow forward warping, where each pixel receives k weighted dynamic vectors (direction + confidence) that collectively determine the future pixel value through normalized aggregation.

## Method

The core contribution is the motion graph, a multi-view sparse graph where nodes correspond to image patches in a downsampled space and edges encode spatial (intra-frame), backward, and forward (inter-frame) semantic relationships determined by top-k cosine similarity matching. Node features are initialized with a tendency component (derived from k dynamic vectors to the next frame via MLP + max-pooling) and a location component (MLP on normalized coordinates), then refined through T−1 rounds of alternating spatial (2D convolution) and temporal (GNN) message passing. After multi-view fusion, features are upsampled to full resolution via a ResNet-based super-resolution network and decoded into per-pixel dynamic vectors. Future frames are generated by multi-flow forward warping with weight normalization, replacing the single-vector backward warping of optical-flow-based methods. This design achieves O(n) space complexity while supporting many-to-many motion correspondences, yielding a 78% smaller model and 47% less GPU memory than the prior SOTA (MMVP) while matching or exceeding its accuracy.

## Results

On UCF Sports (MMVP split), the proposed method achieves SSIM 0.9314, PSNR 30.49, and LPIPS 0.0823 on the full set, outperforming all baselines including MMVP (SSIM 0.9300, PSNR 30.35, LPIPS 0.1062) and STIP (SSIM 0.8817, PSNR 28.17, LPIPS 0.1626). The model is only 0.60M parameters (78% smaller than MMVP's 2.79M) and uses 2.38 GB GPU memory (47% less than MMVP's 4.53 GB). On Cityscapes and KITTI, the method achieves competitive MS_SSIM (94.85 and 87.70 at t+1) while leading on LPIPS (4.13 and 9.50 at t+1) compared to DMVFN (5.58 and 10.74).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On UCF Sports (both STRPM and MMVP splits), the proposed method outperforms all 14+ baselines including MMVP [2], DMVFN [13], STIP [16], SimVP [1], STRPM [15], and earlier RNN/transformer-based methods (PredRNN, ConvLSTM, E3D-LSTM, etc.), achieving the best PSNR, LPIPS, and SSIM across all difficulty subsets. The advantage is most pronounced on the hard subset (SSIM < 0.6), where LPIPS drops from 0.3510 (MMVP) to 0.2320 (ours), indicating superior handling of complex motion. On Cityscapes and KITTI, the method slightly trails DMVFN [13] on MS_SSIM (94.85 vs 95.73 on Cityscapes; 87.70 vs 88.53 on KITTI at t+1) but clearly leads on LPIPS (4.13 vs 5.58; 9.50 vs 10.74), suggesting better perceptual quality. Compared to OPT [53], the method is competitive on MS_SSIM but substantially better on LPIPS across both driving datasets. The method also uses 78% fewer parameters and 47% less GPU memory than MMVP, and 73% less memory than DMVFN on KITTI (0.97 GB vs 3.79 GB), making it the most resource-efficient method in all comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the explicit motion modeling paradigm for video prediction (as opposed to implicit sequence prediction via RNNs/transformers) by replacing the dense motion matrix of MMVP [2] with a sparse graph structure. It draws conceptual inspiration from graph-based motion prediction in trajectory forecasting and human motion prediction, repurposing the graph representation to model spatial-temporal patch relationships in video. The multi-scale feature extraction via pixel unshuffle and the multi-flow forward warping (splatting) technique are borrowed from super-resolution and frame interpolation literature, respectively, but are integrated here into a unified motion-graph pipeline that achieves O(n) space complexity versus O(n²) for motion matrices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The motion graph offers a practical path to deploying high-quality video prediction on resource-constrained hardware, with 78% smaller model size and 47% less GPU memory while matching or exceeding SOTA across three diverse datasets. The representation is general-purpose and could serve as a motion encoding tool beyond this specific pipeline, potentially informing long-term video generation or robotics applications. Key open questions include whether the approach can handle sudden, unpredictable motions (a documented failure mode), whether inference speed can be improved to match optimized baselines like DMVFN, and whether the graph formulation scales to long-horizon prediction or higher resolutions without the current T-1 iterative message-passing cost.
