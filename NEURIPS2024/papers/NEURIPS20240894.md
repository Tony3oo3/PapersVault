## Problem

Existing blockchain graph datasets are predominantly single-chain, node- or edge-level, and lack the hierarchical graph-level structure needed for cross-chain comparative analysis. Meanwhile, Graphs of Graphs (GoG) frameworks, which model relationships between graph-structured entities, remain confined to small, static chemical and molecular datasets with no demonstrated applicability to large-scale, real-world transaction networks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240894/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a two-level graph hierarchy: local directed graphs where nodes are cryptocurrency addresses and edges are token transfers (annotated with value and timestamp), and a global graph where each local graph becomes a node and edges are weighted by the Jaccard coefficient of shared addresses between token transaction networks. Node features for local graphs are incoming, outgoing, and total degree; the global graph uses these local graphs as its node set. Outputs span three tasks: multi-class token category labels (supervised), fraud/anomaly scores (unsupervised), and token-token interaction edge predictions (supervised).

## Method

The core construction models each ERC20/BEP20 token's full transaction history as a local graph, then builds a global graph whose nodes are these local graphs and whose edges are weighted by the Jaccard overlap of their address sets, forming a hierarchical GoG structure across Ethereum, Polygon, and BSC. For downstream tasks, the paper benchmarks two model families: standard GNNs (GCN, GAT, GIN, ResidualGCN, GraphSage) applied to individual local graphs, and GoG-specific models (SEAL, GoGNN, DVGGA) that jointly encode local graph structure and global inter-token relationships. For anomaly detection, multivariate methods (COPOD, IForest, DIF, VAE) operating on graph-property vectors are compared against GNN+autoencoder methods (GAE, DONE, DOMINANT, AnomalyDAE, CoLA) on the graph structure itself. The key finding is that GoG models outperform individual GNNs in classification (up to 44% F1-macro improvement) and show chain-dependent advantages in anomaly detection, though both groups struggle with minor-class and imbalanced settings.

## Results

On the 3-class token classification task, the GoG model SEAL achieves the best F1-macro of 67.31 on Ethereum and 63.77 on BSC, outperforming the best individual GNN (GraphSAGE at 64.17 and 56.70 respectively). On the 5-class task, SEAL leads with 45.09 F1-macro on Ethereum and 31.83 on BSC, while GoGNN surprisingly performs best on Polygon (36.11 for 3-class, 19.70 for 5-class). For fraud anomaly detection, multivariate methods (DIF, IForest) dominate on Ethereum (84.56 and 84.10 AUC), while graph-based methods (DOMINANT at 78.87 AUC) lead on BSC. All models show notably degraded performance on Polygon, the smallest and most imbalanced dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEAL, the strongest GoG model, consistently outperforms all five individual GNN baselines (GCN, GAT, GIN, ResidualGCN, GraphSAGE) on Ethereum and BSC for both 3-class and 5-class classification, with the largest gap being +7.07 F1-macro over GraphSAGE on BSC 3-class (63.77 vs. 56.70) and +6.18 on Ethereum 5-class (45.09 vs. 38.91). GoGNN is the only GoG model that beats all individual GNNs on Polygon (36.11 vs. 31.71 for GraphSAGE in 3-class), but it underperforms SEAL on Ethereum and BSC, and drops sharply in 5-class settings (32.51 on Ethereum vs. 45.09 for SEAL). DVGGA is the weakest model across nearly all settings, trailing the best baseline by 10–25 F1-macro points. For anomaly detection, the advantage shifts: multivariate methods (DIF, IForest) lead on Ethereum (84.56, 84.10 AUC) while graph-based methods (DOMINANT, DONE) lead on BSC (78.87, 65.86 AUC), indicating that no single GoG or GNN approach dominates across all chains. Overall, GoG models provide a clear but moderate advantage in classification, while their benefit in anomaly detection is chain-dependent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Graphs-of-Graphs (GoG) framework—previously developed for chemical and drug interaction prediction (GoGNN, SEAL, DVGGA)—to the blockchain domain, where it had not been applied at scale. It also extends the line of blockchain graph datasets (Chartalist, LiveGraphLab, EX-Graph) by introducing the first large-scale, multi-chain, hierarchical dataset with graph-level (token-level) labels, moving beyond the node-level and edge-level tasks that dominate prior work. The global graph construction via Jaccard coefficient on shared addresses draws on social media group analysis and multi-layer blockchain anomaly detection, but the specific combination of local token transaction graphs with a dense inter-token global graph across three EVM chains is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This dataset (268M+ transactions, 24K tokens, 3 chains) fills a critical gap for blockchain ML research by providing the first cross-chain GoG benchmark with graph-level labels, enabling systematic comparison of token behavior across Ethereum, Polygon, and BSC. Key open questions remain: GoG models still underperform on minor-class classification, the Polygon dataset consistently yields poor results across all tasks, and GoG models do not consistently outperform individual GNNs in link prediction—suggesting that the hierarchical structure helps most when the task benefits from inter-graph context (classification) but less so for edge prediction. The finding that fraud tokens exhibit consistently low reciprocity and clustering across all three chains offers a practical structural signal for detection, but the paper acknowledges that multi-account control and label noise limit the reliability of these signals.
