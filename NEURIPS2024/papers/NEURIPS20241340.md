## Problem

Despite rapid growth in GraphLLM methods that integrate large language models into graph learning, the field lacks a unified benchmark with consistent experimental protocols, making it impossible to fairly compare different categories of GraphLLM approaches (enhancer, predictor, aligner) against traditional GNN and PLM baselines. Additionally, zero-shot graph transferability and computational efficiency of these methods remain largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241340/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes text-attributed graphs where each node carries a raw textual description (paper title+abstract, Wikipedia entry, user post, or profile) alongside a binary adjacency matrix, spanning 7 datasets from citation, web-link, and social-network domains with node counts from 2,708 to 169,343. For supervised evaluation, models receive a labeled subset (ranging from 0.3% to 53.7% of nodes) and must predict labels for the remainder; for zero-shot evaluation, models pre-trained on Ogbn-arxiv or Reddit must classify nodes on entirely unseen graphs with disjoint label spaces. Outputs are reported as Accuracy and Macro-F1, with the benchmark also tracking wall-clock training time and peak GPU memory per method.

## Method

GLBench unifies 7 real-world text-attributed graphs under a single data-processing and splitting protocol, then reimplements 12 GraphLLM methods across three LLM roles (enhancer, predictor, aligner) alongside GNN (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) and PLM (Sentence-BERT, BERT, RoBERTa) baselines, using matched backbones (GraphSAGE for all GNN components, LLaMA2-7B for all 7B-scale LLMs) to isolate the effect of the LLM's role. The benchmark evaluates both supervised node classification and cross-dataset zero-shot transfer, the latter being the first systematic evaluation of its kind for GraphLLM. A key empirical contribution is a training-free baseline (Emb w/ NA) that embeds node texts and class descriptions with a frozen Sentence-BERT, performs iterative neighborhood aggregation, and predicts via cosine similarity—outperforming several pre-trained zero-shot GraphLLM methods. The paper further conducts scaling-law analyses by varying GNN depth/width and LLM parameter count (4M to 13B) to test whether performance improves predictably with model size.

## Results

Under the supervised setting, GraphLLM methods achieve the highest accuracy and F1 on 6 out of 7 datasets compared to traditional GNN and PLM baselines. The LLM-as-enhancer method ENGINE achieves the most robust performance, reaching 75.01% accuracy on Ogbn-arxiv and 81.19% on WikiCS, while the LLM-as-aligner GLEM_GNN attains the best overall accuracy on Cora (82.11%), Pubmed (81.72%), and WikiCS (82.40%). In the zero-shot setting, LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o at 77.96% on Pubmed) and a simple training-free baseline (Emb w/ NA at 74.66% on Pubmed) outperform specialized GraphLLM methods like OFA (46.60%) and GraphGPT (39.85%), revealing that current zero-shot GraphLLM approaches are far from competitive.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GraphLLM methods collectively outperform the best GNN baseline (GCN) and the best PLM baseline (RoBERTa) on 6 of 7 datasets in the supervised setting. Specifically, ENGINE (LLM-as-enhancer) beats GCN by 2.77% accuracy on Citeseer (72.15% vs. 69.84%) and by 1.87% on Instagram (67.62% vs. 65.75%), while GLEM_GNN (LLM-as-aligner) surpasses GCN by 2.62% on Pubmed (81.72% vs. 79.10%) and by 2.05% on WikiCS (82.40% vs. 80.35%). In contrast, LLM-as-predictor methods (InstructGLM, GraphText, LLaGA) consistently underperform even the weakest GNN baseline on citation datasets, with InstructGLM dropping to 39.09% on Ogbn-arxiv versus GCN's 72.24%. In the zero-shot setting, the simple training-free baseline Emb w/ NA (74.66% on Pubmed, 52.30% on WikiCS) outperforms OFA (46.60%, 34.27%) and GraphGPT (39.85%, 38.02%), while LLMs like GPT-4o (77.96% on Pubmed) and DeepSeek-chat (79.23% on Pubmed) achieve the highest zero-shot accuracy, though the authors note potential data leakage. No clear scaling law is observed: ENGINE with a 7B LLM does not significantly outperform OFA with a 22M Sentence-BERT, and GLEM with LLaMA2-13B does not consistently beat GLEM with BERT-base.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLBench carves new ground by unifying the evaluation of GraphLLM methods under consistent data splits and preprocessing, addressing a fragmentation problem where prior works used incompatible protocols (e.g., 0.3% label ratio vs. 60/20/20 splits on Cora). It follows the three-role taxonomy (enhancer, predictor, aligner) from surveys [25, 21] but is the first benchmark to systematically evaluate all three categories alongside traditional GNN and PLM baselines. The zero-shot graph transfer scenario, while explored by individual methods like OFA and ZeroG, had never been benchmarked under a unified protocol, making this a genuine extension of prior evaluation practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first comprehensive GraphLLM benchmark, GLBench provides the field with a common reference point for comparing methods that were previously incomparable, and its findings (no scaling laws, LLM-as-predictor unreliability, simple baseline beating zero-shot methods) directly challenge assumptions about how to best integrate LLMs into graph learning. Key limitations include coverage of only node classification on text-attributed graphs, and the efficiency analysis reveals that current GraphLLM methods carry substantial time and memory overhead that limits deployment on large-scale graphs. Open questions include whether scaling laws emerge with better architectures, how to resolve the output controllability problem for LLM-as-predictor methods, and how to extend evaluation to edge-level and graph-level tasks as graph foundation models mature.
