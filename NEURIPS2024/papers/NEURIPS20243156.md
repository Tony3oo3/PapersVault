## Problem

Neural networks trained on biased datasets learn spurious correlations that hurt generalization, and existing debiasing methods require expensive bias annotations, predefined bias types, or data augmentation. This paper identifies an unexplored structural property: network depth acts as an implicit regularizer on the rank of the attribute subspace encoded in representations, meaning deeper networks preferentially retain lower-rank (bias) attributes while shallower networks retain higher-rank (core) attributes.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (e.g., 28×28 MNIST digits, 32×32 CIFAR-10, 256×256 face images) or text (CivilComments) and produces a single class label. During training, each input is simultaneously processed by both a deep and a shallow feature extractor whose outputs are linearly combined before a shared classifier head; at inference, only the final target branch (of any desired architecture, e.g., ResNet-18) is used. No bias labels, group annotations, or augmented samples are required at any stage.

## Method

DeNetDM is a two-stage training procedure grounded in two theorems: (1) bias-aligned samples lie on a lower-rank manifold than bias-conflicting samples, and (2) the propagation probability of an attribute through network depth decays as O(ρ(φ[d])·r_i^{-d}), so deeper layers preferentially encode lower-rank attributes. In Stage 1, a deep branch and a shallow branch are trained jointly via a Product-of-Experts objective (softmax of the sum of their features through a shared linear classifier), causing the deep branch to specialize on the bias attribute and the shallow branch on the core attribute. In Stage 2, a target branch of the desired architecture is trained with the frozen deep branch as a 'bias expert' (forcing the target to learn features orthogonal to bias) and the frozen shallow branch as a knowledge-distillation teacher (transferring core-attribute knowledge), yielding a fully debiased model without any bias annotations or data augmentation.

## Results

On Colored MNIST, DeNetDM achieves 74.72%–93.54% test accuracy across bias-conflicting ratios of 0.5%–5%, outperforming the best unsupervised baseline (LC) by 11.2–5.4 points and the supervised Group DRO by 15.1–9.1 points. On Corrupted CIFAR-10, DeNetDM reaches 38.93%–56.30%, surpassing LC by 4.4–1.8 points at most ratios (though LC edges ahead by 0.46 points at the 2% setting). On real-world datasets, DeNetDM attains 73.84% and 79.61% on BAR (1% and 5% bias), 75.7% on Biased FFHQ, and 81.04% worst-group accuracy on CelebA, consistently leading all baselines by 3–10 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeNetDM outperforms all six baselines across nearly all dataset–bias-ratio combinations. Against ERM (Vapnik 1999), the gains are the largest: +39.4 points on CMNIST at 0.5% bias and +15.9 on C-CIFAR10 at 0.5%, confirming that standard training is highly susceptible to spurious correlations. Versus the supervised Group DRO (Sagawa 2020), which requires explicit bias group labels, DeNetDM still wins by 15.1 points on CMNIST (0.5%) and 5.5 points on C-CIFAR10 (0.5%), demonstrating that depth modulation captures bias structure without any annotation. Against the unsupervised reweighting method LfF (Nam 2020), DeNetDM leads by 11.3 points on CMNIST (0.5%) and 10.4 on C-CIFAR10 (0.5%). The augmentation-based DFA (Lee 2021) is surpassed by 15.6 points on CMNIST (0.5%) and 9.0 on C-CIFAR10 (0.5%). JTT (Liu 2021) trails DeNetDM by 21.7 points on CMNIST (0.5%) and 14.2 on C-CIFAR10 (0.5%). The closest competitor is LC (Liu 2023), which uses logit correction plus MixUp augmentation; DeNetDM beats it by 11.2 points on CMNIST (0.5%) and 4.4 on C-CIFAR10 (0.5%), though LC narrowly edges DeNetDM by 0.46 points on C-CIFAR10 at the 2% bias ratio (47.81 vs. 47.35). On real-world datasets (Table 2), DeNetDM leads LC by 2.9 points on BAR (1%), 5.3 on BAR (5%), 5.7 on BFFHQ, and 4.2 on CelebA worst-group accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

DeNetDM builds on the Product of Experts framework (Hinton, 2002) by repurposing it for bias segregation: instead of combining multiple equally-capable experts, it pairs a deep and a shallow branch whose architectural asymmetry induces a natural division of labor between bias and core attributes. The theoretical core (Theorem 2, Depth-Rank Duality) extends the simplicity-bias literature (Huh et al., 2023; Wang and Jacot, 2024) by formally linking network depth to the rank of the *attribute* subspace rather than the sample subspace, a connection the authors claim is established for the first time. Empirically, it departs from prior debiasing methods (GDRO, LfF, JTT, DFA, LC) by requiring neither bias annotations nor data augmentation, instead relying on depth modulation and knowledge distillation to transfer debiased knowledge to a target architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, annotation-free debiasing method that achieves ~5% improvement over strong baselines across synthetic and real-world datasets, which is practically valuable given the cost of bias labeling. The theoretical formalization of depth as an implicit rank regularizer on attributes offers a new lens for understanding why deeper networks overfit to spurious correlations, potentially informing architecture design beyond debiasing. Key open questions include scalability to multi-bias settings (where decodability differences may become too subtle), sensitivity to hyperparameter choices, and whether the depth-rank relationship holds uniformly across all architectures and training regimes. The two-stage training pipeline also adds computational overhead compared to single-model debiasing approaches.
