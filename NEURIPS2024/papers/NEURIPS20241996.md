## Problem

Existing work on reward misspecification in AI safety operates under implicit definitions of what makes a reward function 'incorrect,' and no prior work formally defines misspecification or its resolution. The paper shows that when a human's mental model of the task differs from the robot's actual model, no single 'true' reward function may exist that satisfies the user's expectations in both domains, invalidating approaches like inverse reward design that seek to recover a ground-truth reward.

## Input / Output

The method requires the human's domain model D^H (assumed available from prior belief-learning work), the robot's true domain model D^R, and the human-specified reward function R^H. It outputs either an expectation-aligned policy (guaranteed to satisfy all inferred human expectations in the robot's domain), a sequence of targeted user queries asking whether specific states should be avoided or visited, or a no-solution indicator. The expectation set is never directly provided by the user; it is inferred from the reward function via the human model, and the algorithm iteratively refines its estimate through queries.

## Method

The Expectation Alignment (EAL) framework formalizes reward misspecification using occupancy frequencies and the user's theory of mind, proving (Theorem 1) that a jointly satisfying reward function may not exist. The algorithm first computes supersets of forbidden states Ŝ^F and goal states Ŝ^G by solving LPs on the human model that test whether any optimal policy visits or avoids each state. It then solves an LP on the robot model with soft constraints (non-negative bookkeeping variables d) that penalize visiting forbidden states or failing to reach goal states. When the LP cannot drive all d variables to zero, the positive d values identify candidate states to query the user about; confirmed states become hard constraints in subsequent LP iterations. This query-and-refine loop is guaranteed to terminate in finite steps for finite MDPs, and the final policy is guaranteed to satisfy all confirmed expectations.

## Results

The proposed Expectation Alignment method requires between 1.4 and 14.25 queries (mean ± std) and 3.1–225 seconds of computation across five grid-world domains of varying sizes, while guaranteeing zero violated user expectations by construction. The modified Inverse Reward Design (IRD) baseline, though faster (25–728 ms), violates between 1 and 6 expectations per instance in nearly all cases. The Minimax-Regret Querying (MMRQ-k) baseline fails to solve any instance within the 30-minute time limit due to the exponential cost of enumerating dominant policies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: Minimax-Regret Querying (MMRQ-k, k=2) [Zhang et al., 2018] and a modified Inverse Reward Design (IRD) [Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017]. MMRQ-k is computationally infeasible in practice, failing to solve any of the 20 test instances within the 30-minute time limit due to the exponential cost of enumerating dominant policies. The modified IRD baseline is orders of magnitude faster (25–728 ms vs. 3–225 s for the proposed method) but produces policies that violate 1–6 user expectations per instance across nearly all domains, with the worst violations (up to 6) occurring in the Obstacles domain at (11,11) grid size. The proposed method, by contrast, guarantees zero violated expectations by construction: its LP-based query loop only returns a policy when all soft-constraint bookkeeping variables are zero, and it terminates with a 'No Solution' indicator if no such policy exists. The trade-off is a modest overhead of 1.4–14.25 user queries (substantially fewer than the total state space) and seconds-to-minutes of LP solving time, which the authors argue is acceptable given the safety guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first formal definition of reward misspecification through the Expectation Alignment (EAL) framework, grounded in the user's theory of mind about the agent. It explicitly positions against two major prior paradigms: (1) side-effect avoidance methods (e.g., Minimax-Regret Querying, Klassen et al.) that treat the reward as true but add constraints, and (2) inverse reward design (Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017) that treats the specified reward as a proxy for a hidden 'true' reward. The paper's Theorem 1 demonstrates that the IRD paradigm is fundamentally invalidated in settings where human and robot domain models diverge, since no single reward function may satisfy expectations in both models. The method repurposes occupancy frequency (from MDP theory) as the formal language for expressing human behavioral expectations, connecting to prior work on the expressivity of Markov rewards (Abel et al. 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formalization provides a principled lens for diagnosing why existing reward-misspecification methods fail and what their scope limitations are, which is valuable for the AI safety community. However, the empirical evaluation is confined to small grid-world MDPs with fully known models and a restricted expectation form (forbidden/goal states), leaving open whether the LP-based approach scales to high-dimensional or partially observable settings. Key limitations include the assumption that both the human and robot domain models are known upfront, the restriction to Markovian expectations, and the exclusion of transcription/communication errors in reward specification. Future work would need to extend the framework to RL settings with unknown dynamics and richer expectation structures.
