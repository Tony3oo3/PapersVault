## Problem

Existing red-teaming benchmarks for LLM jailbreaking largely omit questions that trigger output-level moderation guardrails, which produce filtered-out errors rather than refusal responses. This gap makes it impossible to evaluate whether jailbreak methods can actually bypass the increasingly common input-output guardrail architectures in production LLMs. The paper addresses this by introducing a benchmark specifically designed to trigger moderation guardrails and a jailbreak method that targets both input and output filtering simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241895/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a malicious question, a GUARD-generated jailbreak prefix, and a 20-token optimized cipher string, composing them into a single prompt with a pre-defined output format instruction. The LLM produces an affirmative response where the cipher characters are inserted as prefixes and suffixes around every word (e.g., 'cipher_word_cipher'), and a simple string-matching decoder strips the cipher tokens to recover the underlying harmful text. The cipher characters are deliberately kept short (20 tokens) and embedded in the response format rather than the input prompt, so that high perplexity appears in the output rather than the input, avoiding detection by input-level filters.

## Method

JAM decouples the jailbreak problem into two orthogonal sub-problems: bypassing input-level guardrails (handled by a GUARD role-playing prefix) and bypassing output-level moderation guardrails (handled by optimized cipher characters). To optimize the cipher characters, the authors first fine-tune a toxic-bert shadow model on a filtered corpus of harmful texts scored by OpenAI's moderation API, making it functionally equivalent to the target guardrail. They then apply greedy coordinate descent over the cipher token sequence, using first-order gradient approximations to select top-k candidate replacements at each position and evaluating exact loss on a batch of harmful texts to pick the token that minimizes the shadow model's harmful score. The key design choice is that cipher characters serve as a response format instruction rather than an input perturbation, so the input prompt retains low perplexity while the output is rendered unrecognizable to the moderation model. A formal lemma (Lemma 3.1) justifies the decoupling by showing that when the gradients of the adversarial loss and the guardrail score are orthogonal, a single solution exists in the union of the two perturbation spaces.

## Results

On JAMBench (160 questions across 4 categories at 2 severity levels), JAM achieves an average jailbreak success rate of 75.17%, approximately 19.88× higher than the baseline average of 3.78%. Simultaneously, JAM reduces the filtered-out rate to 10.21%, roughly 1/6 of the baseline average of 54.76%. Performance is consistent across GPT-3.5 (78.8% success), GPT-4 (76.1%), Gemini (70.6%), and Llama-3 (66.8%), demonstrating transferability across different moderation guardrail implementations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JAM outperforms all five baselines on every model and category. GUARD (role-playing jailbreak prefix) is the strongest baseline, reaching 14–17% average success on GPT-3.5/GPT-4, but JAM more than quadruples this to 76–79% while also cutting the filtered-out rate from ~43% to ~9%. ICA (in-context attack) is the weakest, achieving 0% success and 100% filtered-out on both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, indicating that few-shot demonstrations are entirely blocked by moderation guardrails. GCG (gradient-based suffix) and PAIR (iterative refinement) both stay below 11% success, with GCG performing worst on Llama-3 (1.6%). CipherChat (cipher-based prompt disguise) achieves 3–6% success, showing that input-level cipher alone cannot bypass output-level moderation. The critical differentiator is the filtered-out rate: JAM keeps it under 12% across all settings, whereas baselines average 54.76%, confirming that the optimized cipher characters specifically target the output-level guardrail rather than merely disguising the input.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the jailbreaking literature by specifically targeting output-level moderation guardrails, a layer that prior attacks (GCG, PAIR, CipherChat, ICA) and benchmarks largely ignore. It builds directly on GUARD for input-level jailbreak prefix generation and introduces a shadow-model approach—fine-tuning toxic-bert to mimic OpenAI's black-box moderation API—to optimize cipher characters that reduce output harmfulness scores. The key conceptual departure from prior cipher-based attacks (e.g., CipherChat) is that JAM transforms only the output format rather than the input, decoupling input-level alignment bypass from output-level filter evasion via an orthogonality argument (Lemma 3.1).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

JAMBench fills a concrete gap: existing red-teaming benchmarks (HarmBench, JailbreakBench, In-the-Wild) trigger moderation guardrails on only a small fraction of questions, leaving output-level filters largely untested. The cipher-character attack reveals that output-level moderation is vulnerable to format-based obfuscation, and the two proposed countermeasures (complexity monitoring, LLM-based decoding audit) reduce success to 0%, underscoring that format-aware defenses are necessary. Open questions include whether the shadow-model approach generalizes to non-BERT-based moderation architectures (the TextCNN ablation fails completely), whether the attack transfers to guardrails that do not expose API scores, and whether adaptive cipher characters can evade the proposed countermeasures.
