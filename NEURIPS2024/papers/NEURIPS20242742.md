## Problem

Vision-language retrieval models trained on noisy web-scale data (e.g., CLIP, DataComp) often return semantically correct but aesthetically poor images, violating implicit user expectations about visual quality, style, and cultural context. Industrial systems mitigate this with multi-stage cascades of aesthetic re-rankers, but these add latency, compound model biases, and rely on low-level features (saturation, resolution) that fail for stylistic or knowledge-dependent aesthetics. This paper addresses the gap of aligning retrieval models with human aesthetics in an end-to-end, single-stage manner.

## Input / Output

At inference, the system takes a plain text query (optionally enriched by an LLM rephrasing step that appends stylistic and visual-detail descriptors) and retrieves ranked images from a large database (75M–15M images in experiments). At training, the input is a tuple of (original pseudo-query, LLM-rephrased query, top-400 retrieved images with re-ranking scores from aesthetic models), and the output is a set of partially ordered preference pairs constructed via a 2D grid sampling over semantic and aesthetic dimensions. The final model outputs a single-stage retrieval ranking that directly encodes aesthetic preference without a separate re-ranker.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages: (1) GPT-3.5-turbo rephrases user queries using a <k list> prompt that appends comma-separated visual details and stylistic elements ordered by significance, extending implicit aesthetic expectations; (2) a two-stage retrieval system (pretrained model + aesthetic re-rankers including CLIPIQA, IAP, and MANIQA) generates sorted image sequences that encode both semantic and aesthetic quality; (3) a 2D sampling strategy arranges images in a u×v grid (u=5, v=5, stride=10), extracting ordered sequences along both rows (aesthetic dimension) and columns (semantic dimension) to produce O(n²) preference pairs per query. The alignment fine-tuning uses a ranked DPO loss (adapted from DPO with Bradley-Terry preference modeling) combined with the original contrastive pre-training loss (w_pt=1.0) to stabilize retrieval capability. The key insight is that LLM reasoning captures high-level aesthetic understanding (cultural context, stylistic coherence) that low-level aesthetic models cannot, and this knowledge is distilled into the vision encoder via preference optimization, enabling a single-stage model to match or exceed multi-stage cascades.

## Results

On the proposed HPIR benchmark, alignment fine-tuning (RLFT) substantially boosts aesthetic alignment: Ours-RLFT achieves 67.6% HPIR-Aesthetic and 71.7% HPIR-Accuracy, compared to 59.4%/66.2% for the pretrained model. The method generalizes across backbones: CLIP+RLFT reaches 71.7% HPIR-Aesthetic (vs. 62.1% for CLIP alone) and DataComp+RLFT reaches 71.9% (vs. 62.7% for DataComp alone). On standard retrieval benchmarks, fine-tuning preserves semantic capability (e.g., Ours-RLFT: 82.2% ImageNet-1K-ZS, 40.2% MSCOCO R@1, 66.4% Flickr30K R@1), with only minor drops relative to the pretrained model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP [38], Ours-RLFT wins on HPIR-Aesthetic (67.6% vs. 62.1%) and HPIR-Accuracy (71.7% vs. 68.1%), while CLIP retains a slight edge on Flickr30K (68.7% vs. 66.4%). Against DataComp [8], the proposed method wins on HPIR-Aesthetic (67.6% vs. 62.7%) but trails on MSCOCO (40.2% vs. 45.7%) and Flickr30K (66.4% vs. 73.4%) due to a much smaller training budget. In the loss ablation (Table 8), Ranked DPO outperforms RRHF [66] on both HPIR metrics (71.7/67.6 vs. 68.4/62.2), with RRHF degrading rapidly after ~100 steps due to the absence of KL regularization. On HPIR, the pretrained VL model (Ours-PT: 66.2/59.4) surpasses dedicated aesthetic models CLIPIQA [50] (58.2/58.4) and MANIQA [62] (55.5/53.2) on accuracy, while IAP [42] (61.2/63.3) is competitive on the aesthetic axis, motivating its use as the re-ranker in the training pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the DPO preference-optimization framework from NLP (Rafailov et al., 2023) to the vision-language retrieval setting, where prior RLHF work in vision has focused on captioning or image generation rather than retrieval. It builds on CLIP-style contrastive pretraining (CLIP, DataComp) and incorporates public aesthetic/quality models (CLIPIQA, MANIQA, IAP) as re-rankers to generate training preferences, while introducing LLM-based query rephrasing as a novel mechanism to inject high-level aesthetic understanding into the retrieval query. The HPIR benchmark extends existing aesthetic assessment datasets (AVA, PN) into a retrieval-specific evaluation format, filling a gap the authors identify in standard retrieval benchmarks (MSCOCO, Flickr30K).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in showing that a single-stage aligned model can match or exceed multi-stage retrieval pipelines (semantic search + aesthetic re-rankers), reducing latency and maintenance complexity for production systems. The finding that simply extending query length (even by repetition) improves aesthetic scores reveals an implicit data bias in web-crawled training sets, suggesting that aesthetic quality is partially encoded in caption-image co-occurrence statistics. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond English queries and Western aesthetic norms, how it interacts with diversity requirements, and whether the dependence on public aesthetic models as supervision limits the ceiling of alignment. The authors note the method could extend to other RAI dimensions (race, nationality) but acknowledge cultural specificity as a challenge.
