## Problem

In safety-critical constrained RL, the cost function that encodes unsafe behavior is often prohibitively expensive to design or evaluate (e.g., in autonomous driving, it must account for all scene actors). Prior approaches to inferring costs from feedback either require constraint-abiding expert demonstrations, impose restrictive assumptions like linearity or smoothness, or are limited to state-level feedback that is costly to collect from human evaluators. This paper tackles how to learn a binary cost function from trajectory-level (or segment-level) binary feedback in complex environments while minimizing the evaluator's burden.

## Input / Output

The method consumes state-action trajectories generated by the current on-policy agent, along with binary safe/unsafe labels assigned to contiguous trajectory segments of adjustable length k (ranging from 1 for sparse-violation environments to the full episode length for simpler ones). The evaluator only sees trajectories flagged as novel via a SimHash-based density check, and the cost threshold c_max is assumed known a priori. The output is a binary cost function c(s,a) ∈ {0,1} parameterized by a neural network, which is then plugged into a PPO-Lagrangian policy update to produce a safe constrained policy. The inferred cost is guaranteed to be an upper bound on the true cost, ensuring that any policy satisfying the constraint under the inferred cost also satisfies it under the ground-truth cost.

## Method

RLSF alternates between data/feedback collection and cost inference/policy improvement. The key technical contribution is a surrogate loss that replaces the intractable MLE objective over segment-level products of state probabilities with a state-level binary cross-entropy loss, effectively converting trajectory-level cost inference into supervised classification with noisy labels (unsafe states are always correctly labeled; safe states may receive conflicting labels depending on their segment). This avoids gradient collapse from the product term over long segments while provably remaining an upper bound on the true likelihood loss. To reduce evaluator burden, a novelty-based sampling mechanism uses SimHash discretization to track state densities and only presents trajectories containing at least e novel states, implicitly establishing a decreasing query schedule without requiring ensemble uncertainty estimates. The inferred cost function is then used with PPO-Lagrangian for policy optimization, and the paper proves that any policy safe under the inferred cost is guaranteed safe under the true cost (Corollary 1), with the overestimation bias characterized in Proposition 3.

## Results

On 11 benchmark environments (Safety Gymnasium, Mujoco, and Driver simulator), RLSF achieves near-optimal safe policy performance using only trajectory-level binary feedback. In the hard-constraint setting (c_max=0), RLSF attains cost violation rates below 2% in 9/7 hard-constraint environments (e.g., 0% in Biased Pendulum, 0.01% in Blocked Swimmer, 0.06% in HalfCheetah), while reaching within ~80% of the best PPO-Lagrangian run (known costs) in 7/11 environments. In the soft-constraint Goal/Push tasks, RLSF achieves returns of 24.65 (Point Goal) and 2.68 (Point Push) with costs of 35.08 and 30.51 respectively, closely matching the known-cost upper bounds of 26.16/34.19 and 6.00/26.08. The novelty-based sampling mechanism requires only ~1,000–8,000 evaluator queries (vs. O(10⁴) for baselines) while outperforming entropy-based and random sampling under matched budgets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLSF significantly outperforms SIM (Hoang et al., AAAI 2024) in both return and safety across nearly all environments: in Point Circle, RLSF achieves 36.42 return with 1.9% CV versus SIM's 22.26 return with 35.21% CV; in HalfCheetah, RLSF reaches 2112.63 return versus SIM's 257.34. Against the new SDM baseline (which separates reward and safety signals), RLSF wins on return in most environments (e.g., Car Circle: 9.37 vs. 5.18; Point Goal: 24.65 vs. 1.61) and on safety in the hard-constraint settings (e.g., Point Circle CV: 1.9% vs. 11.43%; Biased Pendulum CV: 0% vs. 39.91%), though SDM occasionally achieves higher return at the cost of severe safety violations (Blocked Swimmer: 86.96 return but 92.8% CV). Compared to the known-cost upper bound PPO-Lagrangian (Achiam et al., 2019), RLSF comes within ~80% in 7/11 environments and actually exceeds it in Biased Pendulum (721.48 vs. 717.43) and Blocked Swimmer (16.09 vs. 22.62 on return, but 0.01% vs. 3.91% CV). In the Driver simulator, RLSF achieves safety comparable to PPO-Lag while dramatically reducing cost violations relative to vanilla PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), confirming effectiveness in realistic autonomous driving scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the constrained RL cost-inference-from-feedback line of work (Bennett et al. 2022; Hoang et al. 2024) by moving from state-level to trajectory/segment-level binary feedback, which prior methods could not handle due to credit-assignment difficulties. The surrogate loss that converts the intractable trajectory-level MLE into a state-level noisy-label classification problem is the key technical departure, while the novelty-based sampling via SimHash draws on count-based exploration literature (Tang et al. 2017; Bellemare et al. 2016) rather than the uncertainty-based active learning approaches used in prior feedback-based RL (Lee et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2022). The policy optimization stage reuses PPO-Lagrangian, positioning the contribution squarely in the cost-inference and feedback-collection pipeline rather than in constrained policy optimization itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that trajectory-level feedback is far cheaper to collect from human evaluators than state-level feedback, making safe RL more tractable in domains like autonomous driving where per-state safety judgments are expensive. The safety guarantee (Corollary 1: any policy safe w.r.t. the inferred cost is safe w.r.t. the true cost) is a useful theoretical anchor, though the overestimation bias it implies can lead to overly conservative policies. Key open questions include validating the approach with actual human evaluators (all experiments use automated scripts), handling noisy/inconsistent human feedback, and whether the overestimation bias correction (adding δ to c_max) can be made principled rather than heuristic. The cost-transfer result across morphologies (Point → Doggo) suggests the learned cost function captures task-agnostic safety structure, but this was tested on only two environments.
