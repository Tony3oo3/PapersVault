## Problem

Ranking LLMs by pairwise comparisons is commonly done using a strong LLM as a proxy for human judgment, but practitioners have no way to quantify the uncertainty introduced by the mismatch between human and model preferences. This paper develops a statistical framework that, given a small set of human pairwise comparisons and a large set of LLM-judged comparisons, constructs rank-sets with provable asymptotic coverage guarantees relative to the true human-preference ranking.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243593/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of n human pairwise comparisons (each consisting of two model outputs to a query and a human preference label) and a large set of N+n pairwise comparisons judged by a strong LLM (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, or Claude-3), with N >> n. It outputs, for each of k models, a rank-set [l(m), u(m)] — an interval of possible ranking positions — along with a confidence ellipsoid in R^k for the vector of true win-rates. The rank-sets carry an asymptotic guarantee that, with probability at least 1-α, they cover the true ranking induced by human preferences, without requiring any assumption on the degree of alignment between human and LLM preferences.

## Method

The core mechanism is prediction-powered inference (PPI): the large LLM-judged dataset provides an initial estimate of the win-rate vector, while the small human-labeled dataset supplies a correction term that estimates the bias between LLM and human preferences, yielding an unbiased PPI estimate θ̂ with an estimated covariance matrix Σ̂. A tunable parameter λ (selected to minimize tr(Σ)) controls how much weight the LLM comparisons receive relative to human comparisons. From θ̂ and Σ̂, a confidence ellipsoid C_α is constructed using a χ² quantile. Rank-sets are then derived by computing, for every pair of models, the distance between the ellipsoid and the hyperplane where their win-rates are equal; a positive distance certifies the ordering of that pair, and the rank-set bounds are assembled by counting certified orderings. Theorem 4.1 proves that the resulting rank-sets cover the true rank-sets with probability at least 1-α asymptotically, requiring no assumptions on the human preference distribution or the LLM's alignment quality.

## Results

On the LMSYS Chatbot Arena dataset (12 LLMs, 14,947 instances), rank-sets constructed using only pairwise comparisons by a strong LLM (e.g., GPT-4) achieve baseline intersection probabilities orders of magnitude lower than those constructed using prediction-powered ranking (PPR) or human-only comparisons, indicating that LLM-only rankings are often inconsistent with human preferences. Specifically, for n=990 human comparisons and α=0.05, the ranking position with highest probability mass under LLM GPT4 differs from the BASELINE in 7 out of 12 models, whereas PPR GPT4 differs in only 3 out of 12. In synthetic experiments with known ground truth, the empirical coverage probability of LLM-only rank-sets drops to 0.38, 0.13, and 0.0 for noise levels u=0.05, 0.1, and 0.3 respectively, while PPR methods maintain coverage near the 1−α=0.9 target as n increases. PPR GPT4 achieves a better trade-off between average rank-set size and baseline intersection probability than HUMAN ONLY, demonstrating that a small number of human comparisons (n≈990) combined with a large set of LLM comparisons (N+n=6,336) is more informative than using human comparisons alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four families of methods: (1) LLM-only (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-3), which construct rank-sets using only strong LLM pairwise comparisons; (2) HUMAN ONLY, using only human pairwise comparisons; (3) PPR (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-3), which combine a small set of human comparisons with a large set of LLM comparisons via prediction-powered inference; and (4) BASELINE, which uses a large set of human comparisons as a proxy for ground truth. LLM-only methods lose decisively to PPR and HUMAN ONLY on baseline intersection probability, with gaps of orders of magnitude, confirming that strong LLM preferences do not reliably match human preferences. Among PPR variants, PPR GPT-4 outperforms PPR GPT-3.5 and PPR CL3, achieving higher baseline intersection probability and smaller average rank-set sizes. PPR GPT-4 also outperforms HUMAN ONLY on the rank-set size vs. intersection probability trade-off, showing that even a modest number of human comparisons (n=990) combined with LLM comparisons yields tighter, more accurate rank-sets than using the same number of human comparisons alone. In the synthetic setting, PPR methods achieve near-nominal coverage (≈0.9 at α=0.1) while LLM-only methods fail catastrophically (coverage as low as 0.0), and PPR also achieves higher rank-biased overlap (RBO) than LLM-only rankings across all noise levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies prediction-powered inference (Angelopoulos et al., 2023; PPI++, 2023) as a subroutine to construct a joint confidence ellipsoid over win-rate vectors, then converts it into rank-sets with coverage guarantees using a geometric distance-to-hyperplane argument inspired by Neuhof and Benjamini (2023) and Al Mohamad et al. (2022). It departs from prior ranking-under-uncertainty work, which estimates per-item confidence intervals independently and requires multiple-comparison correction, by constructing a single joint confidence region over all models simultaneously. It also extends the LLM-ranking literature (Elo, Bradley-Terry, win-rate) by lifting the requirement that all pairwise comparisons come from humans, instead using a large set of strong-LLM comparisons corrected by a small set of human comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: the field widely uses strong LLMs as proxies for human preference in ranking, yet no prior method quantifies the uncertainty introduced by potential human–model mismatch. Empirically, the finding that LLM-only rankings place 7 of 12 models (including top-2 performers) in a different most-likely position than human-preference rankings is a concrete, quantified challenge to the assumption that strong LLMs faithfully proxy human judgment. Open questions include extending to finite-sample (PAC-style) guarantees, handling distribution shift and strategic behavior, and scaling win-rate beyond O(k²) human comparisons for large model sets.
