## Problem

Graph Transformers have been reported to significantly outperform classic message-passing GNNs on node classification benchmarks, but this superiority may be overstated due to suboptimal hyperparameter configurations in prior GNN evaluations. This paper asks whether properly tuned classic GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) can actually match or exceed state-of-the-art GTs across homophilous, heterophilous, and large-scale graphs.

## Input / Output

The method operates on attributed graphs (adjacency matrix + node feature matrix) spanning 18 datasets from 890 to 2.4M nodes, covering homophilous, heterophilous, and large-scale regimes. Output is node class predictions (accuracy) for multi-class tasks or probability scores (ROC-AUC) for binary tasks. The key distinction is that the 'method' is not a new architecture but a systematic tuning protocol applied to existing GNNs, with the same hyperparameter search space and training environment used for both GNNs and GT baselines to ensure fair comparison.

## Method

The paper augments three classic message-passing GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) with standard training components—layer/batch normalization, dropout, linear residual connections, and variable network depth (1–10 layers)—then conducts exhaustive hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, hidden dimension, dropout rate, number of layers, normalization type) across 18 benchmark datasets. All GT baselines (GraphGPS, SGFormer, Polynormer, GOAT, NodeFormer, NAGphormer, Exphormer) are retrained under the identical protocol for fair comparison. Ablation studies isolate each component's contribution, revealing that normalization is critical for large-scale graphs, dropout consistently improves performance, residual connections are especially important on heterophilous graphs (e.g., 16.43% accuracy drop on Roman-Empire without them), and deeper networks benefit heterophilous graphs more than homophilous ones. The central finding is that with these modest architectural additions and proper tuning, classic GNNs achieve top-1 performance on 17 of 18 datasets, matching or exceeding the best GTs.

## Results

With proper hyperparameter tuning (normalization, dropout, residual connections, and depth), classic GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE) achieve the top rank on 17 out of 18 datasets, matching or exceeding state-of-the-art Graph Transformers. The most dramatic gains appear on large-scale graphs: GAT* improves from 72.02% to 85.01% ROC-AUC on ogbn-proteins (+12.99%), and GCN* improves from 75.45% to 86.33% accuracy on pokec (+10.88%). On standard homophilous benchmarks, GCN* reaches 85.10% on Cora and GAT* reaches 81.07% on WikiCS, both surpassing the best GT baselines. On heterophilous graphs, GCN* achieves 45.01% on Squirrel and 91.27% on Roman-Empire, outperforming all specialized heterophily-aware GNNs and GTs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Tuned GCN*, GAT*, and GraphSAGE* outperform or match all 12 baselines across 17 of 18 datasets. Against the strongest GT, Polynormer, the tuned GNNs win on Cora (85.10 vs 83.43), WikiCS (81.07 vs 80.26), Squirrel (45.01 vs 41.97), Chameleon (46.29 vs 41.97), Minesweeper (97.86 vs 97.49), ogbn-arxiv (73.53 vs 73.40), ogbn-products (83.89 vs 83.82), and pokec (86.33 vs 86.06), while Polynormer retains a narrow edge on Roman-Empire (92.66 vs 91.27). Against SGFormer, the tuned GNNs dominate on large-scale graphs: GAT* beats SGFormer* by 5.09% on ogbn-proteins (85.01 vs 79.92) and GCN* by 3.89% on pokec (86.33 vs 82.44). Among heterophily-specific GNNs, GCN* surpasses the best (FSGNN) on Squirrel (45.01 vs 35.92), Chameleon (46.29 vs 40.61), Roman-Empire (91.27 vs 79.92), Minesweeper (97.86 vs 90.08), and Questions (79.02 vs 78.86). Notably, several GTs (GraphGPS, Exphormer) run out of memory on ogbn-products and pokec, while the tuned GNNs handle these graphs efficiently with mini-batch training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the three foundational message-passing GNN architectures—GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE—and the broader GNN message-passing framework, augmenting them with standard training techniques (normalization, dropout, residual connections) that were previously underexplored in GNN evaluations. It extends the line of work questioning GNN limitations on heterophilous graphs (Platonov et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021) by providing a far more comprehensive and systematic re-evaluation across 18 diverse datasets. The paper departs from the dominant narrative in recent Graph Transformer literature (GraphGPS, SGFormer, Polynormer, etc.) by demonstrating that the reported superiority of GTs over GNNs was largely an artifact of suboptimal GNN hyperparameter configurations rather than a fundamental architectural advantage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges a widely held assumption in graph machine learning—that Graph Transformers are fundamentally superior to GNNs for node classification—and provides practitioners with concrete, actionable hyperparameter guidance (normalization for large graphs, dropout universally, residual connections for heterophily, deeper layers for heterophilous graphs). The finding that a 2–10 layer GCN/GAT/GraphSAGE with proper tuning matches or exceeds SOTA GTs on 17/18 datasets has significant practical implications: it suggests that the field may have over-invested in complex GT architectures while under-tuning simpler baselines. Limitations include the focus solely on node classification (no graph-level or edge-level tasks), the restriction to three GNN architectures, and the question of whether these hyperparameter findings generalize to other graph learning settings or emerging architectures.
