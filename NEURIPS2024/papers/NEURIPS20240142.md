## Problem

OOD score propagation along graph edges is a simple post-hoc technique for graph OOD detection, but it is not always beneficial and can even be harmful. The paper identifies that propagation fails when inter-edges (ID-to-OOD connections) dominate the graph structure, as they blur the score separation between ID and OOD nodes. The key question is: under what conditions does propagation help, and how can one design a propagation strategy that reliably improves OOD detection?

## Input / Output

The method operates as a post-hoc pipeline on a pre-trained GNN (e.g., GCN, GAT) without retraining. It consumes the raw OOD score vector (e.g., MSP scores) from the pre-trained model, the graph adjacency matrix, and the labeled/unlabeled node partition. It outputs a propagated OOD score per node, from which a binary ID/OOD decision is made via a threshold set to retain 95% of ID nodes. The propagation step uses k=8 iterations of the row-stochastic adjacency matrix, and the edge augmentation adds a dense subgraph among a selected subset of training nodes.

## Method

GRASP (GRaph-Augmented Score Propagation) is a two-stage post-hoc method. First, it identifies high-confidence ID and OOD test nodes (S_id, S_ood) using the top/bottom 5% of MSP scores, then selects a subset G of training nodes whose edges predominantly connect to S_id over S_ood, scored by h(i) = |E_{i↔S_id}| / (|E_{i↔S_ood}| + 1) and taking the top 50%. Second, it augments the graph by adding a fully-connected subgraph among nodes in G (perturbation E = e_G e_G^T), then propagates OOD scores via g = (A_+)^k ĝ. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.2) proves that if G has more edges to ID than OOD test nodes, the augmentation strictly increases the ID-vs-OOD score separability after propagation, with the improvement scaling with the edge-count difference.

## Results

On 5 common small-scale benchmarks (Cora, Amazon-Photo, Coauthor-CS, Chameleon, Squirrel), GRASP achieves an average FPR95 of 40.88% and AUROC of 85.19%, reducing FPR95 by 17.87% over the strongest baseline GNNSafe (58.75%). On 5 large-scale benchmarks (Reddit2, ogbn-products, ArXiv-year, Snap-patents, Wiki), GRASP achieves an average FPR95 of 49.96% and AUROC of 84.73%, reducing FPR95 by 32.21% over GNNSafe (89.98%). Notably, GRASP achieves FPR95 as low as 2.41% on Reddit2 and 7.84% on Coauthor-CS, while training-based baselines (GKDE, GPN, OODGAT) run out of memory or time out on nearly all large-scale datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRASP outperforms all 9 baselines on both common and large-scale benchmarks. Against the strongest post-hoc graph OOD baseline GNNSafe [77], GRASP reduces average FPR95 by 17.87% (40.88% vs. 58.75%) on common benchmarks and by 32.21% (49.96% vs. 89.98%) on large-scale benchmarks; GNNSafe's naive propagation mechanism causes it to fail completely on heterophily datasets (100% FPR on Chameleon and Squirrel), while GRASP maintains 66.88% and 85.59% FPR respectively. Traditional OOD methods (MSP, Energy, ODIN, Mahalanobis, KNN) all underperform GRASP by 15–48 points in average FPR95 on common benchmarks, confirming their inadequacy for graph-structured data. Training-based graph OOD methods (GKDE, GPN, OODGAT) are impractical at scale: they run out of memory or exceed 48-hour time limits on 4 out of 5 large-scale datasets, and even where they produce results (arXiv-year), GRASP achieves substantially lower FPR (73.93% vs. 95.62% for GPN). GRASP also demonstrates compatibility with multiple scoring functions (MSP, Energy, KNN), consistently improving AUROC over both the raw score and naive propagation across all five common benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on GNNSafe [77], which first proposed propagating OOD scores along graph structure for node-level OOD detection, but departs from it by showing that this naive propagation can be actively harmful when inter-edges (ID-to-OOD) dominate the graph topology. It also positions itself against the broader OOD detection literature (MSP, Energy, ODIN, Mahalanobis, KNN) that assumes i.i.d. data points and ignores graph connectivity, and against graph augmentation methods (GAug, DropEdge) that target node classification accuracy rather than OOD separability. The key conceptual contribution is reframing the propagation problem through the lens of intra-edge vs. inter-edge ratios, providing a theoretical condition (Theorem 3.2) that explains when propagation amplifies or destroys ID/OOD separability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of when score propagation helps or hurts graph OOD detection, resolving a gap that left practitioners without guidance on whether to apply propagation. The GRASP method is practically attractive because it is post-hoc (no retraining), architecture-agnostic, and compatible with any OOD scoring function, making it a drop-in improvement for existing graph OOD pipelines. Limitations include reliance on MSP-based pseudo-labels for selecting S_id/S_ood (susceptible to confirmation bias, as acknowledged), the specific fully-connected augmentation within G, and theoretical guarantees that assume a Bernoulli edge model. Open questions include whether more structured augmentation (e.g., learned edge weights) could further improve performance, and whether the framework extends to graph-level or edge-level OOD detection.
