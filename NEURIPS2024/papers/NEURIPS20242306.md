## Problem

Functional differential equations (FDEs) are fundamental in turbulence theory, quantum field theory, and optimal control, yet their numerical analysis has been hampered by prohibitive computational costs and methods that oversimplify solutions. Existing approaches—power series expansions, Reynolds number expansions, finite difference/element methods, and tensor decomposition—either restrict the class of solvable equations or limit expressivity to low-degree polynomials (e.g., degree ≤ 6 for the state-of-the-art CP-ALS algorithm). No learning-based scheme existed for FDEs, and the cylindrical approximation, while promising, lacked rigorous convergence guarantees for its functional derivative approximation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a functional differential equation in abstract evolution form ∂F([θ],t)/∂t = L([θ])F([θ],t) with a specified initial condition F_0, and an orthonormal basis (Legendre polynomials or Fourier series) truncated at degree m−1. The PINN's inputs are the time t and the m expansion coefficients (a_0, …, a_{m−1}) of the input function θ, sampled via Latin hypercube sampling from finite intervals. The output is the scalar functional value f(a, t) ≈ F([P_m θ], t), and first-order functional derivatives δF/δθ(x) are recovered as ∂f/∂a_k · φ_k(x) via automatic differentiation, enabling evaluation at arbitrary x without additional training.

## Method

The approach is a two-stage hybrid: (1) the cylindrical approximation expands θ(x) = Σ a_k φ_k(x) in an orthonormal basis truncated at m−1, transforming the FDE into an m-dimensional PDE in the coefficients a, with functional derivatives replaced by partial derivatives ∂f/∂a_k; (2) a 4-layer PINN (sin activations + layer normalization) solves this high-dimensional PDE in a mesh-free manner, with the loss combining residual and boundary terms computed via automatic differentiation. A key theoretical contribution is a modified cylindrical approximation of functional derivatives that applies the projection P_m to δF([P_m θ])/δθ(x) (Eq. 2), unlike prior work that silently discards the tail term, for which the authors prove pointwise convergence (Thm 3.1) and solution convergence (Thm 3.2). The computational complexity scales as O(m^r) where r is the functional derivative order (typically 1 or 2), a dramatic reduction from O(m^6) for CP-ALS, enabling degrees up to 1000 versus the previous maximum of 6. Training uses AdamW with cosine-annealed learning rate, smooth L1 loss, and softmax loss-reweighting, achieving typical PINN L¹ relative errors of ~10⁻³ on both the functional transport equation and the Burgers-Hopf equation.

## Results

On the Functional Transport Equation (FTE), the model achieves L¹ relative errors of ~10⁻³ even at degree 1000 under nonlinear initial conditions (8.27×10⁻³ ± 1.19×10⁻³), demonstrating unprecedented expressivity for cylindrical approximation. On the Burgers-Hopf Equation (BHE), relative errors reach ~10⁻⁴ under delta initial conditions (2.42×10⁻⁴ at degree 100) and ~10⁻⁵ under constant initial conditions. The computational complexity scales as O(m^r) (r = 1 or 2), a dramatic reduction from the O(m⁶) of the prior state-of-the-art CP-ALS algorithm, enabling degrees up to 1000 versus the previous maximum of 6.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the CP-ALS algorithm (Venturi, 2018), the state-of-the-art cylindrical approximation method combining tensor decomposition with finite differences. The proposed method achieves a decisive advantage in scalability: it requires O(m^r) computational complexity (r = 1 or 2) versus O(m⁶) for CP-ALS, and extends the maximum representable degree from 6 to 1000. The paper reports a direct numerical comparison with CP-ALS in Appendix H, confirming the accuracy advantage at higher degrees. Additionally, the method is mesh-free (no spacetime discretization required), unlike CP-ALS which relies on finite difference grids. The convergence guarantees (Theorems 3.1 and 3.2) provide theoretical backing that the cylindrical approximation error diminishes as m → ∞, a property not established for the CP-ALS variant that ignores the tail term without justification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two established threads: the cylindrical approximation for FDEs (Venturi 2018; Venturi & Dektor 2021), which transforms FDEs into high-dimensional PDEs via orthonormal basis expansion, and physics-informed neural networks (Raissi et al. 2019), which solve PDEs in a mesh-free manner. The key departure from prior cylindrical-approximation work (notably the CP-ALS algorithm in [91]) is twofold: (i) the authors modify the functional-derivative approximation by explicitly applying the projection P_m rather than silently discarding the tail term, and (ii) they replace the finite-difference + tensor-decomposition solver with a PINN, reducing complexity from O(m^6) to O(m^r). The convergence theorems (pointwise convergence of functional derivatives, convergence of FDE solutions) are new and differ from the uniform-convergence results in [92] because the modified approximation omits the tail term.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FDEs underpin turbulence theory, quantum field theory, density functional theory, and mean-field games, yet numerical solvers have been limited to very low expressivity (m ≤ 6) due to computational cost. By proving convergence guarantees and demonstrating m = 1000 with ~10^{-3} errors, this work opens a path to previously intractable FDE applications. However, the experiments are restricted to 1+1 dimensions, first-order functional derivatives, and abstract evolution equations with known analytic solutions; generalization to higher dimensions, higher-order derivatives, and FDEs without closed-form solutions (e.g., Schwinger-Dyson, Wetterich) remains open. The practical impact will depend on whether the PINN training stability and Latin-hypercube sampling strategy scale to the more complex FDEs that motivate this line of research.
