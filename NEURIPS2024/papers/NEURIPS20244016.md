## Problem

Burns et al. (2023) empirically demonstrated that strong models (e.g., GPT-4) finetuned on labels from weak models (e.g., GPT-2) outperform the weak models on true data, but no formal theory quantified how much gain to expect or why it occurs. This paper asks whether the accuracy gain in weak-to-strong generalization can be provably bounded and precisely characterized, providing a missing theoretical foundation for the phenomenon.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244016/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained strong model representation h_s (e.g., a large transformer's forward pass) and a set of weakly-labeled data where labels are produced by a finetuned weak model f_w ∘ h_w (never true labels). The strong model outputs a finetuned function f_sw from a convex function class F_s (typically linear functions on the representation), and the key quantitative output is the misfit d_P(f_sw ∘ h_s, f_w ∘ h_w), which the theory shows equals the accuracy gain over the weak model. The setting is regression under least-squares loss, with the convexity of F_s being the critical structural assumption.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a representation-theoretic analysis showing that the strong model's finetuning step is equivalent to projecting the weak model's function f_w ∘ h_w onto the convex set V_s = {f ∘ h_s : f ∈ F_s}. By applying the Pythagorean theorem for projections onto convex sets, the paper proves (Theorem 1) that the strong model's true error is at most the weak model's true error minus the misfit, i.e., the gain equals the misfit exactly in the realizable case. Theorem 2 extends this to non-realizable settings (where the target is only ε-approximable by the strong model) and finite samples, adding O(√ε) and O((C_Fs/n)^{1/4}) error terms via uniform convergence. The proof hinges on showing the cross-term in the squared-distance expansion is non-negative when the target lies in V_s, which follows from the convexity of V_s and the projection property. Empirically, the paper validates the theory on synthetic MLPs, MolBERT molecular regression (ESOL, FreeSolv, Lipop), and NLP regression tasks (essay scoring, French reviews), confirming the gain-misfit alignment and deriving a practical heuristic for selecting among multiple weak models.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that the true error of a weakly-supervised strong model is bounded above by the weak model's true error minus the misfit (the strong model's error on weak labels), i.e., the gain in accuracy is at least the misfit. Empirically, across synthetic (realizable and non-realizable) and real-world molecular (ESOL, FreeSolv, Lipop) and NLP (essay scoring, French reviews) datasets, the observed gain in accuracy closely matches the misfit, confirming the theory. On the Lipop dataset, the heuristic of selecting the weakly-supervised strong model with the smallest (weak error − misfit) correctly identifies the model with the lowest true error (1.0713 ± 0.0489 for hidden dim 96, versus 1.2396 ± 0.0310 for hidden dim 8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so it does not benchmark against competing weak-to-strong generalization methods. Instead, the central comparison is between the weak model (e.g., a 2-layer MLP or small transformer) and the weakly-supervised strong model (e.g., an 8-layer MLP or full MolBERT) on the same finetuning task. The strong model consistently outperforms the weak model, and the magnitude of the improvement is quantified by the misfit: across all experimental settings (synthetic pretraining, synthetic perturbation, MolBERT on ESOL/FreeSolv/Lipop, essay scoring, French reviews), the gain in accuracy tracks the misfit almost exactly. The paper also compares different weak models (varying hidden dimensions from 8 to 96) to validate the model-selection heuristic derived from Theorem 2: the weak model yielding the smallest (weak error − misfit) gap reliably produces the best-performing strong model. In the low-sample regime (T=5, N_r=250), the roles of weak and strong reverse, and the less expressive model produces a higher-quality representation, confirming that 'strength' is determined by representation quality rather than raw capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first theoretical characterization of the weak-to-strong generalization phenomenon empirically demonstrated by Burns et al. (2023), who showed that GPT-4 finetuned on GPT-2 labels outperforms GPT-2 on true data. The analysis draws on the representation-theoretic framework of Tripuraneni et al. (2020) for transfer learning, modeling the key difference between weak and strong models as a disparity in representation quality. Unlike prior teacher-student and knowledge distillation theory (e.g., Mobahi et al. 2020, Wei et al. 2020), the strong model here has no access to ground-truth labels and no regularization term, and the student is strictly more powerful than the teacher. The proof technique—a Pythagorean theorem argument for projections onto a convex set—connects the result to the broader literature on disagreement-based generalization bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean characterization that the accuracy gain equals the misfit between weak and strong models provides a principled, computable quantity for predicting and optimizing weak-to-strong supervision, with immediate algorithmic implications (e.g., selecting among candidate weak models by minimizing weak error minus misfit). The finding that 'weak' and 'strong' roles can reverse in low-sample regimes—where a less expressive model produces a higher-quality representation—challenges the assumption that model size alone determines the supervision hierarchy. Key limitations include the restriction to regression with convex function classes, the absence of classification results, and the reliance on realizability or near-realizability assumptions; extending the theory to non-convex settings, classification losses, and ensembling across multiple weak models are natural open directions.
