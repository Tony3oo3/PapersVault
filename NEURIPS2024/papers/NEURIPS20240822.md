## Problem

PCA is widely used as a denoising tool for high-dimensional data, but its denoising effect has only been quantified in narrow settings (e.g., specific K-Means analyses) and lacks a general, algorithm-independent geometric characterization. In particular, how PCA differentially compresses intra-community versus inter-community distances in the presence of heavy noise remains unquantified, and no principled outlier detection method leverages this geometric structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a d×n data matrix X (e.g., single-cell RNA-seq with d up to ~49,000 genes and n from 222 to 6,498 cells), a PCA dimension k' (typically k−1 where k is the number of communities), and a removal fraction or count o. It produces per-pair compression ratio values, a ranked list of outlier indices (points with lowest variance of compression ratio), and downstream clustering accuracy (NMI and purity) after removing the flagged points. The method operates in the n = Ω(d) regime with sub-Gaussian or bounded noise, and is not overly sensitive to the choice of k' beyond k−1.

## Method

The paper introduces the compression ratio Δ(i,i') = ‖x_i − x_i'‖ / ‖Π_{k'}(x_i) − Π_{k'}(x_i')‖, the ratio of pre-PCA to post-PCA pairwise distance, and proves via a random vector mixture model with γ-spatially unique centers that intra-community compression ratios are strictly larger than inter-community ones even when noise norm dominates center distances. The proof combines a symmetrization trick (mapping the rectangular data matrix to a symmetric block matrix), Davis-Kahan spectral perturbation bounds, Vu's random projection concentration, and Hanoine's centering analysis to control the spectral gap δ_{k−1}(Y). For outlier detection, the key insight is that outlier points (modeled as random convex combinations of community centers plus noise) have uniformly low compression ratios with all other points, yielding a low variance of compression ratio, whereas clean points exhibit high variance due to the gap between their intra- and inter-community compression values. Algorithm 1 simply computes the variance of each point's compression ratios against all other points and flags the o points with the lowest variance as outliers, which is then shown to recover all planted outliers with high probability under the mixture model.

## Results

Across 9 single-cell RNA-seq datasets, the average intra-community compression ratio consistently exceeds the inter-community compression ratio (e.g., 7.47 vs 2.54 on Koh; 14.81 vs 1.97 on Kumar), confirming PCA's denoising effect in real data. For outlier detection, the variance-of-compression method achieves the best average rank (2.33) in 5 out of 6 experimental settings (NMI and purity at PCA dim k−1 with 5% and 10% removal, and at dim 2k with 10% removal), outperforming all 8 baselines. In simulations, the method achieves the highest AUROC for outlier detection, with the performance gap over the next-best method (PCA+KNN-dist) widening as noise increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The variance-of-compression method outperforms all four baseline families (LOF, KNN-dist, Isolation Forest, ECOD) and their PCA-augmented variants in 5 out of 6 evaluation settings, achieving the best average rank. Against LOF, the gap is the largest: the proposed method ranks 2.33 vs. LOF's 4.22–6.56 across settings, with the advantage growing as noise increases. Against KNN-dist, the proposed method wins in 5 of 6 settings but is slightly outperformed on Purity at dim=k−1 with 5% removal (rank 3.44 vs. 3.11). Against Isolation Forest, the proposed method dominates in all 6 settings (best rank 2.11 vs. Isolation Forest's worst 6.00). Against ECOD, the proposed method wins in 5 of 6 settings, with the closest margin on Purity at dim=2k (2.56 vs. 3.00). In simulation experiments, the method achieves the highest AUROC for outlier detection, with the performance gap over PCA+KNN-dist widening as noise level increases, highlighting the normalizing effect of the compression ratio metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the compression ratio as an algorithm-independent geometric metric to quantify PCA's denoising effect, departing from the prior literature that studied PCA's denoising primarily through its interaction with K-Means (Ding & He 2004, Kumar & Kannan 2010, Awasthi & Sheffet 2012) in the n ≫ d regime. The theoretical framework builds on the random vector mixture model from those works but shifts to the n = Ω(d) setting relevant to single-cell data, and leverages spectral perturbation tools (Davis-Kahan, Vu 2018) to bound compression ratios under spatially unique center assumptions. The outlier detection application repurposes this geometric insight into a simple variance-based scoring rule, positioning it alongside established methods (LOF, KNN-dist, Isolation Forest, ECOD) without directly extending any of them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The compression ratio provides a principled, algorithm-agnostic lens for understanding why PCA helps downstream tasks, which could inform method design beyond the specific outlier detection application shown here. The practical relevance to single-cell RNA-seq (where n < d and noise is substantial) is concrete, though the evaluated datasets are relatively small (222–6,498 cells). Key limitations include sensitivity to the removal percentage, potential failure on highly unbalanced communities, and the theoretical gap for PCA dimensions beyond k−1 (acknowledged in Appendix D as a technical challenge). Open questions include whether the compression ratio framework extends to non-convex or non-linear dimensionality reduction, and whether the spatially unique center assumption can be relaxed.
