## Problem

Existing metanetworks for processing neural networks account only for permutation symmetries (reordering hidden neurons), but ignore scaling symmetries that arise from activation functions—e.g., ReLU admits arbitrary positive rescaling of incoming weights/biases, while tanh and sine admit sign flips. These scaling symmetries are function-preserving transformations that remain unexplored in the metanetwork literature, leaving a weaker inductive bias than the full symmetry group of the input NN.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a feedforward or convolutional neural network as a computational graph where vertices carry bias values (or a constant for input neurons) and edges carry weight values, augmented with learnable positional encodings that break unwanted permutation symmetries (e.g., distinguishing input/output neurons from hidden ones). For invariant tasks (classification, generalization prediction) the output is a scalar or class label; for equivariant tasks (INR editing) the output is a full set of modified vertex and edge features that must transform covariantly under the same permutation and scaling group as the input.

## Method

ScaleGMN extends graph metanetwork message passing with three novel building blocks: (1) Scale Invariant/Equivariant layers that achieve invariance via canonicalization (x/‖x‖ for positive scaling) or symmetrization (MLP(x)+MLP(−x) for sign) and equivariance by elementwise-multiplying linear projections with the invariant output; (2) Rescale Equivariant layers using Hadamard products to handle inputs scaled by different multipliers (central vertex, neighbour, edge); and (3) Augmented Scale Equivariant layers that treat positional encodings as non-symmetric arguments. In message passing, the message function applies ReScaleEq to the neighbour-edge pair to eliminate the neighbour's scaling, then wraps the result in a ScaleEq layer; the update function simply concatenates and applies ScaleEq. A bidirectional variant inverts backward edge features at initialization so both directions share the same symmetry structure. The paper proves that with sufficient depth (L iterations for forward, 2L for backward), ScaleGMN can simulate the forward and backward pass of any input FFNN, and empirically outperforms permutation-equivariant baselines (DWS, NFN, NG-GNN) on INR classification, editing, and generalization prediction across ReLU, tanh, and sine activations.

## Results

On INR classification (Table 1), ScaleGMN-B achieves 96.59% on MNIST, 80.78% on FashionMNIST, 38.82% on CIFAR-10, and 56.95% on Augmented CIFAR-10, outperforming the previous best (NG-GNN) by 5.2%, 12.8%, 2.8%, and 11.3% respectively. On generalization prediction (Table 2), ScaleGMN achieves Kendall-τ of 0.9418 on CIFAR-10-GS Tanh and 0.8736 on SVHN-GS Tanh, setting new state-of-the-art. On INR editing (Table 3), the bidirectional variant ScaleGMN-B achieves the lowest MSE of 1.89×10⁻² for MNIST dilation, surpassing NG-GNN-64 (2.06×10⁻²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ScaleGMN consistently outperforms all five baselines across all three tasks. Against the strongest graph-based baseline NG-GNN [33], ScaleGMN-B gains +5.2% on MNIST (96.59 vs 91.40), +12.8% on F-MNIST (80.78 vs 68.00), +2.8% on CIFAR-10 (38.82 vs 36.04), and +11.3% on Aug. CIFAR-10 (56.95 vs 45.70) for INR classification. Against the non-local equivariant baselines DWS [54] and NFN [85], the margins are even larger (e.g., +10.9% over DWS on MNIST, +13.7% over NFN_HNP on F-MNIST). On generalization prediction, ScaleGMN surpasses NFN_HNP [85] by +0.015 Kendall-τ on CIFAR-10-GS Tanh (0.9418 vs 0.9339) and NG-GNN [33] by +0.012 on SVHN-GS Tanh (0.8736 vs 0.8620). On INR editing, ScaleGMN-B achieves MSE 1.89×10⁻², beating NG-GNN-64 [33] (2.06×10⁻²) and all NFN variants (~2.55–2.65×10⁻²). The ablation (Table 4) further shows that adding scaling data augmentations to permutation-equivariant baselines yields only modest gains (e.g., DWS 67.06→71.42 on F-MNIST) and cannot close the gap to ScaleGMN (80.46), confirming that architectural equivariance is more effective than augmentation-based approximations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph metanetwork (GMN) paradigm of Kofinas et al. [33] and Lim et al. [44], which treat input NNs as graphs and use message-passing GNNs to process them while respecting permutation symmetries. The key departure is the incorporation of scaling symmetries (positive scaling for ReLU, sign flips for tanh/sine) that arise from activation functions, building on the intertwiner group characterization of Godfrey et al. [25] and the sign-equivariant network design of Lim et al. [40]. Novel building blocks—Scale Invariant/Equivariant layers and Rescale Equivariant layers—generalize the sign-specific constructions to arbitrary 1-dimensional scaling groups, and are applied throughout the message-passing pipeline. The work also contrasts with the concurrent non-local approach of Tran et al. [73], which addresses scaling but suffers from limited expressivity and cannot handle diverse activation functions without redesigning weight-sharing patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since scaling symmetries are the only function-preserving transformations for many practical activations (ReLU, tanh, sine), ignoring them forces metanetworks to implicitly learn invariance to an unbounded or discrete group, wasting capacity and hurting generalization. The theoretical guarantee that ScaleGMN can simulate the forward and backward pass of any input FFNN provides a strong expressivity foundation, and the consistent SOTA improvements (up to 13% on F-MNIST INR classification) across three tasks and three activation functions validate scaling as a critical inductive bias. Open questions include extending the framework to architectures with skip connections, softmax, and batch normalization layers (which introduce additional symmetries), completing the expressivity characterization of the scale/rescale equivariant blocks, and understanding why positive-scaling data augmentations fail to match the architectural approach—a gap that suggests continuous unbounded group invariance is fundamentally harder to approximate via sampling.
