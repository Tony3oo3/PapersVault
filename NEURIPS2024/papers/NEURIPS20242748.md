## Problem

Causal discovery for a mixture of co-existing DAGs over the same variables has been studied exclusively with observational data, which is fundamentally insufficient because the skeleton cannot be recovered from conditional independence tests alone due to emergent edges (inseparable pairs with no true edge) and cyclic relationships across component DAGs. The role of interventions in this setting was entirely unexplored: it was unknown what size of intervention is necessary and sufficient to identify true edges, and no algorithm existed for learning them.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes samples from the interventional mixture distribution p_{m,I} for adaptively chosen intervention sets I ⊆ V, along with the number of nodes n and number of mixture components K. It outputs the set of true directed edges E_t (edges present in at least one component DAG) with their orientations, equivalently the mixture parents pa_m(i) for each node i. Notably, the individual component DAGs are shown to be non-identifiable even with interventions, so the output is the union of all true edges rather than a partition into K graphs. The algorithm is fully adaptive: each intervention is designed based on results of all prior interventions.

## Method

The paper first establishes matching necessary and sufficient intervention sizes: |pa_m(i)|+1 for general DAG mixtures and K+1 for directed tree mixtures, where pa_m(i) is the union of parents of i across all components. Guided by these bounds, the CADIM algorithm proceeds in four steps: (1) identify mixture ancestors via n single-node interventions; (2) break cycles among the mixture ancestors by intervening on a minimal breaking set B(i) (whose size τ_i is the cyclic complexity number); (3) decompose the cycle-free ancestors into topological layers; (4) sequentially process each layer, intervening on the already-identified parents plus the breaking set plus the candidate node, and using a marginal independence test to confirm true edges. The total number of interventions is O(n²), and the maximum intervention size is optimal (|pa_m(i)|+1) when the mixture ancestors are cycle-free; in the general case the gap over the optimal size is bounded by τ_i, the number of nodes needed to break all cycles among the ancestors of i.

## Results

The paper establishes that the necessary and sufficient intervention size for identifying whether j ∈ pa_m(i) is |pa_m(i)| + 1 (Theorem 1), and for mixtures of directed trees this tightens to K + 1 (Theorem 2). The proposed CADIM algorithm recovers all true edges using O(n²) interventions total, with maximum intervention size at most |pa_m(i)| + τ_i + 1, where τ_i is the cyclic complexity number. Empirically, on Erdős-Rényi DAGs with K=3 components and 5000 samples, the algorithm maintains high precision and recall even at n=30 nodes, and the average cyclic complexity remains small (≈1.5 for K=3, n=10), confirming near-optimal intervention sizes in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the observational-only approach using exhaustive conditional independence tests (Algorithm 2 from [11]), which recovers the set of inseparable pairs E_i. Figure 2a empirically confirms that this observational method cannot distinguish true edges from emergent edges — the inseparable pairs strictly contain the true edge skeleton, making observational data fundamentally insufficient for skeleton recovery in mixture models. In contrast, the proposed interventional CADIM algorithm successfully recovers oriented true edges with high precision and recall. The paper also quantifies the optimality gap: in the cycle-free case (τ_i = 0), the algorithm achieves the worst-case necessary intervention size of |pa_m(i)| + 1 exactly (Theorem 3), while in the general cyclic case the gap is bounded by τ_i (Theorem 4). Empirically, the estimated τ_i values used by the algorithm are nearly identical to ground truth, confirming the theoretical guarantees hold in the finite-sample regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: observational causal discovery in mixtures of DAGs (Saeed et al., ICML 2020; Varici et al., TMLR 2024) and interventional causal discovery for single DAGs (Eberhardt & Scheines; Hauser & Bühlmann; Shanmugam et al.). Prior mixture-DAG work was restricted to observational data and could not distinguish true edges from emergent pairs, while prior intervention-design work assumed a single underlying DAG. The paper introduces the I-mixture DAG as a unified graphical object that extends the observational mixture DAG to the interventional setting, and leverages the Δ-through path and emergent edge machinery from Varici et al. (2024) to derive identifiability conditions under interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal analysis of how interventions can resolve the fundamental identifiability limitations of observational mixture-DAG causal discovery, establishing matching necessary and sufficient intervention sizes and an O(n²)-intervention adaptive algorithm. The introduction of cyclic complexity as a precise measure of the optimality gap is a clean conceptual contribution that quantifies exactly how cross-component cycles degrade intervention efficiency. Key open questions remain: lower bounds on the number of interventions under size constraints, conditions under which individual component DAGs can be recovered (the paper shows they generally cannot be), and extensions to settings with partial domain knowledge or latent variables.
