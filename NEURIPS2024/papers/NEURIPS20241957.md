## Problem

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) adapts pre-trained transformers to downstream tasks but often sacrifices generalizability in pursuit of task-specific performance. Prior analyses fail to explain why this happens or provide effective remedies: naive alignment of the fine-tuned model with the pre-trained one does not guarantee gradient reduction and can even cause gradient explosion, leaving a gap between the theoretical link between small gradient norms / large data and good generalization, and a practical PEFT strategy that achieves both.

## Input / Output

The method operates on 224×224 RGB images (vision tasks) or tokenized text sequences (NLP tasks), fine-tuning a frozen pre-trained transformer backbone (ViT-B/16, Swin-B, RoBERTa-base, Phi-3-mini) with lightweight adapters (LoRA_mul + VPT_add). During training, each forward pass applies independent multiplicative Gaussian noise (z ~ N(1, σ²I)) to the adapter's feature outputs, and the consistency loss is computed between two such noised passes on the same input. At inference, noise is discarded and adapter weights are merged into the backbone, yielding zero additional latency or memory overhead.

## Method

PACE perturbs the adapter's feature outputs Δh(X) with element-wise multiplicative noise Z ~ N(1, σ²I) (shared across tokens within a sample) and enforces that two forward passes with independent noise realizations produce consistent outputs, adding λ‖f₁(x) − f₂(x)‖² to the standard classification loss. Theoretically, Theorem 2 shows this consistency loss approximates 2σ²‖Δθ⊙∇‖² + σ⁴‖(ΔθΔθᵀ)⊙H‖_F², implicitly penalizing first- and second-order weight gradients for better generalization; Theorem 3 further proves that minimizing this loss upper-bounds the FP-distance between fine-tuned and pre-trained outputs, implicitly retaining pre-training knowledge. The noise variance σ decreases linearly with transformer block depth (motivated by DropKey). Efficient variants (PACE_fast, PACE_lazy^half) reduce the double-forward cost by reusing stored outputs across epochs or applying regularization lazily at half-batch size, matching baseline compute while retaining most of the gain.

## Results

On VTAB-1K (19 datasets, ViT-B/16), PACE achieves 79.0% mean accuracy, surpassing the previous SOTA GLoRA (78.0%) by 1.0% and the strong LoRA_mul+VPT_add baseline (76.4%) by 2.6%. On FGVC (5 fine-grained datasets), PACE reaches 91.5% mean accuracy vs. 90.8% for the baseline and 91.0% for RLRR. On domain adaptation (ImageNet → 4 OOD targets), PACE achieves 46.3% mean accuracy vs. 44.1% for GLoRA. For text tasks, PACE improves LoRA by 1.0% on GLUE (87.6% vs. 86.6%) and by 3.11% on GSM-8K (78.77% vs. 75.66%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PACE consistently outperforms all 16 compared baselines across the six evaluation benchmarks. On VTAB-1K, PACE (79.0%) beats GLoRA (78.0%), the prior SOTA, by 1.0% and RLRR (76.7%) by 2.3%, while also surpassing RepAdapter (76.1%), AdaptFormer (74.7%), LoRA (74.5%), and NOAH (74.2%). On FGVC, PACE (91.5%) edges out RLRR (91.0%), SSF (90.7%), and ARC (90.7%), all of which use augmented ViT backbones. On domain adaptation, PACE (46.3% mean) improves over GLoRA (44.1%) by 2.2% and NOAH (40.5%) by 5.8%, with the largest gains on ImageNet-A (16.3% vs. 13.3%) and ImageNet-R (35.2% vs. 31.0%). For few-shot learning, PACE yields the most substantial improvements at low-shot settings (e.g., 1-shot FGVC-Aircraft: 12.3% vs. 10.5% for LoRA_mul+VPT_add). On GLUE, PACE (87.6%) outperforms LoRA (86.6%), VeRA (85.2%), and BitFit (85.4%), and on GSM-8K it improves LoRA by 3.11% (78.77% vs. 75.66%). The ablation (Table 8) further confirms that PACE surpasses individual gradient-regularization (GP, SAM) and alignment (FPA, L2SP, DELTA, FTP) strategies, as well as their naive combination (FPA+GP).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three research threads: the generalization theory of Foret et al. (SAM) linking gradient norms to population loss, the unified PEFT framework of GLoRA that parameterizes adapters as residual branches, and the consistency regularization lineage from NICE (GANs) and R-Drop (NLP). It departs from prior alignment-based PEFT methods (L2SP, DELTA, FTP, VioLET) by proving that naive output-space alignment (FP-distance) does not guarantee gradient reduction and can even cause gradient explosion. The key conceptual departure is showing that a single consistency loss on noise-perturbed adapter features simultaneously achieves both gradient regularization and implicit model alignment, unifying two previously separate objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical explanation for why PEFT methods generalize better than full fine-tuning (smaller gradient norms + retention of large-scale pre-training knowledge), filling a gap acknowledged in prior PEFT analyses. The negative result—that minimizing FP-distance can cause gradient explosion—cautions against the widely-used but theoretically unjustified practice of aligning fine-tuned outputs with pre-trained ones. Practical limitations include the need for two forward passes per step (mitigated by PACE_fast and PACE_lazy variants) and two additional hyperparameters (λ, σ) whose optimal values depend on dataset size. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantees hold for non-linear adapters, whether the approach extends to multi-task or continual learning settings, and how it interacts with other regularization strategies at scale.
