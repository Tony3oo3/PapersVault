## Problem

While fact-checking research addresses objective inaccuracies, a subtler form of misinformation—answers that are factually correct but lack clarity, relevance, or specificity—remains largely unmeasured in formal QA settings like earnings calls. No manually annotated dataset existed that captured multiple subjective dimensions of answer quality across diverse domains, leaving a gap in detecting 'soft misinformation' where executives obscure transparency through evasive or vague responses.

## Input / Output

Each input is a long-form QA pair (average ~60-word question, ~127-word answer) extracted from earnings call transcripts, with metadata including ticker, sector, year, quarter, asker, and responder. The output is a three-level ordinal rating (0 = negatively demonstrates the feature, 1 = neutral/no evident relation, 2 = positively demonstrates the feature) independently assigned for each of six features: Clear, Assertive, Cautious, Optimistic, Specific, and Relevant. The dataset comprises 2,747 QA pairs from 120 ECTs (2007–2021) yielding 49,446 total annotations, with features verified to be statistically independent (Pearson correlations between −0.08 and 0.39).

## Method

The dataset was constructed by sampling 120 ECTs balanced across 7 consolidated sectors and time periods, extracting QA pairs via Selenium/BeautifulSoup scraping, and manually cleaning verbal fillers. Feature selection was guided by PaLM-2 (text-bison-002) to identify the 10 most prevalent answer properties per QA pair, then narrowed to 6 independent features validated through correlation analysis. Nine annotators (3 per ECT, anonymous to each other) rated each QA pair on all six features using a structured Excel-based protocol, with final labels determined by majority vote (ties default to 1). For benchmarking, PLMs (BERT-base, RoBERTa-base, FinBERT-tone) were fine-tuned as sequence classifiers with cross-entropy loss using grid search over seeds, batch sizes, and learning rates, while LLMs (Llama-3-70b/8b-Chat, Mixtral-8x22B/8x7B Instruct, GPT-4o) were evaluated via structured prompts at temperature 0.0. Transfer learning was tested by fine-tuning RoBERTa-base on SubjECTive-QA and evaluating on 65 QA pairs from White House Press Briefings and Gaggles, achieving a mean weighted F1 of 65.97%.

## Results

Across the six subjective features on SubjECTive-QA, the best per-feature models achieve weighted F1 scores ranging from 49.10% (Assertive, RoBERTa-base) to 82.75% (Relevant, Llama-3-70b-Chat). RoBERTa-base attains the highest average weighted F1 of 63.95% across all features. On the transfer test (65 QA pairs from White House Press Briefings and Gaggles), the best per-feature models achieve a mean weighted F1 of 65.97%, with Clear scoring highest (84.15%) and Cautious lowest (35.93%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks three PLMs (BERT base, RoBERTa-base, FinBERT-tone) against five LLMs (Llama-3-70b-Chat, Llama-3-8b-Chat, Mixtral-8x22B Instruct 141B, Mixtral-8x7B Instruct 46.7B, GPT-4o-06-08-204). RoBERTa-base achieves the highest overall average weighted F1 (63.95%), but no single model dominates all features: BERT wins on Clear (80.93%) and Cautious (60.66%), Llama-3-70b-Chat leads on Relevant (82.75%), and domain-specific FinBERT-tone excels on Specific where general-purpose models struggle with financial jargon. The key finding is that for lower-subjectivity features (Relevant, Clear), PLMs and LLMs perform nearly identically (mean gap of 2.17%), whereas for higher-subjectivity features (Specific, Assertive), the gap widens to 10.01%, indicating that current models still struggle with nuanced tone detection. Mixtral-8x22B Instruct (141B) outperforms Llama-3-70b-Chat on average, suggesting scale helps but does not close the subjective-feature gap. On the White House transfer test, all features see score shifts (e.g., Cautious drops to 35.93%), confirming that political QA exhibits different subjective profiles than financial QA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a multi-dimensional subjective feature annotation framework for long-form QA pairs in earnings call transcripts, departing from prior FinNLP datasets that focus on unidimensional sentiment, numerical reasoning (FinQA, TAT-QA), or argumentative structure (FinArg). It repurposes the concept of misinformation detection—traditionally framed around objective factual inaccuracies—into a 'soft misinformation' lens where answers are factually correct but deficient in clarity, specificity, assertiveness, or relevance. The feature selection process is novel in using an LLM (PaLM-2) to surface prevalent answer properties, then narrowing to six features validated for independence, a pipeline not seen in prior financial QA dataset construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SubjECTive-QA provides the first manually annotated resource for multi-dimensional subjective quality assessment of corporate QA discourse, enabling researchers to study how executives' communication style (beyond sentiment) may signal firm performance or investor confidence. The cross-domain transfer to White House Press Briefings (65.97% mean weighted F1) suggests the feature framework generalizes beyond finance, opening avenues for subjectivity detection in political and journalistic QA. Key limitations include reliance on written transcripts (excluding prosodic cues), a single-exchange/timeframe scope (NYSE, 2007–2021), and all-male annotators with shared educational backgrounds, which may constrain the generalizability of the subjective ratings themselves.
