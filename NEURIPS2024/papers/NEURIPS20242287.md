## Problem

Active learning for 3D medical segmentation has been underexplored, and existing diversity-based AL methods rely on generic Euclidean metrics that ignore the inherent hierarchical groupings (patient, volume, adjacent slices) present in medical imaging. Additionally, most prior work uses costly volume-based AL rather than slice-based AL, and the combination of active learning with weak supervision (e.g., scribbles) in medical settings remains unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method operates on individual 2D slices extracted from 3D medical volumes (e.g., 212×212 cardiac MRI slices from ACDC, ~144-slice abdominal CT from CHAOS) or video frames (480p DAVIS), treating each slice/frame as an independent unit for selection. The contrastive encoder produces a fixed-dimensional embedding per slice, which the Coreset algorithm uses to select the most diverse subset for annotation. The downstream segmentation model (UNet or DMPLS) then maps annotated 2D slices to pixel-wise masks, which are stacked into 3D volume predictions scored by 3D DICE.

## Method

The core contribution is a Group-based Contrastive Learning (GCL) loss that extends NT-Xent by promoting similar embeddings for slices within the same group (patient, volume, or adjacent slices) while encouraging dissimilarity across groups, with a custom batch sampler that ensures co-grouped slices appear together in each mini-batch. This loss trains a SimCLR network with a ResNet-18 encoder to learn a task-relevant feature representation g_φ, whose L2 distance defines the metric d_φ used in place of the standard Euclidean metric in the Coreset selection problem (solved via K-Center Greedy). The overall contrastive objective is a weighted sum of NT-Xent and up to three group losses (patient, volume, slice), with ablation showing that volume + patient + NT-Xent yields the best performance. Selected slices are then annotated (as scribbles for weak supervision or full masks for full supervision) to train a segmentation model, enabling iterative active learning rounds with minimal annotation budget.

## Results

On the ACDC dataset (the primary medical benchmark), the proposed method achieves 52.3 DICE at 2% weak annotation budget, outperforming the next-best method (Coreset at 45.2) by over 7 points, and 70.9 DICE at 2% full annotation, surpassing CoreGCN (67.7) by 3.2 points. At 5% weak annotation, the method reaches 76.1 DICE versus 74.1 for CoreGCN. Across all four datasets and both annotation regimes, the method achieves the best DICE score on 21 out of 27 comparison points, with the largest margins (up to 10+ DICE points) concentrated in the low-budget (2–5%) regime most relevant to costly medical annotation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all eight baselines on the majority of evaluation points (21/27). Vanilla Coreset [64], the direct methodological predecessor, is consistently beaten by 3–10 DICE points at low budgets (e.g., 52.3 vs. 45.2 at 2% weak on ACDC) but converges to similar performance at 40% annotation. CoreGCN [7], the closest competitor among graph-based diversity methods, is outperformed on nearly every point (e.g., 70.9 vs. 67.7 at 2% full on ACDC; 44.3 vs. 27.0 at 2% weak on MS-CMR). Random sampling [48] is the strongest uncertainty-free baseline, winning 5/27 points (mostly at higher budgets on ACDC full and CHAOS), while Stochastic Batches [20] wins 4/27 points. Among uncertainty-based methods, BALD [21] and Variance Ratio [3] are clearly outperformed at low budgets but remain competitive at 40%. VAAL [66] and TypiClust [23] are outperformed across all tested budgets. On the non-medical DAVIS video dataset, the method is competitive but does not dominate: it leads at 10% and 20% budgets (42.8, 45.2) but trails BALD (43.6) at 10% and Random (48.5) at 40%, suggesting the group-structure advantage is most pronounced in medical data with natural patient/volume hierarchies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Coreset active learning framework (Sener & Savarese, 2017) by replacing its fixed Euclidean metric with a learned metric from contrastive learning, specifically a Group-based Contrastive Learning (GCL) loss built on NT-Xent that exploits inherent hierarchical groupings (patient, volume, adjacent slice) in medical imaging. It departs from prior AL-for-segmentation work (BALD, deep ensembles, VAAL, COLLOSSAL) in two key ways: operating at the slice level rather than the volume level, and integrating weak supervision (scribbles) with the AL loop—both of which had been explored in isolation but never jointly. The metric learning component draws on SimCLR-style contrastive encoders, but the group-aware loss formulation and the custom batch sampler that ensures same-group slices co-occur in a batch are novel contributions not present in prior contrastive or AL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for medical imaging where expert annotation is expensive and time-consuming: slice-based AL with weak supervision can reduce annotation cost by an order of magnitude compared to volume-based full annotation, and the method achieves up to 10% DICE improvement at 2–5% annotation budgets where clinical utility is most constrained. The finding that the method consistently outperforms across both weak and full supervision—where competing methods typically excel in only one setting—suggests the learned metric captures task-relevant structure broadly. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger multi-site datasets with more complex group hierarchies, how sensitive it is to training-set bias (acknowledged but not addressed), and whether the volume-group loss's dominance over adjacent-slice-group loss generalizes to other modalities with different intra-volume correlation structures.
