## Problem

Single-image super-resolution (SISR) methods have achieved strong results on photo-realistic images, but their application to confocal fluorescence microscopy is severely limited by the absence of publicly available datasets with real low-resolution/high-resolution image pairs. Existing microscopy SISR works rely on private or synthetic data, hindering reproducible progress. This paper introduces SR-CAC0-2, the first public confocal fluorescence microscopy SISR dataset, and provides an extensive benchmark of 16 state-of-the-art methods to establish baselines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241913/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input consists of real low-resolution confocal fluorescence microscopy patches captured at three downscale factors (X2: 256×256, X4: 128×128, X8: 64×64) from a Zeiss LSM700 microscope, and the output is the corresponding high-resolution patch at 512×512. All images are single-channel grayscale fluorescence of Caco-2 epithelial cells imaged with three distinct protein markers (Survivin, E-cadherin/GFP-tubulin, mCherry-Histone H2B). Critically, the LR images are real acquisitions at lower scan resolution (single slice, no averaging) rather than synthetic bicubic downsampled versions, making the degradation process fundamentally different from standard SISR benchmarks. Patches are pre-filtered to contain at least 20% cell content to avoid trivial background reconstruction.

## Method

The core contribution is a dataset construction and benchmarking protocol rather than a new SISR architecture. The dataset is built by capturing 22 tiles (~9,300×9,300 px) of 3D Caco-2 cysts at four resolution levels, then extracting 9,937 aligned LR/HR patch pairs per scale and marker using a sliding-window strategy on the brightest channel (CELL2) with TV-L1 optical-flow registration to correct sub-micron stage shifts. For benchmarking, 16 SISR methods spanning four architectural families (pre-upsampling, post-upsampling, iterative up-and-down sampling, progressive upsampling) are trained under a unified protocol with a composite L2 + SSIM loss, per-method/scale/cell-type learning-rate grid search, and 100 epochs on an A100 GPU. A key empirical finding is that pre-upsampling CNNs (e.g., SRCNN, VDSR) outperform the more recent post-upsampling and transformer-based methods, which the authors attribute to the noise-rich texture of HR confocal images that CNNs tend to smooth rather than reconstruct. Downstream evaluation via StarDist nucleus segmentation on 30 test images shows several methods perform comparably to HR ground truth despite poor visual fidelity.

## Results

On the SR-CAC0-2 test set (ROI only), all 16 SISR methods outperform bicubic interpolation, but performance degrades sharply with scale: mean PSNR drops from ~35 dB at X2 to ~29 dB at X8. The best per-family representatives achieve PSNR of 35.11 (Omni-SR, X2), 35.08 (SRCNN, X2), 33.79 (Omni-SR, X4), and 29.19 (SRCNN, X8). Notably, pre-upsampling methods (e.g., SRCNN) outperform the more recent post-upsampling family (e.g., Omni-SR) at X8 by ~1.4 dB PSNR, and all methods produce visually blurry outputs that fail to reconstruct fine HR textures, confirming the dataset's difficulty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 16 SISR methods beat bicubic interpolation across every scale and cell type, confirming that learned upscaling helps even on this challenging microscopy data. However, the expected hierarchy is inverted: pre-upsampling methods (SRCNN, VDSR, DRRN, MemNet) consistently outperform the state-of-the-art post-upsampling family (Omni-SR, SwinIR, ACT, GRL, etc.), particularly at X8 where SRCNN achieves 29.19 dB PSNR versus Omni-SR's 27.74 dB. The progressive-upsampling family (MS-LapSRN, ProSR) shows the weakest performance, with MS-LapSRN failing to converge on the dimmest cell type (CELL0), dropping to 33.88 dB at X2 versus 35.08 for SRCNN. The iterative up-and-down family (DBPN, SRFBN) falls in the middle. Across all methods, CELL0 (Survivin, dimmest) is the easiest to super-resolve while CELL2 (mCherry-Histone H2B, brightest) is the hardest, suggesting that the noise-like texture in bright HR images is particularly difficult for CNN-based models to reconstruct. In downstream cell segmentation (StarDist), VDSR, DRRN, NLSN, DFCAN, ENLCN, ACT, Omni-SR, DBPN, and ProSR are statistically indistinguishable from HR ground truth at X2, while bicubic and LR are significantly worse (p < 0.001).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first public dataset for single-image super-resolution in confocal fluorescence microscopy, a modality where prior SISR work (e.g., [8, 35, 51, 60, 63, 65]) has relied exclusively on private or synthetic data. Conceptually, it departs from the standard SISR paradigm where LR images are produced by deterministic bicubic downsampling; here, LR images are real captures at lower microscope settings, and the paper empirically demonstrates (Section C) that these differ substantially from interpolated versions in noise, intensity span, and structural fidelity. The benchmarking framework draws on the four established SISR families (pre-upsampling, post-upsampling, iterative, progressive) but applies them to a domain where the degradation process is unknown and non-deterministic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a critical gap that has hindered progress in microscopy SISR, enabling reproducible training and evaluation that was previously impossible with private data. The finding that all 16 state-of-the-art methods produce smooth, texture-poor outputs—attributed to the fact that HR image 'detail' is partly detector noise from multi-scan averaging that CNNs inherently filter—raises an open question about whether standard pixel-wise reconstruction losses are appropriate for this modality. Limitations include fixed (non-live) cells, a single cell line, three protein markers, and no oversaturation/undersaturation conditions, all of which constrain generalizability to live-imaging deployment. The downstream segmentation analysis showing that several methods match HR performance on cell counting despite poor visual quality suggests that perceptual quality metrics may misalign with biological utility.
