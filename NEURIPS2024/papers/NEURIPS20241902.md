## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detectors have focused on cross-generator generalization but remain unexplored for cross-scene generalization, i.e., detecting images from different semantic domains (e.g., bedrooms vs. churches vs. faces). The paper reveals that detectors overfit to 'semantic artifacts'—unique spectral patterns inherited from the training data's content and preprocessing—causing near-total accuracy collapse (to ~0%) on cross-scene generated images while Average Precision remains high, indicating a score-shift rather than a true detection failure.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image, zero-pads it so the shorter edge is 256 pixels, then randomly crops to 256×256. The image is segmented into 32×32 patches (64 patches total), which are shuffled into a random sequence before being fed individually into the patch-based feature extractor. The output is a single binary real/fake score produced by a linear classifier over the concatenated patch features, with a 50% threshold for the final decision.

## Method

The core approach combines two mechanisms to break semantic artifacts: (1) a Patch Shuffle preprocessing step that randomizes the spatial arrangement of 32×32 image patches, destroying global semantic structure; and (2) an end-to-end patch-based classifier where each patch is processed independently through an initial convolution followed by N=18 same-convolutional blocks, then downsampled via 3 basic blocks. All patch features are flattened into a single 1D vector and passed through a linear layer trained with binary cross-entropy loss. Unlike full-image CNNs (e.g., ResNet-50) whose large receptive fields encourage learning of global scene semantics, the per-patch processing with limited receptive fields forces the network to rely on local generator artifacts rather than scene-level patterns. The model is trained on 48k Stable Diffusion v1.4 images paired with 48k LAION-5B real images using Adam (lr=1e-4) with early stopping, and achieves 92.52% average accuracy across 31 open-world test sets spanning 7 GANs, 18 diffusion model variants, and 6 CNN-based generators.

## Results

On cross-scene detection (6 variants of Latent Diffusion across different real datasets), the proposed patch-based approach with zero padding achieves 92.52% average accuracy and 95.58% mAP, outperforming the best baseline NPR (90.44% Acc., 94.84% mAP) by 2.08% absolute accuracy. On the full open-world evaluation across all 31 test sets (18 Diffusion Models, 7 GANs, 6 CNN-based generators), the method reaches 85.97% average accuracy and 90.00% mAP, surpassing NPR by 10.59% absolute accuracy. The gap between accuracy and mAP is notably small for the proposed method (e.g., 92.52% vs. 95.58%), whereas several baselines show large gaps (e.g., F3Net: 56.82% Acc. vs. 85.39% mAP), confirming that existing detectors overfit to semantic artifacts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed patch-shuffle + patch-based classifier outperforms all 9 baselines on both cross-scene and open-world metrics. The closest competitor is NPR (Tan et al., CVPR 2024), which uses Neighboring Pixel Relationships to capture local up-sampling artifacts; the proposed method beats NPR by 2.08% on cross-scene accuracy (92.52% vs. 90.44%) and by 10.59% on open-world accuracy (85.97% vs. 75.38%). Ojha (CVPR 2023), which leverages a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone, is the second-strongest baseline at 66.38% cross-scene and 67.12% open-world accuracy, trailing the proposed method by 26+ points. Earlier frequency- and patch-based methods (F3Net, PatchFor, Durall, CNN) all fall below 70% cross-scene accuracy, with F3Net showing the largest accuracy–mAP gap (56.82% vs. 85.39%), confirming severe overfitting to semantic artifacts. DIRE and Dogoulis, which target diffusion-specific or cross-concept settings respectively, also underperform substantially (≤67% cross-scene, ≤57% open-world). The consistent win across all baselines, particularly the large margin over NPR on open-world, validates that breaking global semantic artifacts via patch shuffling and local feature extraction is the key factor for generalized detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AI-generated image detection literature by identifying a previously unexplored failure axis: cross-scene generalization, distinct from the well-studied cross-generator generalization. Prior work (Wang et al. 2020, Ojha et al. 2023, Tan et al. 2024) focused on detectors generalizing across different generative architectures, and Corvi et al. characterized 'generator artifacts' as the source of cross-generator difficulty. Here, the authors introduce 'semantic artifacts'—dataset-specific spectral patterns present in both real and synthetic images that detectors overfit during training—drawing on the noise-residual power spectrum analysis tradition but applying it to a new diagnostic question. The proposed patch-shuffle + local CNN classifier repurposes the local-feature-extraction idea from PatchFor (Chai et al. 2020) but combines it with explicit global-semantic destruction via shuffling and a deliberately small receptive field to also limit generator-artifact overfitting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that cross-scene accuracy collapses to near 0% while AP remains near 100% exposes a critical blind spot in how the field evaluates generalization: high AP can mask complete binary-classification failure, and the optimal decision threshold becomes scene-dependent and impractically low. The paper's qualitative (but visually compelling) identification of semantic artifacts in real images raises the open question of whether these can be formally characterized and quantified, which would enable more principled defenses. Limitations include the absence of error bars, a single training set for main results, and the fact that the 'semantic artifact' analysis remains interpretive rather than theoretically grounded. For practitioners, the simple patch-shuffle preprocessing is immediately adoptable, but the approach's reliance on a small local CNN may be outperformed by more expressive architectures as the field evolves.
