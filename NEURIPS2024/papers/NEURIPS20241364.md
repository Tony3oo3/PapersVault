## Problem

LLMs consistently overexpress confidence—both explicitly (e.g., 'I'm 100% sure') and implicitly (authoritative tone, hallucinated details)—regardless of whether their answers are correct, misleading users who interpret model language pragmatically. Prior calibration work has focused on aligning the speaker's internal output distribution with correctness, but has not addressed how a listener actually perceives and responds to the phrasing of a long-form answer, leaving a gap between model confidence and listener trust.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text question (e.g., from TriviaQA) and produces a long-form natural-language answer in which confidence is embedded both explicitly (numeric scores, epistemic markers like 'I'm very sure') and implicitly (tone, level of detail, personal backstories). For evaluation, a short-form answer (typically 1–3 words) is extracted from the response for correctness checking, while the full response is scored by a listener model for perceived confidence. The output may also be an abstention (e.g., 'I don't know'), which is treated as a valid response indicating high uncertainty.

## Method

LACIE casts confidence calibration as a pragmatic, listener-aware preference optimization problem using a two-agent speaker–listener game. A speaker model generates k diverse long-form responses per question; a separate listener LLM (with the extracted answer anonymized to prevent prior-knowledge leakage) assigns an accept/reject probability based solely on perceived confidence in the phrasing. A conservative preference function ranks response pairs so that (correct, accepted) and (incorrect, rejected) are preferred over (correct, rejected) and (incorrect, accepted), with false negatives ranked above false positives. The speaker is then finetuned via DPO (with QLoRA, rank 16, 250 steps) to maximize the margin between preferred and dispreferred utterances, effectively training it to induce a well-calibrated accept/reject distribution in the listener rather than calibrating its own output probabilities. This multi-agent framing allows the method to capture both explicit and implicit confidence cues in long-form text, and it generalizes across model sizes (Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B, Llama3-70B) and to out-of-distribution truthfulness evaluation.

## Results

On TriviaQA with a Mistral-7B listener, LACIE achieves an average 20.7-point AUROC gain over base models (e.g., Mistral-7B base: 0.54→0.74; Llama3-70B base: 0.53→0.80), a 7.8-point ECE reduction, and an 18% absolute precision increase. In a human evaluation, LACIE reduces false accepts by 47% (32→17) while maintaining recall (0.84→0.81), improving human precision from 0.49 to 0.64. On out-of-distribution TruthfulQA, LACIE improves truthfulness from 0.27 (base) to 0.55, a 28-point absolute gain, at a 9-point cost to informativeness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LACIE outperforms the Tian et al. (2023) prompt-based calibration baseline on AUROC and precision for all three models: for Mistral-7B, LACIE (base) achieves 0.74 AUROC vs. 0.70 for Tian et al. and 0.69 vs. 0.45 precision; for Llama3-70B, LACIE (base) reaches 0.80 AUROC vs. 0.69 and 0.84 vs. 0.81 precision. Against the truthful-only baseline (which optimizes correctness without modeling the listener), LACIE wins by an average of 13.3 AUROC points on base models and 11.7 on chat variants, confirming that listener-aware preference optimization captures calibration signals beyond answer accuracy alone. The Tian et al. baseline achieves high recall (0.95–0.98) because it rarely abstains, but this comes at the cost of lower precision (0.45–0.67 for Mistral-7B), reflecting overconfident outputs that the listener accepts. LACIE trades some recall for substantially higher precision, and this tradeoff is validated in the human evaluation where the recall drop seen in automated metrics does not materialize (only 1 additional false reject, p=1.0). On TruthfulQA, LACIE (0.55 truthfulness) substantially outperforms the truthful-only baseline (0.39), demonstrating that pragmatic listener-aware training generalizes beyond the training distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) as its training algorithm but departs from prior LLM calibration work in a key conceptual way: instead of calibrating the speaker's output probability distribution (as in Mielke et al. 2022, Lin et al. 2022, Tian et al. 2023), it calibrates the *listener's* induced acceptance probability via the speaker's natural-language utterance. This pragmatic, multi-agent speaker-listener framing draws on Gricean pragmatics and RSA-style communication games (Frank and Goodman 2012, Wang et al. 2021) but applies them to open-ended QA rather than fixed-interpretation games. The preference function encodes a conservative calibration objective (false rejects preferred over false accepts) and captures both explicit epistemic markers and implicit cues like tone, detail, and backstories, going beyond the explicit-marker focus of prior verbalized-confidence work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LLM overconfidence is a well-documented trust and safety risk, and this paper shows that a relatively lightweight DPO-based finetuning can substantially reduce the rate at which humans accept incorrect answers (47% reduction) without hurting acceptance of correct ones. The emergent abstention behavior—where models learn to say 'I don't know' on uncertain questions despite no abstention examples in training—is a notable side effect that trades recall for precision. Open questions include whether the conservative utility function generalizes to settings where usability matters more than safety, how to simultaneously optimize for calibration and politeness (the trained model's authoritative tone can be off-putting), and whether the approach scales to more complex multi-turn or domain-specific settings beyond trivia QA.
