## Problem

Most group fairness methods require prior demographic information (e.g., worst-off group size bounds or proxy sensitive attributes) during training, which is often unavailable due to privacy constraints. This paper asks whether Rawlsian fairness—minimizing group utility disparity—can be improved without any demographic prior while preserving overall model utility, a setting the authors term 'harmless Rawlsian fairness.'
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242570/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard supervised learning inputs (tabular features or RGB images with corresponding labels) and requires no sensitive attribute information during training. It produces either discrete class predictions or continuous regression values depending on the task. During evaluation, sensitive attributes (e.g., gender, race) become available to partition the test set into groups for fairness metrics such as maximum utility disparity (MUD), total utility disparity (TUD), and variance of prediction error (VAR).

## Method

VFair's core insight is that minimizing the variance of per-example training losses serves as a valid proxy for Rawlsian fairness, since a Dirac-delta-like loss distribution implies equal utility across any possible group partition (Theorem 1). The optimization objective minimizes loss standard deviation subject to a constraint on mean loss (utility), formulated as a constrained problem. Rather than solving the constraint directly, VFair uses a dynamic gradient update where a coefficient λ = max(λ₁, λ₂) is computed at each step: λ₁ (from the gradient view) ensures the combined gradient never opposes the primary utility descent direction, while λ₂ (from the loss view) guarantees all implicit example reweights remain non-negative. Theorem 2 shows this update is equivalent to reweighting each example by its loss Z-score plus λ, connecting VFair to worst-case fairness reweighting. In practice, an exponential moving average approximates the global mean loss for efficient mini-batch computation, and the method requires roughly 2× the training time of ERM.

## Results

On regression tasks, VFair achieves significant fairness improvements with negligible utility loss: on COMPAS, MUD drops from 2.50 (ERM) to 0.93 and VAR from 3.23 to 0.47, while MSE stays at 23.15 vs. 23.08. On Law School, MUD improves from 7.33 to 6.63 and VAR from 4.89 to 3.66 with MSE at 12.95 vs. 12.88. On AgeDB, VFair even improves utility (MSE 3.57 vs. 4.25) while reducing MUD from 0.15 to 0.12. On classification tasks, improvements are limited due to quantized accuracy metrics, though VAR drops substantially (e.g., 33.89→8.17 on UCI Adult, 15.63→1.86 on COMPAS) and CelebA shows meaningful MUD reduction (3.64→2.74).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VFair outperforms ARL (the closest prior-free baseline) on all fairness metrics across regression datasets while maintaining comparable utility: on COMPAS, VFair reduces MUD from 2.92 to 0.93 and VAR from 3.19 to 0.47, whereas ARL barely moves from ERM. DRO achieves near-zero MUD/TUD by converging to a uniform regressor (MSE ≈ 25 on Law School and COMPAS), sacrificing utility by 90–120% relative to ERM, making it non-harmless. BPF, MPFR, and FKL all require demographic priors and degrade utility substantially (e.g., BPF inflates Law School MSE from 12.88 to 18.75; MPFR from 23.08 to 36.26 on COMPAS). On classification, VFair matches or slightly exceeds ERM accuracy (84.74% vs. 84.67% on UCI Adult) while reducing VAR by 76% (33.89→8.17), outperforming FairRF (which uses proxy attributes) on CelebA (MUD 2.74 vs. 3.64). In Appendix F.6, MMPF (which has access to sensitive attributes) achieves lower MUD on COMPAS (2.15 vs. 6.25) but at a utility cost, confirming that quantized classification metrics require demographic priors for significant fairness gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem setting—harmless Rawlsian fairness with no demographic prior—by combining three threads that previously existed in isolation: worst-case fairness without demographics (DRO, ARL, BPF), utility-preserving 'harmless' fairness (Li & Liu, Coston et al.), and dynamic barrier gradient descent (Gong & Liu). The key conceptual departure is reframing Rawlsian group-utility disparity as a variance-of-losses minimization problem (Theorem 1: MUD is upper-bounded by the standard deviation of per-example losses), which then admits a simple example-reweighting interpretation (Theorem 2) connecting it to the DRO/ARL reweighting literature. The dynamic λ update mechanism is directly inspired by the bi-objective barrier descent of Gong & Liu [28], repurposed here to guarantee that the fairness gradient never opposes the utility gradient.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central practical finding is an asymmetry: regression tasks (continuous MSE utility) can achieve significant, statistically significant fairness gains with no demographic prior, while classification tasks (quantized accuracy) cannot—because the discrete decision boundary caps the achievable disparity reduction regardless of how flat the loss curve becomes. This clarifies a boundary condition for prior-free fairness that the literature had not articulated. Limitations include roughly 2× training cost over ERM, and the method's fairness gains in classification remain modest (high p-values vs. ERM on most accuracy-based metrics). Open questions include applicability to non-IID data, whether the variance proxy remains tight under distribution shift, and whether richer continuous utility metrics (e.g., F1, calibration error) in classification can unlock the same gains seen in regression.
