## Problem

Existing top-down pose estimators require capturing interacting objects (extensions like hockey sticks) within the bounding box, which expands the field-of-view and introduces confusing background features that degrade performance in cluttered real-world scenes. The paper asks how to accurately predict keypoints that lie outside the bounding box—treating them as unseen—without explicitly including the object in the crop.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243267/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a cropped RGB image (256×192 or 384×288) where the bounding box encloses only the human body (not the extension), along with keypoint-specific text prompts processed by CLIP's frozen text encoder. It outputs 2D (x, y) coordinates and a per-keypoint scale parameter σ for all K_out keypoints, including extension keypoints that are spatially outside the input crop. The text prompts are the only channel through which the model receives semantic information about the unseen extension keypoints.

## Method

TokenCLIPose is an encoder-decoder architecture that fuses three token types in a joint multimodal embedding space: image tokens (multi-scale CNN features projected via MLP), location tokens (coarse keypoint proposals from an MLP head), and text tokens (CLIP text-encoder embeddings of keypoint-specific prompts used as initialization priors for learnable keypoint tokens). A transformer decoder with standard self-attention treats all tokens as a homogeneous set—rather than using cross-attention between modalities—to capture global spatial dependencies, followed by MLPs that regress final keypoint coordinates and scales. Training uses a Residual Log-Likelihood Estimation (RLE) loss with RealNVP normalizing flows, applied jointly to both the coarse (backbone) and final (decoder) predictions. The key design choice over prior language-guided pose estimators (e.g., LAMP) is to use text embeddings as initialization priors that guide image features through the transformer, rather than contrastively aligning image features to text embeddings, which the authors show loses global upper-body/lower-body spatial structure.

## Results

On the ice hockey dataset (PCKh@0.5), TokenCLIPose with HRNet-W48 backbone achieves 76.28% mean accuracy, outperforming the best baseline ViTPose (71.92%) by 4.36%. In zero-shot transfer to the Lacrosse dataset, TokenCLIPose reaches 78.61% mean PCKh@0.5, surpassing ViTPose (76.26%) by 2.35%. On the CrowdPose benchmark, TokenCLIPose achieves 76.2 AP, exceeding the previous best HRFormer-B (72.4 AP) by 3.8%. The largest per-keypoint gains are on extension keypoints (stick toe: +7.35%, stick heel: +6.49%), confirming the method's strength in predicting out-of-bounding-box keypoints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TokenCLIPose outperforms all 12 compared baselines across all three datasets. On the ice hockey dataset, it beats the strongest baseline ViTPose (71.92% mean PCKh) by 4.36%, with the largest margins on extension keypoints (stick toe: 66.15 vs. 58.80; stick heel: 67.82 vs. 61.33). On CrowdPose, TokenCLIPose (76.2 AP) surpasses HRFormer-B (72.4 AP) by 3.8% and Transpose-H (71.8 AP) by 4.4%, while also outperforming the multimodal LAMP method (71.4 AP) by 4.8% despite using a smaller input resolution (384×288 vs. 512×512). The zero-shot Lacrosse transfer shows TokenCLIPose (78.61%) beating ViTPose (76.26%) by 2.35% without any fine-tuning, demonstrating that the language priors generalize across sports domains. Notably, the gains are consistently larger for extension/stick keypoints than for body keypoints, validating the core hypothesis that language priors are most beneficial for keypoints outside the bounding box.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the regression-based pose estimation framework of Li et al. (RLE loss with RealNVP flows) and Poseur's encoder-decoder transformer architecture, but departs from the standard top-down paradigm by reframing extension keypoint prediction (e.g., hockey sticks) as an unseen-keypoint problem rather than expanding the bounding box. It also builds on the CLIP-based language-prompt approach for pose estimation (LAMP, CLAMP), but critically departs from their contrastive-alignment strategy: instead of pulling image features toward text embeddings, TokenCLIPose uses CLIP text embeddings only to initialize learnable keypoint tokens, letting a self-attention transformer decoder capture global spatial dependencies across all modalities. The t-SNE analysis in Fig. 1(b) motivates this departure by showing that CLIP text embeddings encode local joint similarity but lose the global upper-body/lower-body spatial structure that contrastive alignment would impose.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces two first-of-their-kind sports datasets (ice hockey, lacrosse) with extension keypoint annotations, filling a notable gap in the pose estimation literature where no public benchmarks exist for human-object extension pose. The zero-shot transfer from hockey to lacrosse—achieved by merely changing text prompts and reducing stick keypoints from 3 to 2—suggests that language priors provide a form of semantic generalization that purely visual methods lack. However, the lacrosse dataset is extremely small (300 annotations), no error bars are reported, code is not released, and the approach has not been tested on more complex extensions (e.g., multi-joint tools, vehicles). Open questions include whether the language-prior approach scales to arbitrary object extensions beyond simple sticks, and whether the gains persist in multi-person scenarios where extensions overlap with other people.
