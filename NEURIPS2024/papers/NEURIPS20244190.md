## Problem

Prior work established that noise in data generation processes leads practitioners to find simpler models, but did not quantify how much simplification to expect for a given noise level. This paper addresses the open question: given a noise level ρ, how much simpler can ML models become while maintaining comparable generalization performance, and what are the precise quantitative relationships between noise and model complexity across different hypothesis spaces and loss functions?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244190/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a dataset with injected noise (uniform random label noise with flip probability ρ, or additive Gaussian attribute noise with standard deviation σ), a hypothesis space (sparse decision trees, rule lists, scoring systems, or linear models), a loss function (0-1 misclassification or exponential), and a regularization parameter λ. The key output is not a single model but an equivalence: the optimal model under noise with regularization λ is provably identical to the optimal model under cleaner data with a stronger, explicitly computed regularization (λ/(1−2ρ) for label noise, or σ²/2 ℓ₂ penalty on log-loss for attribute noise). Additionally, the paper produces complexity bounds for Rashomon set members and the size of the set of 'good features.'

## Method

The core insight is that noise acts as an implicit regularizer, and the paper provides exact closed-form equivalences rather than asymptotic bounds. For 0-1 loss with uniform label noise, Theorem 1 proves that argmin over the noisy distribution with penalty λ equals argmin over the clean distribution with penalty λ/(1−2ρ), because the noisy risk decomposes as L_{D_ρ}(f) = (1−2ρ)L_D(f) + ρ, making the optimization invariant to the constant shift and rescaling. For exponential loss with additive Gaussian attribute noise, Theorem 9 shows the expected loss multiplies by exp(σ²‖ω‖²/2), so minimizing noisy expected loss is equivalent to minimizing log-loss plus an explicit ℓ₂ penalty with parameter σ²/2. The paper further proves that models entering the noisier Rashomon set are strictly simpler than the clean-data optimum (Theorem 3), that the set of good features (by AUC) grows with noise because high-AUC features lose signal faster (Corollary 6), and that the fraction of grounded decision trees increases accordingly (Theorem 8). Empirical validation uses the GOSDT-guesses algorithm on 24 real-world and synthetic datasets from criminal justice, lending, and other domains.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that for any model optimized with 0-1 loss and regularization λ under uniform random label noise ρ, the problem is exactly equivalent to optimizing on clean data with regularization λ/(1−2ρ), meaning noise implicitly strengthens regularization. For linear models under exponential loss with additive Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ (Theorem 9), the effective ℓ₂ regularization parameter is σ²/2. Empirically, across 24 datasets (recidivism, credit, churn, etc.), the number of leaves in optimal sparse decision trees decreases monotonically with increasing label noise (0.0 to 0.3), and the ℓ₂ norm of linear model weights decreases as additive noise σ increases from 0.05 to 0.5. The Rashomon set of sparse decision trees also shifts toward simpler models (fewer leaves) as noise is injected, confirmed across 23 real-world and synthetic datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily theoretical and does not benchmark a proposed method against competing algorithms. Instead, the empirical validation compares models trained on noisy data against the theoretical prediction that they should match models trained on clean data with stronger regularization. In Figure 1(a)–(b), the observed number of leaves in optimal sparse decision trees trained on noisy data (solid lines) closely tracks the number of leaves predicted by Theorem 1 (dashed lines, i.e., clean data with regularization λ/(1−2ρ)), confirming the equivalence. In Figure 1(c), the ℓ₂ norm of linear model weights under additive noise matches the theoretical prediction from Theorem 9. The paper also compares the complexity distribution of Rashomon set models under different noise levels (Figure 3), showing that the distribution of leaf counts shifts leftward (simpler) as noise increases, with the effect visible across all 24 datasets. The generalization gap between train and test accuracy shrinks with more noise, consistent with the increased effective regularization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the qualitative observation from Semenova et al. (2023) that noise in data generation leads to simpler models by providing precise quantitative equivalences: for 0-1 loss with random label noise, the effective regularization parameter becomes λ/(1−2ρ), and for exponential loss with additive Gaussian attribute noise, it becomes σ²/2. It builds on the Rashomon set framework from Semenova et al. (2022) to show that noisy-data Rashomon sets provably contain simpler models, and on Bishop's (1995) classic result that attribute noise acts as Tikhonov regularization for neural networks, extending that insight to linear models under exponential loss and to sparse decision trees under 0-1 loss. The paper also introduces the concept of 'grounded models' and 'good features' to explain why unregularized hypothesis spaces still benefit from noise through an enlarged set of usable features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative theoretical guarantees connecting noise levels to model simplicity, which is directly actionable for policymakers and practitioners in high-stakes domains (criminal justice, lending) where outcome noise is inherent. This supports the argument that simple, interpretable models are provably sufficient when data contains noise, potentially shifting regulatory guidance away from black-box models. Key limitations include the restriction to expected loss over noise draws (not single draws), coverage of only 0-1 and exponential losses, and the uniform label noise assumption. Open questions include extending results to hinge/logistic losses, non-uniform noise models, and bounding complexity for single noise draws at finite sample sizes.
