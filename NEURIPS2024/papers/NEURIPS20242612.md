## Problem

Classical online bipartite matching focuses on maximizing the total number of matches, leaving fairness among different online agent types unaddressed. This paper introduces long-run fairness—maximizing the minimum matching rate across all online types—as the objective for online matching under known stationary Poisson arrivals, and establishes the first competitive algorithms and hardness bounds for this problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242612/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a bipartite eligibility graph G=(I,J,E), integer capacities b_i for each offline agent type, and known Poisson arrival rates λ_j for each online type, all available before the time horizon [0,1] begins. Upon each arrival, the algorithm must make an immediate, irreversible decision to either reject the agent or assign it to one eligible offline agent with remaining capacity. Performance is evaluated by the long-run fairness metric: the minimum over all online types j of E[X_j]/λ_j, where X_j is the expected number of type-j agents served.

## Method

The core algorithm SAMP solves a benchmark LP (maximizing a scale variable s subject to capacity and demand constraints) and uses the optimal solution {x*_ij, s*} to define a sampling distribution: when type j arrives, it samples offline neighbor i with probability x*_ij/(s*·λ_j) and assigns if i still has capacity, otherwise rejects. The key insight is that the LP serves a dual role—providing a valid upper bound on the clairvoyant optimum while simultaneously shaping the online sampling probabilities. SAMP achieves (1−1/e)-competitiveness and is asymptotically optimal (competitiveness → 1) when minimum offline capacity b→∞, when s*→0+ (demand dominates supply), or when s*→∞ (supply dominates demand). Hardness results show that no non-rejecting policy can exceed 1/2-competitiveness, no policy can exceed (√3−1)≈0.732-competitiveness, and SAMP's (1−1/e) bound is tight. Extensions include SAMP-G and RESERVE for group-level fairness, and Prob-Rej for short-run fairness, each with matching competitive guarantees.

## Results

The SAMP algorithm achieves a competitive ratio of at least 1−1/e ≈ 0.632 for OM-LF, and is asymptotically optimal (competitive ratio → 1) when the minimum offline capacity b→∞, or when demand dominates supply (s*→0⁺), or when supply dominates demand (s*→∞). For the single-offline-agent case, FCFS is 1-competitive (optimal). Hardness results show that no non-rejecting policy can exceed 1/2-competitiveness, no randomized policy can exceed (√3−1) ≈ 0.732-competitiveness, and SAMP's 1−1/e bound is tight. For group-level fairness, SAMP-G achieves a competitive ratio of 1−e^{−b}b^b/b! (approaching 1 as b→∞), and for short-run fairness, FCFS is 0.863-competitive when λ≤1 while no algorithm can exceed 0.942 when b=λ=1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are between algorithmic guarantees and hardness lower bounds. SAMP's 1−1/e competitive ratio is provably tight (Theorem 2, Part 3), meaning no improvement is possible within the SAMP framework. SAMP strictly surpasses the 1/2 barrier that applies to all non-rejecting policies (including FCFS in multi-server settings), demonstrating that the ability to reject incoming agents is essential for better fairness. The (√3−1) ≈ 0.732 upper bound for any randomized policy establishes a gap between SAMP's guarantee (≈0.632) and the best possible algorithm, leaving room for future improvement. For group-level fairness, FCFS drops from 1-competitive (individual fairness, single server) to zero-competitive (group fairness, single server), highlighting a fundamental tension between individual and group fairness objectives. The short-run fairness results show that even FCFS degrades to 0.863-competitive (from 1-competitive under long-run fairness), and the 0.942 upper bound at b=λ=1 indicates that short-run fairness is inherently harder to achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online bipartite matching literature under known stationary (Poisson) arrivals by replacing the standard total-weight-maximization objective with a fairness objective: maximizing the minimum service rate across online agent types. It builds on the KIID/Poisson arrival framework of Huang & Shu [19] and Manshadi et al. [31], and uses dependent rounding [16] in its RESERVE and Prob-Rej algorithms. A key departure from Ma et al. [28], which studied fairness among *offline* agents under integral arrival rates, is the focus on fairness among *online* types with arbitrary (fractional) arrival rates, requiring a parameter-dependent competitive analysis rather than a parameter-free one. The benchmark LP here plays a dual role—serving both as an upper bound on OPT and as the sampling distribution for the online algorithm—unlike prior benchmark LPs that only provide upper bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first competitive guarantees and hardness bounds for fairness among online types in online matching, with the √3−1 ≈ 0.732 upper bound contributing to the short list of known hardness results for OM-KIID. The striking contrast between FCFS being 1-competitive for individual long-run fairness (single offline agent) yet zero-competitive for group-level fairness highlights that fairness objectives interact non-trivially with group structure. Open questions include extending the analysis to unknown arrival distributions, addressing the root causes of differential service rates, and developing algorithms that avoid the FCFS-violation side effect of rejecting earlier arrivals to preserve capacity for protected groups.
