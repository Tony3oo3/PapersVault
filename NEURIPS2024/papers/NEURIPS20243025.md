## Problem

Existing CAT methods optimize point-estimation accuracy (e.g., MSE of ability scores) but this does not guarantee correct student ranking, which is the critical objective in high-stakes selective exams. The paper formally defines the 'ranking consistency dilemma' and demonstrates empirically that even state-of-the-art selection algorithms like BECAT can perform at or below random-selection level on ranking metrics. The asynchronicity and independence of individual test-takers during CAT further prevent leveraging cross-student information to resolve ranking inconsistencies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-calibrated question bank Q with IRT parameters (discrimination a_i, difficulty b_i), the tested student's real-time binary responses, and a set of M collaborative students who have already answered all questions in Q (their responses serve as fixed anchors). At each of T steps it outputs one selected question, and at test completion it outputs a scalar ranking score defined as the mean over collaborative students of a sigmoid-weighted indicator of whether the tested student outperforms each anchor, not a point ability estimate. In practice, missing collaborative responses are imputed via the IRT probability P_i(theta_c), and the unknown true-ability comparison is approximated by the current MLE estimate passed through a sigmoid.

## Method

CCAT introduces a collaborative framework with two coupled components. For question selection, it replaces the standard information-maximization criterion with a ranking-consistency objective that picks questions where higher-ability collaborative students are likely to succeed while the tested student may fail, and vice versa for lower-ability anchors, weighted by the estimated probability of each ability ordering. For ability estimation, it discards the IRT MLE point estimate in favor of a collaborative voting score: the tested student's final result is the mean over all M collaborative students of a sigmoid of the weighted sum of response differences, effectively counting how many anchors the student outperforms. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves via Hoeffding's inequality that with a sufficient number of collaborative students, the probability of correct pairwise ranking among tested students is at least 1-delta, even when each individual tested-vs-collaborative comparison has error probability rho. The framework is plug-and-play: it can wrap any existing selection algorithm (FSI, KLI, MAAT, BECAT, etc.) by simply replacing the final IRT estimate with the collaborative ranking score.

## Results

On intra-class ranking consistency (Kendall coefficient), CCAT achieves 0.8543 on NIPS-EDU and 0.9066 on JUNYI at 20 test steps (IRT via GD), outperforming the best baselines (NCAT: 0.8359, KLI: 0.8817). The advantage is most pronounced at short test lengths: at 5 steps, CCAT reaches 0.8092 on JUNYI versus 0.7748 for KLI (the strongest baseline), a gap of ~3.4 percentage points. With MCMC-estimated IRT, CCAT attains 0.8149 (NIPS-EDU) and 0.8448 (JUNYI) at 5 steps, again leading all baselines. The paper reports an average ~5% improvement in ranking consistency over other methods, with gains concentrated in short-test scenarios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CCAT outperforms all six baselines on ranking consistency across both datasets and most test lengths. Against the strongest traditional baselines (FSI, KLI), CCAT leads by 1–3 percentage points, with the gap widening at shorter test lengths (e.g., +3.4 pts over KLI at 5 steps on JUNYI). Against data-driven methods, CCAT's advantage is substantial: NCAT drops to 0.5198 at 5 steps on JUNYI (vs. CCAT's 0.8092), and BOBCAT lags by over 10 points at 5 steps on NIPS-EDU (0.5770 vs. 0.7533). The one exception is NCAT at 5 steps on NIPS-EDU (0.7611 vs. CCAT's 0.7533), where NCAT's reinforcement-learning-based selection slightly edges out CCAT. BECAT, the SOTA accuracy method highlighted in the paper's motivation, is consistently outperformed by CCAT by 3–6 points, confirming the paper's central claim that accuracy-optimized selection does not guarantee ranking consistency. The ablation CCAT(w/o C) (CCAT's question selection with standard IRT estimation) already beats most baselines, showing that the collaborative question selection itself contributes meaningfully, while the collaborative ability estimation (the -C component) adds a further 1–2 point gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the well-established CAT literature rooted in Item Response Theory (IRT) and its various question selection strategies (FSI, KLI, MAAT, BOBCAT, NCAT, BECAT), but fundamentally reframes the optimization objective from minimizing ability estimation error (MSE) to maximizing ranking consistency among students. The collaborative student mechanism draws on collaborative ranking ideas from information retrieval, repurposing them as fixed anchors that mediate pairwise comparisons between otherwise independent test-takers. The theoretical guarantee via Hoeffding's inequality connects to standard concentration-of-measure arguments, while the practical framework is designed to be a drop-in replacement for any existing selection algorithm, preserving the IRT estimation pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that state-of-the-art accuracy methods (e.g., BECAT) can perform at or below random level in student ranking challenges a core assumption in the CAT community that better ability estimation automatically yields fairer assessments — a concern that is critical for high-stakes exams like GRE and GMAT where relative standing determines outcomes. The collaborative framework provides a clean theoretical guarantee (ranking consistency ≥ 1−δ with sufficient anchors) and a ~5% average improvement, with the largest gains in short-test scenarios where accuracy methods are most fragile. Key limitations include the strong assumption that collaborative students have answered the entire question bank, the diminishing advantage as test length grows, and evaluation on only two datasets; open questions include how to handle truly asynchronous real-world deployment and whether the collaborative anchor set can be dynamically updated.
