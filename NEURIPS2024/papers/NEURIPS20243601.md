## Problem

Medical imaging (MI) datasets on Community-Contributed Platforms (CCPs) like Kaggle and HuggingFace are often shared without the documentation, licensing, and metadata that their clinical context demands. Unlike general computer vision datasets, MI datasets require de-identification, patient-level data splits, and demographic/origin metadata to avoid biased or over-optimistic models, yet the current CCP governance model fails to enforce these practices, leading to uncontrolled duplication, vague licenses, and missing critical information.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes 30 datasets (10 medical imaging, 10 computer vision, 10 NLP) selected systematically from Papers with Code by modality, and consumes their documentation via Kaggle and HuggingFace APIs, original hosting websites, and associated publications. Outputs include a structured comparison of licensing, persistent identifiers, and storage across platforms; a gap analysis mapping CCP data-card fields against the Datasheets for Datasets framework; and a quantitative inventory of duplicate datasets (e.g., 640 ISIC-related datasets totaling 2.35 TB on Kaggle versus 38 GB at the source).

## Method

The paper conducts a multi-pronged empirical audit: (1) it queries Papers with Code to select the top-10 most-cited datasets per modality, then catalogs each dataset's original hosting source, distribution terms, license, and presence on CCPs and regulated platforms (TensorFlow, Keras, PyTorch); (2) it automatically extracts documentation parameters from Kaggle and HuggingFace data cards via their public APIs and categorizes them against the seven Datasheets dimensions (motivation, composition, collection, preprocessing, uses, distribution, maintenance); (3) it performs a case study on the ISIC skin-lesion archive, quantifying uncontrolled proliferation (27 HF datasets, 640 Kaggle datasets) and identifying missing licenses and source citations in highly-downloaded copies; (4) it extends the duplication analysis to other MI datasets (BraTS, INBreast, PAD-UFES-20, ACDC) and checks whether patient-level split information survives the re-upload process. The key differentiator is the explicit comparison of MI datasets against CV and NLP baselines to isolate the additional governance failures specific to clinical data, and the mapping of platform-specific documentation fields to established data-governance frameworks rather than treating CCP metadata as sufficient.

## Results

Across 30 top-cited datasets (10 CV, 10 NLP, 10 MI), the majority of CV datasets lack a clear license, and less than half of MI datasets specify one. The duplication problem is severe: ISIC skin lesion datasets (38 GB original) appear as 640 copies totaling 2.35 TB on Kaggle, while BraTS has 350 duplicates and LIDC-IDRI has 43 duplicates totaling 539.7 GB (vs. 69 GB original). Patient-level data splits are clearly reported for only 6 out of 10 MI datasets, and HuggingFace documentation is more structured than Kaggle's, though both leave critical Datasheets fields (preprocessing, uses, motivation) largely empty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no algorithmic baselines. The key comparisons are between platforms and dataset categories. HuggingFace provides more structured documentation (task categories, annotation creator info, version history) than Kaggle, but both leave Datasheets-critical fields like preprocessing, cleaning, and motivation largely unfilled. CV and NLP datasets are predominantly hosted on author/university websites with unspecified licenses, while MI datasets use a wider range of sources (grand-challenge, PhysioNet, university sites) but are less commonly available on regulated platforms (TensorFlow, Keras, PyTorch). MI datasets face uniquely severe consequences from missing metadata: without patient IDs, data splits can leak patient information across train/test sets, producing overoptimistic results, and without scanner or demographic metadata, spurious correlations (e.g., chest drains, pen marks) go undetected. The paper finds that even Kaggle's 100% usability score does not guarantee meaningful documentation, as the scoring covers only 4 of 7 Datasheets aspects and allows 'never' for update frequency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the data governance and documentation literature (FAIR principles, Datasheets for Datasets, data cascades) by applying it specifically to the practical reality of Community-Contributed Platforms (Kaggle, HuggingFace) for medical imaging datasets. While prior work has proposed governance frameworks [40, 122, 54] and audited dataset provenance at scale [76, 33], and Yang et al. [129] analyzed HuggingFace documentation, no prior study systematically compared MI, CV, and NLP datasets across CCPs with attention to MI-specific concerns like patient-level splits, de-identification, and scanner metadata. The paper carves new ground by demonstrating that the CCP model, designed to democratize data access, actively degrades MI dataset quality through uncontrolled duplication and metadata loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings have direct practical implications: practitioners downloading MI datasets from Kaggle or HuggingFace may unknowingly use duplicates missing patient IDs (causing overoptimistic benchmarks), lacking demographic metadata (hiding biases), or with ambiguous licenses (creating legal risk). The paper's call for commons-based governance with paid data stewards and PhysioNet-style vetting is a concrete but under-resourced recommendation that requires institutional buy-in. Open questions include how to quantify documentation completeness meaningfully, how to track dataset versions across informal updates, and whether the neuroimaging community's more mature open-data practices can be adapted to broader MI. A key limitation is the study's scope (30 datasets, primarily quantitative) and the authors' acknowledgment that qualitative input from practitioners and affected populations is still needed.
