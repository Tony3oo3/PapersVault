## Problem

Existing test-time adaptation (TTA) methods operate independently on each device, ignoring valuable knowledge learned by other devices in multi-device deployments. This leads to suboptimal adaptation performance, infeasibility on resource-limited devices that cannot support backpropagation, and catastrophic forgetting during lifelong adaptation. The paper addresses how to enable efficient, privacy-preserving, and communication-light knowledge sharing across heterogeneous devices during the test phase.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243905/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes batches of test images (e.g., 64×224×224 RGB) along with a pre-trained ViT-Base or CLIP-RN50 model and a shared set of domain vectors—each being the delta of affine norm-layer parameters (~0.15 MB per vector) accumulated from prior adaptations. It outputs class predictions and, upon detected domain shifts, new domain vectors that are stored and intermittently shared across devices. Domain shift detection relies only on stem-layer feature statistics (mean and std) updated via exponential moving average, requiring no data preservation.

## Method

CoLA maintains a shared set of domain knowledge vectors T, each representing learned norm-layer parameter deltas for a specific domain, and automatically detects domain changes via KL-divergence between online-estimated and current-batch stem-layer statistics. For resource-abundant Principal Agents, it jointly optimizes new domain-specific parameters Δθ and a reweighting vector α (with adaptive temperature T_l) via backpropagation to reprogram existing shared knowledge while learning new knowledge (Eqn. 2). For resource-limited Follower Agents, it performs forward-only, optimization-free adaptation by aggregating shared domain vectors weighted by inverse KL-divergence between the current distribution and each stored distribution, with a fixed temperature T_f to encourage diverse knowledge mixing (Eqn. 3–5). The approach is plug-and-play, integrating with existing TTA objectives (entropy minimization, contrastive learning, etc.) and requiring only intermittent transmission of small parameter deltas rather than full model weights or raw data.

## Results

On ImageNet-C (level 5), CoLA as a follower agent (forward-only, no backpropagation) achieves 64.1% average accuracy with ETA-based shared knowledge, compared to 29.9% for the unadapted model and 28.2–30.5% for existing BP-free methods (T3A, LAME), a gain of over 30 percentage points while maintaining nearly identical inference latency (51 s vs. 50 s) and memory (821.9 MB vs. 816.6 MB). In the lifelong TTA setting (10 rounds, 150 corruptions), ETA+CoLA reaches 64.8% average accuracy versus 46.4% for ETA alone (+18.4 pp), and achieves up to 78× sample-efficiency speedup. On principal agents under collaborative TTA, DeYO+CoLA attains 62.6% average accuracy across three devices, outperforming EATA (61.5%) and CoTTA (44.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoLA is evaluated as a plug-and-play module on top of five backpropagation-based TTA methods (CoTTA, ETA, EATA, SAR, DeYO) and compared against two BP-free methods (T3A, LAME). On follower agents (Table 3), CoLA(ETA) achieves 64.1% average accuracy on ImageNet-C, surpassing NoAdapt (29.9%), T3A (28.2%), T3A* (30.5%), and LAME (29.4%) by 33–36 pp, while matching standard inference speed (51 s vs. 50 s) and memory (821.9 MB vs. 816.6 MB). In the lifelong TTA setting (Table 1), ETA+CoLA reaches 64.8% average over 10 rounds versus 46.4% for ETA alone and 59.3% for EATA, demonstrating that CoLA mitigates catastrophic forgetting far more effectively than the anti-forgetting regularizer in EATA. Under collaborative TTA across three principal devices (Table 2), DeYO+CoLA attains 62.6% average, edging out EATA (61.5%) and substantially outperforming CoTTA (44.2%). In single-domain TTA (Table 4), CoLA consistently adds 1–3 pp over its integrated baseline (e.g., ETA+CoLA 59.3% vs. ETA 56.1%), and on unseen distributions (Table 6) it still yields a 4.4 pp gain for SAR. The only scenario where CoLA does not clearly dominate is the first corruption in lifelong TTA, where the weight-decay regularizer on Δθ slightly reduces initial performance (e.g., 61.4% ETA vs. 62.0% ETA+CoLA is a gain, but SAR+CoLA drops from 59.1% to 59.1% in round 1), though the advantage grows rapidly in subsequent rounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-device test-time adaptation (TTA) paradigm—exemplified by methods like ETA, SAR, DeYO, and CoTTA—into a multi-device collaborative setting where devices with heterogeneous computational resources share learned domain knowledge. Conceptually, it draws on parameter-wise model merging (Adamerging, WEMOE) for the knowledge aggregation mechanism, on MECTA's KL-divergence-based shift detection for automatic domain boundary identification, and on the anti-forgetting regularization idea from EATA for lifelong adaptation. The key departure from prior work is the explicit principal/follower agent distinction: principal agents perform backpropagation-based knowledge reprogramming (Eqn. 2), while follower agents perform optimization-free, similarity-weighted knowledge aggregation (Eqn. 3), enabling TTA on devices that cannot support gradient computation at all.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: real-world deployments involve many devices with varying resources, yet all prior TTA methods assume a single device adapting in isolation. The follower-agent result—boosting accuracy from ~30% to ~64% on ImageNet-C with only forward passes and no backpropagation—demonstrates that cross-device knowledge sharing can make TTA feasible on edge devices where it was previously infeasible. Limitations include the evaluation being confined to ImageNet corruption benchmarks with a single architecture (ViT-Base), the assumption that devices can intermittently communicate learned norm-layer parameters, and the restriction of domain vectors to affine parameters of normalization layers. Open questions include how the approach scales to truly heterogeneous model architectures, whether the domain vector representation captures sufficient knowledge for more complex distribution shifts, and how privacy is maintained in adversarial multi-device settings.
