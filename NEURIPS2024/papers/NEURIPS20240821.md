## Problem

Algorithmic losses built from differentiable relaxations of non-differentiable algorithms (sorting, shortest-path) are non-convex and suffer from vanishing and exploding gradients, making them a bottleneck in training deep networks. While second-order optimization of the full network is computationally prohibitive and can hurt generalization, the loss function itself operates in a much lower-dimensional output space, suggesting that curvature information can be exploited cheaply at the loss level without applying second-order methods to the network parameters.

## Input / Output

For ranking supervision, the input is a set of n four-digit MNIST digit images (n ∈ {5, 10, 15}), and the network outputs one scalar per image; supervision is only the relative ordering (a ground-truth permutation matrix), not the absolute values. For shortest-path supervision, the input is a 12×12 Warcraft terrain map rendered as a 96×96 RGB image, and the network predicts a 12×12 cost embedding matrix; supervision is only the ground-truth shortest path from top-left to bottom-right, while the underlying cost matrix remains hidden. In both cases the loss is defined between the output of a differentiable algorithm (permutation matrix or path indicator) and the weak supervision signal.

## Method

Newton Losses splits the optimization into two alternating steps: (1) take one Newton step (or empirical Fisher-preconditioned step) on the loss ℓ in the network's output space to compute a target z*, and (2) train the network with standard SGD using a convex MSE loss between the network output and z*. Two variants are provided: a Hessian-based variant that inverts the (regularized) Hessian of the loss for a stronger curvature estimate, and an empirical Fisher variant that approximates curvature from outer products of gradients, requiring only first derivatives and enabling an in-place backward-pass modification (InjectFisher). Tikhonov regularization (strength λ) ensures the curvature matrix is well-conditioned even when the loss is non-convex. Because the loss operates on the output dimension m (typically small) rather than the full parameter space, the matrix inversion is cheap, and the Fisher variant adds negligible runtime overhead. The approach is loss-agnostic and can be applied on top of any existing differentiable algorithm without modifying the algorithm itself.

## Results

On the four-digit MNIST sorting benchmark (n=10), Newton Losses more than double the ranking accuracy for the hardest-to-optimize baselines: NeuralSort improves from 24.26% to 48.76% (Hessian) and SoftSort from 27.46% to 55.07% (Hessian). For the already well-optimized Cauchy DSN, improvements are modest (55.29% → 56.49% at n=10). On the Warcraft shortest-path benchmark, Hessian Newton Losses improve Perturbed Optimizers with Fenchel-Young losses by up to 2.7% (80.71% → 83.45% at 30 samples), while Fisher-based Newton Losses provide consistent small gains across all shortest-path variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Newton Losses consistently outperform all seven baselines across both benchmarks. The largest gains appear for NeuralSort and SoftSort at n=10, where vanishing/exploding gradients are most severe: Hessian Newton Losses roughly double ranking accuracy (24.26%→48.76% and 27.46%→55.07%), while the Fisher variant also improves substantially (39.23% and 54.00%). Logistic DSN benefits dramatically at n=10 (12.31%→42.14% with Hessian), though the Fisher variant is less effective here (25.72%). Cauchy DSN, already quasi-convex and well-optimized, shows only marginal improvements (~1% at n=10), confirming that Newton Losses target the hardest-to-optimize losses. On the Warcraft shortest-path benchmark, AlgoVision-relaxed Bellman-Ford (already at 94–96%) sees small but consistent Fisher-based gains (e.g., 95.90%→96.08% for For+L2²). Stochastic smoothing improves by 1–2% with 10+ samples, and Perturbed Optimizers with Fenchel-Young losses gain up to 2.7% with the Hessian variant at 30 samples, while the Fisher variant is essentially neutral for Fenchel-Young losses (as their gradient structure makes the empirical Fisher uninformative).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established threads: differentiable algorithmic losses (NeuralSort, SoftSort, DSNs, AlgoVision, stochastic smoothing, perturbed optimizers) and second-order optimization (Newton's method, quasi-Newton, Fisher-based pre-conditioning). The key conceptual departure is applying second-order information to the *loss function* (low-dimensional, cheap to evaluate) rather than to the network parameters (high-dimensional, expensive), thereby decoupling the two: Newton's method on the loss, SGD on the network. The authors explicitly position this as the first work combining second-order optimization of loss functions with first-order optimization of neural networks for algorithmic losses. They extend stochastic smoothing (Abernethy et al.) to estimate Hessians of smoothed functions and extend perturbed optimizers with Fenchel-Young losses (Berthet et al.) to compute the Hessian of the FY loss, enabling the Hessian-variant Newton loss in those settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Newton Losses provides a nearly drop-in, computationally cheap (especially the Fisher variant, which is in-place during backprop) improvement for a broad class of algorithmic supervision losses, with the practical advantage of robustness to the single hyperparameter λ over 6+ orders of magnitude. The method is most impactful for losses that suffer from vanishing/exploding gradients (NeuralSort, SoftSort, Logistic DSN at n=10), where it more than doubles accuracy, but yields only marginal gains for already well-optimized losses (Cauchy DSN). Limitations include the requirement that the loss be non-convex, differentiable, and not solvable in a single GD step; the Hessian variant can be 10%–2.6× slower for analytical relaxations; and the approach is restricted to the output space of the network rather than its parameters. Open questions include whether the technique extends to very high-dimensional output spaces (mitigated by the Woodbury identity), whether it composes with other second-order network optimizers, and whether the improvements persist in larger-scale or more complex algorithmic settings beyond the two benchmarks tested.
