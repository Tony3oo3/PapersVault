## Problem

Standard molecular contrastive learning assigns hard positive/negative labels to augmented molecule pairs, but graph augmentations like node masking and edge removal can produce false positives (same molecule, structurally dissimilar) and false negatives (different molecules, structurally similar). This mislabeling confuses the learning signal and leads to suboptimal molecular representations, yet existing methods lack a principled mechanism to automatically detect and down-weight these erroneous pairs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241850/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 2D molecular graphs (atoms as nodes, bonds as edges) or 3D molecular graphs with static atom spatial positions, applying two stochastic augmentations per molecule to form a positive pair within a batch of N molecules. The encoder (GNN, Transformer, or Equiformer) produces a fixed-dimensional representation vector per augmented molecule, which is then used in a weighted contrastive loss. The final output is a molecular embedding suitable for downstream property prediction (classification via ROC-AUC or regression via RMSE/MAE) or protein-ligand binding pose prediction.

## Method

The paper reformulates molecular contrastive learning as a Bayesian model where each positive pair receives a learnable weight w_i^+ and each negative pair receives w_ik^-, both treated as random variables with Gamma or Bernoulli priors. To make posterior inference tractable, an auxiliary variable u_i is introduced via Bayesian data augmentation, yielding a joint posterior p(θ, u, w | D) with conjugate Gamma/Bernoulli conditionals that can be sampled directly. Training proceeds via stochastic EM: at each iteration, (u, w) are sampled from their posteriors given the current encoder, then the encoder parameters θ are updated by stochastic gradient descent on the resulting weighted contrastive loss (equivalent to the original InfoNCE loss with pair-specific weights). The Gamma prior is chosen for its conjugacy and flexibility in modeling positive continuous weights, while the Bernoulli option provides a discrete on/off mechanism for negative pairs. This framework is plug-and-play, integrated into MolCLR (2D GNN), Uni-Mol (3D Transformer), and Equiformer (3D equivariant Transformer) without architectural changes.

## Results

On MoleculeNet classification tasks, the proposed Gamma-prior variant achieves an average ROC-AUC of 80.1%, outperforming Uni-Mol (78.5% avg) and GEM (77.5% avg) by 1.3–2.6 points. On regression tasks, the Bernoulli variant achieves a mean RMSE of 0.883 and MAE of 18.541, improving over Uni-Mol (0.957 / 13.940) by 7.6% in RMSE. On QM9, the method achieves state-of-the-art results on 8 out of 12 quantum properties, with notable gains on α (0.037 vs 0.046 for Equiformer), ΔE (24.2 vs 30), and ZPVE (1.09 vs 1.26). On the non-chirality MoleculeNet, the method outperforms i-MolCLR by 2.0 points on average (85.1 vs 83.1 ROC-AUC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Uni-Mol (the strongest 3D baseline), the Gamma variant wins on 6 of 9 classification tasks (e.g., BBBP 76.7 vs 72.9, BACE 88.2 vs 85.7, HIV 83.0 vs 80.8) but trails on ClinTox (89.4 vs 91.9), SIDER (63.6 vs 65.9), and MUV (79.0 vs 82.1). Against GEM, the Gamma variant leads on BBBP (+4.3), BACE (+2.6), Tox21 (+2.0), and PCBA (+3.0), while GEM retains an edge on SIDER (+3.6) and MUV (+2.7). On the non-chirality MoleculeNet, the method surpasses i-MolCLR on 7 of 9 tasks with a 2.0-point average gain (85.1 vs 83.1), with the largest margin on BACE (94.8 vs 88.5) and Tox21 (84.9 vs 79.9), though i-MolCLR leads on ClinTox (95.4 vs 91.4) and MUV (90.8 vs 88.0). On QM9, the method achieves the best MAE on 8 of 12 properties (e.g., α: 0.037 vs 0.046 Equiformer; ΔE: 24.2 vs 30; ZPVE: 1.09 vs 1.26), though Equiformer remains stronger on E_homo (15 vs 21.1) and R² (0.251 vs 0.082). In the protein-ligand binding pose task, the Gamma variant outperforms Uni-Mol at 3.0 Å (88.42% vs 87.02%) and 5.0 Å (96.84% vs 94.04%), while the Bernoulli variant leads at 1.0 Å (48.77% vs 43.16%). Appendix D shows that the Bayesian inference weighting outperforms both fingerprint-based and encoder-based similarity weighting on 7 of 9 MoleculeNet tasks, confirming the advantage of the learned probabilistic approach over heuristic similarity metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the molecular contrastive learning lineage initiated by MolCLR (Wang et al., 2022) and its 3D successor Uni-Mol (Zhou et al., 2023) by replacing hard positive/negative pair assignments with a probabilistic weighting scheme. The key conceptual departure is from i-MolCLR (Wang et al., 2022), which mitigates faulty negatives via a fixed fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity penalty; here, the authors instead treat pair weights as latent random variables with Gamma or Bernoulli priors, inferred via Bayesian data augmentation and optimized through stochastic EM (Nielsen, 2000). The Bayesian augmentation trick itself is borrowed from Chen et al. (2022), who showed that introducing an auxiliary exponential variable makes the InfoNCE posterior conjugate, and the authors transplant this idea into the molecular graph setting with per-pair weights.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The false positive/negative pair problem is a genuine and under-addressed limitation of graph-augmentation-based contrastive learning for molecules, and the probabilistic weighting framework offers a principled, architecture-agnostic remedy that can be plugged into GNN, Transformer, or equivariant backbones. However, the empirical gains are moderate (≈1.3% ROC-AUC on MoleculeNet classification, modest QM9 improvements), and the authors themselves note that on smaller datasets like QM9 the benefit is marginal, raising questions about when the added Bayesian machinery is worth its computational overhead. Open questions include whether the independence assumptions on molecular features hold in practice, whether the approach scales to the 100M+ molecule regime without the quadratic weight-storage cost, and whether the stochastic EM procedure converges reliably at very large batch sizes.
