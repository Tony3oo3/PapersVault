## Problem

Despite widespread use of in-context learning in large language models, the internal mechanism by which a model identifies and locates the task from its prompt context remains largely unexplored. Prior work has focused on prompt engineering (which examples to provide) or characterized ICL through linear functions, but has not identified at which layer in the forward computation the model transitions from recognizing the task to executing it. This paper asks: where in the layer stack does in-context learning actually happen?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained causal decoder-only LLM (GPT-Neo 2.7B, BLOOM 3B, LLaMA 3.1-8B, StarCoder2-7B) and a standard ICL prompt consisting of optional natural-language instructions, 5 few-shot examples (source-target pairs for MT or program descriptions for code), and a query sentence, all delimited by neutral 'Q:'/'A:' tokens. The key experimental variable is the masking layer index ℓ: from layer ℓ onward, all attention weights from query and generated tokens to context tokens (instructions and/or examples) are set to −∞. The output is a layer-wise performance curve (BLEU for translation, Pass@1 for code) as ℓ sweeps from 1 to n_ℓ, from which the task recognition layer and an upper bound on computational savings are extracted.

## Method

The core technique, 'layer-from context-masking,' applies a causal attention mask over the prompt context starting at layer ℓ and incrementing ℓ across all layers, revealing a three-phase performance curve: near-zero performance (context not yet processed), a steep jump of >80% of ceiling performance at critical middle layers (task location), and a plateau where further context attention is unnecessary (task recognized). The authors ablate over five masking settings—masking examples only, instructions+examples, the query, or the entire input—to isolate which context components drive task recognition. They further validate the findings with two complementary analyses: (1) training discrete attention-head gates with differentiable L0 regularization, showing only ~10% of heads are redundant (contrasting with >50% pruning in supervised MT), and (2) single-layer LoRA fine-tuning experiments demonstrating that the most effective layers for lightweight task-location tuning are precisely those preceding the task recognition point. The key insight is that once the task is encoded into the hidden representations at the recognition layer, all subsequent self-attention over the prompt context is computationally redundant, yielding up to 45% savings with 5 examples.

## Results

The paper identifies a 'task recognition' layer where attention to in-context examples is no longer needed: layer 14/32 for LLAMA3.1-8B on en→fr MT, ~layer 25 for GPTNEO2.7B, ~layer 15–20 for BLOOM3B, and layer 19 (3B) / 20–23 (7B) for STARCODER2 on code generation. Masking context attention from the task-recognition layer onward yields ~45% computational savings with 5 prompt examples on LLAMA3.1-8B. Only ~10% of attention heads are fully maskable via L0-regularised gates, in stark contrast to >50% prunable heads in supervised MT models. LoRA fine-tuning of individual layers shows peak effectiveness strictly before the task-recognition point (layers 10–15 for LLAMA on fr→en), with no layers successfully tuned for en→fr under the 400-example budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'comparisons' take the form of model-to-model and ablation contrasts. Instruction-tuned LLAMA3.1-8B-INSTRUCT and non-instruction-tuned LLAMA3.1-8B reach the same task-recognition plateau (~layer 14 for en→fr, ~layer 13 for fr→en), but the instruct model achieves higher BLEU in earlier layers when instructions are present and examples are masked, confirming its instruction-following advantage. For code generation, LLAMA2-7B shows only a gradual masking effect (no sharp task-recognition jump) due to poor base performance, whereas STARCODER2 exhibits the same sharp three-phase pattern seen in MT, validating that the phenomenon is task-agnostic. On the L0 attention-gate experiments, regularised gates (λ=0.01) generalise across prompt counts (0 vs. 5) and language directions (en→fr vs. en→pt) with no more than 0.5 BLEU degradation, while unregularised soft gates (λ=0) overfit and deteriorate on out-of-domain conditions. The ~10% maskable-head finding directly contrasts with supervised MT literature (Voita et al., Michel et al.) where >50% of heads are prunable, suggesting decoder-only ICL models distribute task information more uniformly across heads.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the task-recognition framing of in-context learning (Pan 2023; Xie et al. 2021) and the function/task vector literature (Todd et al. 2023; Hendel et al. 2023), which established that ICL activates pre-existing latent task representations. It introduces a complementary mechanistic lens—layer-from context-masking—that localizes *where* in the depth of the network this recognition occurs, extending layer-wise interpretability methods (Hewitt & Liang 2019; Voita et al. 2019; Phang et al. 2021) to the ICL setting. The attention-redundancy and pruning literature (Michel et al. 2019; Voita et al. 2019; Bian et al. 2021) provides the conceptual backdrop for the efficiency claims, but this paper repurposes those ideas to show that redundancy is over the *context tokens* rather than over individual heads, a distinction that contrasts sharply with supervised MT pruning results.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication of ~45% inference savings when using 5-shot prompts is significant for deployment cost, and the finding that LoRA fine-tuning for task location is most effective at earlier layers (before the task-recognition point) directly contradicts the common PEFT heuristic of targeting later layers. However, the paper lacks a theoretical framework explaining *why* task recognition localizes to specific middle layers, and the results are demonstrated only on machine translation and code generation—tasks with unambiguous success criteria. Open questions include whether the three-phase pattern generalizes to more ambiguous tasks (classification, reasoning), why different models exhibit different task-recognition depths, and whether the identified critical layers are stable across prompt formulations and example counts.
