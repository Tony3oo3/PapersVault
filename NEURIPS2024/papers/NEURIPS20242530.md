## Problem

Existing graph diffusion models either treat graph data in a continuous state space (losing topological sparsity and requiring discretization) or operate in discrete-state discrete-time settings (e.g., DiGress), where the number of sampling steps is rigidly tied to the training schedule. The discrete-state continuous-time formulation for graph generation has never been studied, leaving open the question of whether one can preserve the discrete nature of graph data while gaining the flexible, step-size-adjustable sampling that continuous-time diffusion provides.

## Input / Output

At generation time, the model samples an initial graph from a reference distribution (uniform or empirical marginal over node/edge types) and then simulates the reverse CTMC from time T to 0 using τ-leaping, where the step size τ (and hence the number of sampling steps) is a free hyperparameter decoupled from training. The output is a complete graph represented by an edge-type matrix E ∈ {1,…,a+1}^{n×n} (where type a+1 denotes absence of an edge) and a node-type vector F ∈ {1,…,b}^n, both with categorical attributes. During training, the input is a noisy graph G_t obtained by applying the forward CTMC to a ground-truth graph G_0 for a randomly sampled time t ~ U(0,T).

## Method

DISCO formulates graph corruption as a factorized continuous-time Markov chain where each node and edge type independently transitions according to a shared rate matrix scaled by a corruption schedule β(t), converging to a uniform or marginal reference distribution. The reverse process is parameterized by a graph-to-graph backbone (either the DiGress graph Transformer or a lighter MPNN with PNA aggregation and FiLM conditioning) that predicts the clean graph probabilities p_{0|t}^θ from the noisy graph, and the training objective is a cross-entropy loss whose connection to the reverse rate-matrix approximation error is rigorously bounded (Theorem 3.3). Sampling uses τ-leaping to batch multiple node/edge transitions per step, making the number of reverse steps adjustable at inference time. The paper further proves that both the training loss and the sampling distribution are permutation-invariant, and that the MPNN backbone is permutation-equivariant, providing principled inductive biases for graph-structured data.

## Results

On plain graph benchmarks (SBM, Planar), DISCO-GT achieves competitive performance with DiGress: on SBM, DISCO-GT obtains Deg. MMD 0.8 vs. DiGress 1.6, but slightly lower Validity (66.2% vs. 67.5%); on Planar, both achieve similar Orb. MMD (~1.7) and Validity (~84–85%). On QM9, DISCO-MPNN substantially outperforms DiGress on Valid-Unique-Novel (68.7% vs. 31.8%) while matching on Validity (98.9% vs. 99.0%). On MOSES, DISCO-GT achieves higher Validity (88.3% vs. 85.7%) and Novelty (97.7% vs. 95.0%) than DiGress, though DiGress retains a slight edge on FCD (1.19 vs. 1.44).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISCO-GT, which shares the Graph Transformer backbone with DiGress, achieves near-parity on plain graph benchmarks: on SBM it improves Deg. MMD (0.8 vs. 1.6) and Clus. MMD (0.8 vs. 1.5) but trails slightly on Validity (66.2% vs. 67.5%); on Planar both models are essentially tied on Orb. MMD (1.7) and Validity (~84–85%). On QM9, DISCO-MPNN dramatically outperforms DiGress on V.U.N. (68.7% vs. 31.8%) and V.U. (98.7% vs. 95.2%), while DISCO-GT matches DiGress on Validity (99.3% vs. 99.0%) but lags on V.U.N. (56.2% vs. 31.8% — actually DISCO-GT is better here). On MOSES, DISCO-GT beats DiGress on Validity (88.3% vs. 85.7%), Novelty (97.7% vs. 95.0%), and Scaf (15.1 vs. 14.8), while DiGress retains advantages on FCD (1.19 vs. 1.44) and SNN (0.52 vs. 0.50). On GuacaMol, DISCO-GT edges out DiGress on Validity (86.6% vs. 85.2%) and FCD (59.7 vs. 68.0, lower is better), while DiGress slightly leads on KL divergence (92.9 vs. 92.6). DISCO-MPNN consistently trails DISCO-GT on Validity across all datasets (e.g., 41.9% vs. 66.2% on SBM, 68.7% vs. 86.6% on GuacaMol), suggesting the lighter MPNN backbone has lower expressiveness for enforcing structural constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: the continuous-time Markov Chain (CTMC) framework for discrete denoising (Campbell et al., τ-LDR) and the discrete-state diffusion approach to graph generation (DiGress). While DiGress established that treating graph topology and features as discrete categorical variables preserves structural properties lost in continuous-state formulations, it was locked into a fixed number of sampling steps equal to the training diffusion steps. DISCO is the first to combine the discrete-state formulation (preserving graph sparsity and categorical structure) with continuous-time dynamics (enabling flexible, decoupled sampling via τ-leaping), and it further introduces a lightweight MPNN backbone as an alternative to the graph transformer used in DiGress.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The continuous-time formulation gives practitioners a post-training knob (step size τ) to trade off generation quality against sampling cost, which is practically valuable for applications like drug discovery where thousands of molecules may need to be generated. The rigorous Theorem 3.3 connecting the cross-entropy training loss to the rate-matrix approximation error provides a principled justification that was previously missing in the continuous-time discrete diffusion literature. Open questions include whether the MPNN backbone's expressiveness gap (visible in validity on Planar graphs) can be closed without resorting to full self-attention, and how the approach scales to graphs with thousands of nodes where the O(n²) edge factorization becomes prohibitive.
