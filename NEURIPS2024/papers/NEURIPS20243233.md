## Problem

Preference learning algorithms like DPO and RLHF are designed to train LLMs to assign higher likelihoods to preferred outputs over dispreferred ones, yet the relationship between the training objective, ranking accuracy, and downstream win rate has remained unclear. This paper asks: do these algorithms actually learn the preference rankings they are supposed to learn, and if not, why do they fail?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a reference model π_Ref (typically an SFT checkpoint), a pairwise preference dataset with per-triple rater proportions α(x,y_w,y_l), and a preference-tuned model π_θ, then computes sequence likelihoods by factorizing into per-token conditional probabilities. Outputs include empirical ranking accuracy (both length-normalized and unnormalized), a closed-form idealized ranking accuracy R* derived from the reference model and rater proportions, and the alignment gap between the two. For datasets with n>2 outputs (e.g., UltraFeedback), ranking accuracy is generalized to exact-match over the full permutation of all candidates.

## Method

The paper derives two key theorems. Theorem 3.1 shows that perfectly optimizing DPO or RLHF on ground-truth Bradley-Terry preferences yields an optimal policy whose likelihood ratio is the reference model ratio scaled by (α/(1-α))^(1/β), giving a closed-form idealized ranking accuracy that is computable without training. Theorem 4.1 proves that for a datapoint with reference model log-ratio c, the DPO loss must drop below -log σ(βc) to flip the ranking, revealing that even mild reference model errors (small positive c) require vanishingly small losses to correct. Empirically, the authors train GPT-2, Pythia 2.8B, and Llama 2-7B with DPO on HH-RLHF and show that <10% of initially misranked points are flipped before overfitting, and that ranking accuracy and win rate correlate only while the model remains close to the reference, diverging (anti-correlating) once the model moves far from π_Ref.

## Results

Across four state-of-the-art preference-tuned models (Zephyr-7B-DPO, Tulu-2-DPO-7B, Gemma-7B-IT, Llama-2-7B-Chat), the observed ranking accuracy on the Alpaca Farm validation set is only 40–42% (non-length-normalized) or 53–54% (length-normalized), far below the idealized ranking accuracy of 67–100% (non-length-normalized) or 73–100% (length-normalized) achievable under perfect DPO/RLHF optimization. This yields an alignment gap of 19–59 percentage points (measured to the median idealized value). On broader datasets (HH-RLHF, Stanford Human Preferences, UltraFeedback, StackExchange), ranking accuracies for both reference and preference-tuned models remain below 60%, with UltraFeedback exact-match accuracy dropping to as low as 1.2–3.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analytical paper rather than a method comparison, so it does not propose a new algorithm to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares observed model performance against a theoretically derived idealized upper bound (Corollary 3.3) computed from the reference model and ground-truth preference proportions. All four evaluated preference-tuned models fall well short of this bound: Gemma-7B-IT shows the largest gap (19 pp to median idealized R̃*), while Zephyr-7B-DPO and Llama-2-7B-Chat show gaps of ~44 pp. The paper further compares on-policy (win rate via Alpaca Eval GPT-4) and off-policy (ranking accuracy) metrics, finding they are strongly correlated when the model is close to the reference model but become anti-correlated as the model diverges, with win rate declining even as ranking accuracy continues to increase. Theorem 4.1 provides a per-datapoint formula showing that even a mild reference model log-ratio error (c > 0) requires the DPO loss to drop below −log σ(βc) to flip the ranking, explaining why DPO rarely corrects incorrect rankings in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the DPO objective (Rafailov et al., 2023) and RLHF/PPO (Stiennon et al., 2020), analyzing them through the lens of ranking accuracy—a metric that motivated their original design but has not been systematically measured. It extends the theoretical framework of the Bradley-Terry model and the KL-constrained reward maximization to derive closed-form expressions for idealized ranking accuracy (Theorem 3.1) and the loss threshold required to flip a ranking (Theorem 4.1). Conceptually, it departs from prior analyses of preference learning (e.g., Azar et al. on IPO, Tang et al. on generalized preference optimization) by focusing specifically on the conditioning effect of the reference model and the gap between off-policy classification behavior and on-policy generative behavior, rather than on loss landscape properties or reward model quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that state-of-the-art preference-tuned models achieve less than 60% ranking accuracy—far below the idealized ceiling of 97–100%—challenges the standard narrative that DPO and RLHF successfully teach models to prefer human-chosen outputs, and suggests that the reference model's initial (often incorrect) rankings are largely preserved. Theorem 4.1 provides a simple, tunable-free formula for identifying hard-to-learn datapoints, which could inform data curation and curriculum strategies. Open questions include whether these findings generalize beyond the single training dataset (HH-RLHF) used in the dynamics experiments, how the alignment gap evolves with model scale, and whether iterative/on-policy variants can close the gap between ranking accuracy and win rate as the model diverges from the reference.
