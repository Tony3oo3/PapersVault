## Problem

Standard spectral GNNs apply nonlinear activation functions after linear spectral filter layers, which breaks equivariance to graph functional shifts (unitary operators commuting with the graph shift operator). This means multi-layer spectral GNNs cannot serve as universal approximators of continuous functions that respect these signal-space symmetries, unlike CNNs which maintain translation equivariance through their architecture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244070/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph with adjacency matrix, node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×d}, and a graph shift operator (combinatorial or normalized Laplacian). The analysis transform produces a fixed-dimensional vector of channel-wise norms of projections onto eigenspaces or filter bands, independent of the specific eigenvector basis chosen. Synthesis reconstructs node-level or graph-level representations by scaling the input signal's own projections, making the spectral domain a fixed Euclidean space transferable across graphs of different sizes and topologies.

## Method

The paper introduces Nonlinear Spectral Filters (NLSFs) that achieve full equivariance by applying nonlinear frequency responses (MLPs) to the norms of eigenspace projections rather than to the signal values themselves. The key mechanism is a new analysis-synthesis pair: analysis computes channel-wise norms of projections onto eigenspaces (or dyadic filter bands), and synthesis uses the input signal's own projections as the basis for reconstruction, eliminating dependence on arbitrary eigenvector choices. Three variants are defined—Node-level, Graph-level, and Pooling-NLSFs—along with a Laplacian attention mechanism that adaptively combines Index NLSFs (using the combinatorial Laplacian) with Value NLSFs (using the normalized Laplacian) via a softmax-weighted mixture. Theoretical results establish that linear NLSFs exhaust all linear operators commuting with functional shifts, and that NLSFs with MLP frequency responses have universal approximation properties under a pulled-back spectral metric.

## Results

On semi-supervised node classification, att-Node-level NLSFs achieve 85.37% on Cora, 75.41% on Citeseer, 50.58% on Chameleon, and 38.39% on Squirrel, outperforming all spectral GNN baselines on these four datasets. On graph classification, att-Pooling-NLSFs achieve the best accuracy on 6 of 8 benchmarks, including 86.89% on MUTAG, 71.02% on PTC, 69.94% on ENZYMES, 84.89% on PROTEINS, 55.28% on IMDB-M, and 82.19% on COLLAB. On the filtered Chameleon/Squirrel dense-split setting (addressing data leakage), att-Node-level NLSFs achieve 42.53% and 42.66% respectively, again outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On node classification (Table 1), att-Node-level NLSFs outperform the strongest spectral baselines on Cora (85.37% vs. JacobiConv 84.12%), Citeseer (75.41% vs. Specformer 73.45%), Chameleon (50.58% vs. Specformer 49.79%), and Squirrel (38.39% vs. Specformer 38.24%), while trailing APPNP on Pubmed (82.22% vs. 82.69%) and Actor (35.13% vs. 35.98%). On graph classification (Table 3), att-Pooling-NLSFs achieve the best accuracy on 6 of 8 datasets—MUTAG (86.89% vs. APPNP 84.45%), PTC (71.02% vs. ChebNetII 70.03%), ENZYMES (69.94% vs. ChebNetII 64.29%), PROTEINS (84.89% vs. DiffPool 79.47%), IMDB-M (55.28% vs. ARMA 53.60%), and COLLAB (82.19% vs. DiffPool 80.16%)—and rank second on NCI1 (80.95% vs. ChebNetII 81.14%) and IMDB-B (76.78% vs. ChebNetII 77.09%). The Pooling-NLSFs consistently outperform the Graph-level NLSFs across all graph classification benchmarks, confirming the theoretical expressivity advantage. On the filtered Chameleon/Squirrel dense-split (Table 2), att-Node-level NLSFs lead all baselines (42.53% and 42.66%), showing robustness to the data-leakage issue identified in [77]. Message-passing baselines (GCN, GAT, SAGE) are consistently outperformed by both the proposed NLSFs and stronger spectral methods, while kernel methods (GK, WL) are substantially weaker on all tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Geometric Deep Learning principle of respecting data symmetries from CNN translation equivariance to general graphs by defining 'graph functional shifts'—unitary operators commuting with the graph shift operator—as the relevant symmetry group. It departs from standard spectral GNNs (ChebNet, CayleyNet, ARMA, etc.) by identifying that their activation functions break functional-shift equivariance, and proposes Nonlinear Spectral Filters (NLSFs) that operate on eigenspace-projected signal norms rather than eigenvector-dependent spectral coefficients, making the spectral domain transferable between graphs. The Laplacian attention mechanism draws on random geometric graph analysis from Paolino et al. [74], and the low-rank approximation justification relies on the Constructive Weak Regularity Lemma from Finkelshtein et al. [29]. The signal-space equivariance framing connects to Lim et al. [64] on sign-equivariant spectral networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled equivariant framework for spectral GNNs with universal approximation guarantees, addressing a gap where standard spectral methods lack a natural active symmetry analogous to CNN translation. The transferability property—representing graphs as fixed-dimensional vectors in a graph-independent spectral space—opens the door to cross-graph generalization without retraining. However, a key limitation is that for large graphs the method relies only on leading eigenvectors and their orthogonal complement, potentially discarding informative intermediate-frequency content; the authors acknowledge this and suggest adaptive band selection as future work. The Laplacian attention mechanism, while practical, is somewhat ad-hoc and its theoretical grounding (uniform vs. non-uniform sampling) may not cover all real-world graph distributions.
