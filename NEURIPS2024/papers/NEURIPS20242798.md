## Problem

Classical Bayesian optimization assumes the optimizer can query any point in the search space at each step, but many physical systems (transient flow reactors, environmental monitoring vehicles, particle accelerators) impose Markov transition constraints that restrict which configurations are reachable from the current state. This necessitates planning over the full experimentation horizon rather than greedy one-step acquisition, a setting that prior BayesOpt methods either ignore or handle only with ad-hoc truncation heuristics.

## Input / Output

The method takes a black-box function with known (possibly stochastic) Markov transition dynamics, a GP prior with a specified kernel, an initial state, and a set of candidate maximizers defined via GP credible sets. It produces a history-dependent, non-Markovian policy over a horizon of H steps that respects all transition constraints, ultimately yielding a maximizer estimate and the surviving set of potential maximizers. For continuous search spaces, the dynamics must be expressible as a linear state-space model x_{h+1}=Ax_h+Ba_h, and the candidate maximizer set is approximated by a finite set of size K via Thompson Sampling or UCB maximization.

## Method

The key reformulation expresses the maximizer-identification utility (maximum pairwise posterior variance among candidate maximizers) as a convex function of the state-action visitation distribution d_π, leveraging Nyström feature embeddings so the objective depends only on visited states, not their order. The convex planning problem min_{d_π∈D} U(d_π) is solved via the Frank-Wolfe algorithm, where each linearization sub-problem reduces to a standard RL problem (solved exactly by dynamic programming for discrete MDPs, or heuristically via model predictive control for continuous spaces). Adaptive resampling—receding horizon re-planning at every step h of every trajectory t—incorporates all past visited states into the utility, yielding a non-Markovian policy despite each individual step following a Markov policy. The approach supports episodic, immediate, and asynchronous feedback, and the paper proves convergence to the optimal policy under episodic feedback via a Frank-Wolfe convergence argument adapted to the time-varying candidate maximizer set.

## Results

The paper evaluates MDP-BO (with Thompson Sampling or UCB variants) across real-world and synthetic benchmarks over 25 runs. On the Knorr pyrazole synthesis (discrete, episodic feedback, 10 episodes × 10 steps), MDP-BO achieves the highest correct-identification rate and lowest regret. On Lake Ypacarai (50-step episodes), MDP-BO correctly identifies the maximum in ~50% of runs by episode 2, while MDP-EI struggles for several episodes. On the free-electron laser (heteroscedastic, transition-dependent noise), MDP-BO substantially outperforms stateless BO. On continuous synthetic benchmarks (Michalewicz, Hartmann, SnAr, Branin, Levy), MDP-BO-UCB matches or exceeds LSR in the synchronous setting, while MDP-BO-TS outperforms TrSnAKe in the asynchronous setting with lower variance in chosen paths.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines: Greedy-UCB (no planning), MDP-EI (planning with Expected Improvement instead of the derived utility), TrSnAKe (truncated path-based BO minimizing movement distance), and LSR (local search region-constrained BO). MDP-BO clearly dominates Greedy-UCB and MDP-EI across all real-world benchmarks, with MDP-EI particularly struggling to distinguish between multiple optima on Lake Ypacarai. Against the strongest baselines, LSR performs very competitively in the synchronous setting, frequently matching MDP-BO-UCB on Hartmann and Michalewicz benchmarks, while TrSnAKe is competitive across all synthetic benchmarks. However, MDP-BO is more robust, exhibiting lower variance in chosen paths (visible in the 10th/90th quantile bands) and generalizing to more complex transition constraints beyond local box-constraints. In the asynchronous setting (25-iteration observation delay), MDP-BO-TS achieves the best performance, with TrSnAKe remaining competitive but less consistent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three research threads: the state-action distribution formulation and MDP planning framework from Mutný et al. [17], the variance-reduction utility for best-arm identification from Fiez et al. [30] (originally for linear bandits), and the Frank-Wolfe convex optimization machinery from Hazan et al. [34]. The key departure is extending this combination from the linear bandit setting to GP-based Bayesian optimization with transition constraints, and introducing adaptive resampling (receding horizon re-planning) to construct non-Markovian policies from Markovian sub-problems. Unlike prior constrained BO methods (SnAKe, LSR, TrSnAKe) that rely on truncation heuristics or local box constraints, this work formulates the problem as a principled sequential decision problem with provable convergence guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap: many physical systems (chemical reactors, environmental sensors, particle accelerators) impose hard transition constraints that render standard BO inapplicable, yet prior methods handled these with ad-hoc heuristics. The convergence guarantee (Proposition C.1) showing that greedy policy optimization with receding horizon approximates the optimal non-Markovian policy is a meaningful theoretical contribution. Open questions include extending the continuous-space variant beyond linear state-space models (the experiments use A=B=I), handling more general stochastic dynamics, and whether the identification-based objective can be replaced with richer criteria. The non-convexity of the MPC sub-problem in continuous settings remains a practical limitation requiring heuristic solvers.
