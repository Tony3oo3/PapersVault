## Problem

Autoregressive LLMs trained on 'A → B' consistently fail to infer the semantically equivalent reverse 'B ← A' (the reversal curse), yet no prior work provided a theoretical explanation rooted in the training process itself. Existing analyses focused on model expressivity or proposed empirical mitigations (data reversal, bidirectional attention) without explaining why standard cross-entropy optimization inherently produces this asymmetry.

## Input / Output

The models consume short token sequences (length 2–3) over a synthetic vocabulary of entity tokens and relationship tokens (e.g., 'A_i →' as a 2-token input) and output a next-token probability distribution via softmax over the full vocabulary. The bilinear model takes a single token embedding as input, while the one-layer transformer takes a sequence and applies self-attention over contextual tokens before decoding. The key evaluation metric is the probability assigned to the correct next token in the unseen (reversed or indirect) direction versus the seen (forward) direction.

## Method

The paper analyzes the training dynamics of (stochastic) gradient descent under cross-entropy loss for two autoregressive models: a bilinear model with logits x^T Θ y (a simplified one-layer transformer) and a full one-layer transformer reparameterized into attention-score matrix Z and decoder matrix Y. The central finding is that the CE gradient update for Y only modifies the row indexed by the contextual token, so training on sequence (x₁, x₂, x₃) increases Y(x₁, x₃) while leaving Y(x₃, x₁) at zero—producing a fundamental weight asymmetry that prevents reversal generalization. The authors prove a separation theorem showing the reversal loss remains Ω(1) even as the training loss approaches zero, and extend the framework to chain-of-thought to show intransitivity: learning A→B and B→C separately does not increase the weight for A→C. Empirical validation on multi-layer GPT-2 transformers confirms the predicted asymmetry in learned logits.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 3) shows that for one-layer transformers trained with SGD on three-token sequences, training sequences achieve next-token probability p → 1 while unseen reverse-direction test sequences remain at p ≤ 1/M (random guess level). Theorem 2 establishes that the reversal loss is lower-bounded by a constant C (e.g., log(m/2)) even when the training loss is driven arbitrarily small. Empirically, a 24-layer GPT-2 (12 heads, vocab size 800, 340 training pairs, 60 validation pairs) confirms this: training negative log-probability converges to near 0 while validation NLP remains flat at approximately ln(800) ≈ 6.68, matching the random-guess baseline. Weight visualizations (Figure 2) confirm the predicted asymmetry: logits for trained directions grow large while reverse-direction logits remain near zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper rather than a method comparison study, so there are no competing baselines to benchmark against. The paper's contribution is to explain *why* the reversal curse occurs (weight asymmetry under cross-entropy loss and unconstrained optimization) rather than to propose a new method that outperforms existing mitigations. The empirical validation in Section 5 compares the theoretical predictions (from the one-layer transformer analysis in Section 4) against multi-layer GPT-2 transformers, confirming that the reversal curse persists even with 24 layers and 12 attention heads. The paper also contrasts its training-dynamics perspective with prior expressivity-based analyses of chain-of-thought [15], arguing that the failure to deduce A~C from A→B and B→C is not a representational limitation but a consequence of the intransitivity of learned weights under standard training. No direct head-to-head comparison with mitigation strategies (e.g., reversed training data [12], bidirectional attention [13], or semantic permutation [11]) is conducted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the reparameterization framework and gradient analysis of Tian et al. [14] ('Scan and snap') for one-layer transformers, reusing their definitions of Z and Y matrices and their gradient lemmas, but applies them to a fundamentally different question: generalization failure on logical reasoning rather than optimization convergence. It departs from the empirical reversal curse literature (Berglund et al. [10], Allen-Zhu & Li [9]) by providing the first theoretical explanation rooted in training dynamics rather than data manipulation or architecture changes. For COT, it contrasts with Feng et al. [15], which analyzes chain-of-thought through transformer expressivity, by instead showing that weight intransitivity under gradient descent is the operative mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled explanation for why autoregressive LLMs fail at the reversal curse: the CE loss combined with unconstrained optimization inherently produces asymmetric effective weights, making the reverse direction unlearnable without explicit training examples. This reframes the problem from a data or architecture issue to a fundamental property of the training objective, with direct implications for why ICL, data augmentation, or planning remain necessary for multi-step logical reasoning. Key limitations include theoretical results restricted to one-layer transformers with three-token sequences and specific initialization assumptions, while multi-layer validation is purely empirical. Open questions include extending the analysis to multi-layer transformers theoretically, handling multi-token entities, and finding a unified framework covering reversal curse, COT, and other logical reasoning failures.
