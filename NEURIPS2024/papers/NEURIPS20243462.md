## Problem

While Chain-of-Thought reasoning is known to improve transformer performance both empirically and in terms of theoretical expressivity, the mechanistic conditions under which CoT capabilities emerge during training remain poorly understood. This paper addresses that gap by studying CoT emergence in a controlled setting of iterative tasks, where the contrast between single-token prediction (hard) and CoT (easy) is sharp and the attention patterns are interpretable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243462/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a token sequence of the form [Problem][x_1]...[x_L][EoI] where tokens are drawn from a finite field F_p (or binary), and must autoregressively generate the intermediate state sequence [s_1]...[s_L][EoS] that unrolls an iterative algorithm s_t = F(s_{t-1}, x_t). Three tasks are studied: binary copy (F(s,x)=x), parity (F(s,x)=s+x in F_2), and polynomial iteration (F(s,x)=XY+1 in F_11). Sequence lengths range from 1 to 32, with 1024 random sequences per length for training and testing.

## Method

The paper identifies and validates an 'iteration head': a two-layer, single-head-per-layer transformer circuit where the first attention head implements a 'Are you EoI?' query to retrieve the positional encoding p_{L+1}, and the second attention head performs position subtraction (computing t = (L+t)−(L+1)+1) to retrieve the t-th input token x_t. The residual stream carries the previously generated state s_{t-1}, and the second-layer MLP computes the successor function F(s_{t-1}, x_t) = s_t. The authors demonstrate this circuit emerges naturally during supervised training, validate it via attention patching (zeroing the EoI or p_t focus drops accuracy to near-random), and show that pretraining on an easier polynomial iteration task induces the iteration head, which then transfers to the harder parity problem in fewer than 30 fine-tuning epochs versus 1000 from scratch.

## Results

On the polynomial iteration task (P(X,Y)=XY+1 in F₁₁), a two-layer transformer with CoT achieves near-perfect test accuracy for sequence lengths up to L=32 when the embedding dimension d≥64, while the same architecture without CoT (next-token prediction only) shows a sharp accuracy drop for longer sequences, and a one-layer CoT transformer also underperforms (Figure 7). For the parity problem, pretraining on polynomial iteration for 200 epochs followed by fine-tuning on parity data achieves full accuracy in under 300 total epochs, compared to ~1000 epochs when training on parity data alone (Figure 9). Fine-tuning only the second-layer MLP of a copy-trained model on parity data reaches 100% accuracy in fewer than 20 epochs, confirming that the attention layers implement a task-agnostic iteration head while the MLP encodes the task-specific successor function F.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead ablates architectural and training choices. Two-layer CoT consistently outperforms two-layer next-token prediction (no CoT) on the polynomial iteration task, with the gap widening as sequence length increases and embedding dimension decreases (Figure 7). Two-layer CoT also outperforms one-layer CoT, confirming that the iteration head circuit requires at least two attention layers to implement position subtraction and state retrieval. For the parity problem specifically, next-token prediction with a small embedding (d=32) can actually match or slightly beat CoT for short sequences (L≤32), because the parity can be solved by a single-layer MLP performing uniform-attention summation; however, CoT generalizes better to longer sequences and induces the model to learn the underlying iterative structure rather than overfitting (Figure 10). The skill-transfer experiment shows that pretraining on the easier polynomial iteration task (which has a unique circuit due to order-dependence) and then fine-tuning on parity reduces total training epochs from ~1000 to <300, demonstrating that data curation toward tasks with a single dominant circuit accelerates learning of harder tasks with competing solutions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability and the theoretical study of transformer expressivity. It builds on the bottom-up interpretability tradition (Olsson et al.'s induction heads, Garg et al.'s in-context learning analyses) and the theoretical results showing that CoT lifts the expressivity ceiling of transformers (Merrill & Sabharwal, Li et al., Pérez et al.), but departs from both by identifying a concrete, named attention circuit — the 'iteration head' — that implements iterative reasoning, and by studying its emergence in a fully controlled setting. The paper also connects to the data-curation literature (Ma et al. on code data aiding reasoning) by showing, in a minimal setting, how pretraining on one iterative task induces the shared attention circuit that accelerates learning of another.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic, attention-level account of how CoT reasoning is implemented in a transformer, reducing a high-level phenomenon to a specific two-head query-key pattern and a position-subtraction operation. This gives a testable hypothesis for what to look for in larger LLMs and a concrete explanation for why data curation (code, math) may improve reasoning: it biases training toward forming reusable iteration-head circuits. Key limitations include the extremely small model scale (two layers, one head, d=128) and simple tasks, leaving open whether the same circuit structure persists in deep, multi-head architectures. The statelessness of transformers — requiring all intermediate states to be encoded in token space — is identified as a fundamental architectural constraint that more complex reasoning may not overcome.
