## Problem

Zero-shot coordination (ZSC) requires agents to generalize to unseen partners at deployment, but current evaluation methods suffer from a distribution gap between evaluation and deployment-time partners and rely on mean episode returns that ignore generalization performance. No comprehensive toolkit exists that simultaneously provides diverse, deployment-representative evaluation partners and metrics that capture the generalization gap rather than raw task performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241500/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The toolkit takes a trained ego agent policy and a cooperative game environment (Overcooked with 9 layouts or GRF 3v1 with Keeper) and produces a BR-Prox generalization score with 95% confidence intervals and inter-quartile ranges, a selected set of M evaluation partners (up to 30 in Overcooked, 6 in GRF), and algorithm performance rankings. Evaluation partners are generated by sampling event-based reward functions from a constrained linear space (bounded weight norm B_max=20, at most C_max=3 active events), so the designer specifies behavioral preferences rather than hand-crafting policies. The BR-Prox score is a ratio (ego performance / approximate BR performance) per partner, aggregated via inter-quartile mean to focus on the middle 50% of partners.

## Method

ZSC-Eval is a three-stage pipeline. First, it generates candidate evaluation partners by sampling behavior-preferring reward functions (linear combinations of event-based features plus the original game reward) and training each agent and its best response via independent PPO to approximate a Nash Equilibrium in the resulting Markov game. Second, it selects a representative subset by maximizing Best Response Diversity (BR-Div), defined as the determinant of the similarity matrix of the partners' best responses rather than the partners themselves, using Determinantal Point Process sampling; this ensures the selected partners require diverse coordination skills from the ego agent, since different partners can elicit similar BRs. Earlier training checkpoints of selected candidates are also included to span skill levels. Third, it measures ZSC capability via the BR-Prox metric, which computes the ratio of the ego agent's return to the approximate best response's return for each evaluation partner, then aggregates across partners using the inter-quartile mean to balance partners of differing cooperation capability and expose the generalization gap.

## Results

ZSC-Eval's evaluation results show the highest Spearman's rank correlation with human evaluation, achieving r_s = 0.90 on Coord. Ring and r_s = 1.00 on Counter Circ., compared to 0.60–0.90 for human proxy agents and 0.10–0.70 for trained self-play agents. In the Google Research Football benchmark, TrajeDi achieves the highest BR-Prox score of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.75–0.89), followed by HSP (0.80), FCP and MEP (both 0.78), COLE (0.75), E3T (0.66), and self-play (0.20). In Overcooked, co-play algorithms (FCP, MEP, TrajeDi, HSP) consistently outperform evolution-based (COLE) and end-to-end (E3T) methods, with performance gaps widening in more complex layouts featuring multi-recipe mechanisms and higher coordination demands.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six ZSC algorithms plus a self-play baseline using ZSC-Eval's BR-Prox metric. TrajeDi (0.81) and HSP (0.80) achieve the highest BR-Prox in GRF, with FCP and MEP tied at 0.78, indicating that co-play methods with explicit diversity mechanisms perform best. COLE (0.75), an evolution-based method, trails the top co-play methods by 3–6 points, while E3T (0.66), an end-to-end approach without a pre-trained population, lags significantly. Self-play (0.20) is the weakest baseline, confirming that agents trained only against themselves fail to generalize to unseen partners. Critically, ZSC-Eval's evaluation partners produce algorithm rankings most consistent with human evaluation (r_s = 0.90–1.00), whereas human proxy agents (r_s = 0.60–0.90) and trained self-play agents (r_s = 0.10–0.70) yield less reliable rankings, validating ZSC-Eval as a more faithful proxy for deployment-time partner distributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ZSC problem formulation from cooperative MARL (self-play, population-based training, fictitious co-play) and repurposes two ideas from adjacent fields: Determinantal Point Processes from submodular optimization for diversity-based subset selection, and inverse reinforcement learning / reward shaping for generating behavior-preferring evaluation partners. It departs from prior ZSC evaluation methods (human proxy agents, trained self-play pools, cross-play among trained agents) by proposing a unified generation–selection–measurement pipeline where evaluation partners are generated from event-based reward functions to approximate deployment-time distributions, selected via Best Response Diversity rather than partner diversity, and scored via the BR-Prox generalization metric rather than mean episode returns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ZSC-Eval is the first comprehensive, reproducible evaluation toolkit for ZSC, addressing the critical distribution gap between evaluation and deployment-time partners that has undermined prior comparisons. The finding that current ZSC algorithms perform worse with expert evaluation partners—revealed by BR-Prox—suggests a fundamental limitation in how these algorithms construct training populations, pointing toward the need for better diversity mechanisms at higher skill levels. Key limitations include the handcrafted nature of event-based rewards (which may not fully capture deployment-time partner distributions) and the current scope to two environments; the authors suggest automatic reward design via LLMs as a promising direction.
