## Problem

Existing poisoning defenses in federated learning (e.g., KRUM, FLTrust) require the server to inspect individual gradients in plaintext, making them fundamentally incompatible with secure aggregation protocols that hide per-user updates. The prior secret-sharing-based solution, BREA, resolves this conflict but incurs O(MN+N) per-user communication and O((N²+MN)log²N log log N) server computation, and assumes unrealistic direct client-to-client channels. RFLPA addresses both the efficiency bottleneck and the communication-model assumption.

## Input / Output

Each of N clients holds a local dataset and computes a gradient that is quantized into a finite field F_p before being split into packed Shamir shares; the server additionally maintains a small clean root dataset (e.g., 200 samples) used to compute a reference gradient g₀ for normalization and trust scoring. The protocol outputs a trained global model, per-client trust scores derived from cosine similarity with g₀, and the aggregated global gradient, while the server learns only the final scalar dot-product values (cosine similarity and norm square) and never the element-wise products or individual gradients. The server acts as a relay for all client-to-client secret-share exchange, so no direct peer-to-peer channel is required.

## Method

RFLPA runs a four-round protocol built on verifiable packed Shamir secret sharing, where l = O(N) gradient elements are encoded in a single degree-d polynomial to cut per-user communication from O(MN) to O(M+N). In rounds 1–2, clients distribute encrypted, signed gradient shares and locally compute partial dot products (cosine similarity and norm square) against the server's reference gradient, then perform a secret re-sharing step that converts degree-2d partial-product shares into degree-d final-product shares via matrix operations (B_{e_j}^{-1} Chop_d) and Reed-Solomon decoding. In round 3 the server verifies gradient norms, recovers the single scalar cosine similarity per client, and broadcasts trust scores; in round 4 clients perform FLTrust-style weighted aggregation on their local shares and the server reconstructs the global gradient. A novel dot-product aggregation protocol ensures the server receives only the final scalar dot product rather than the full element-wise product vector, preventing gradient reconstruction attacks that packed sharing would otherwise enable. Security over the server-mediated channel is guaranteed by Diffie-Hellman key exchange with symmetric encryption for secrecy and UF-CMA signatures for integrity, with formal security proven under DL, t-polyDH, and t-SDH assumptions.

## Results

On MNIST, RFLPA maintains 95% accuracy under 30% gradient manipulation attackers, compared to FedAvg's collapse to 32%. On CIFAR-10, RFLPA achieves 69% accuracy at 30% attackers versus FedAvg's 13%, while incurring only a 3.46% accuracy drop relative to the no-attack setting (74% → 69%). The framework reduces per-user communication cost by over 75% and per-user/server computation by over 80% compared to BREA, with per-client communication remaining stable at ~82.5 MB regardless of the number of participating clients.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FedAvg, RFLPA trades a small accuracy cost in the no-attack setting (e.g., 96% vs 98% on MNIST) for dramatic robustness gains under attack (95% vs 32% at 30% gradient manipulation on MNIST; 69% vs 13% on CIFAR-10). Compared to Bulyan and Trim-mean, RFLPA is competitive on MNIST but substantially outperforms them on CIFAR-10 under 30% attackers (69% vs 27% and 47% respectively), where those methods' reliance on pairwise distances or coordinate-wise medians breaks down. Against LDP and CDP, RFLPA dominates on CIFAR-10 (69% vs 12% and 12% at 30% gradient manipulation), where differential privacy noise destroys utility. Against BREA (the closest secret-sharing-based competitor), RFLPA achieves comparable accuracy on MNIST and F-MNIST (95% vs 93%, 82% vs 81% at 30% attackers) and a slight edge on CIFAR-10 (69% vs 68%), while reducing per-user communication from O(MN+N) to O(M+N) and cutting computation by over 80%. The HE-based baselines (PEFL, PBFL, ShieldFL) are excluded from accuracy comparison due to impractical per-iteration runtimes of 1.5–6.5 days, whereas RFLPA completes a single iteration in under 43 minutes per user at 400 clients.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on two lines of work: FLTrust (Cao et al., NDSS 2021) for the cosine-similarity-based robust aggregation rule, and BREA (So et al., IEEE JSAC 2020) as the first secret-sharing-based secure aggregation framework for Byzantine-robust FL. The key departure from BREA is the use of packed Shamir secret sharing to reduce per-user communication from O(MN+N) to O(M+N), combined with a novel dot product aggregation protocol that prevents the information leakage inherent in packed sharing. The paper also positions itself against HE-based approaches (PEFL, PBFL, ShieldFL) by arguing that secret sharing avoids their prohibitive computation costs and the reliance on two non-colluding parties.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a fundamental tension in federated learning: secure aggregation hides individual gradients from the server, but most poisoning defenses require inspecting those gradients in plaintext. The 75%+ communication reduction over BREA is practically significant for mobile-device FL deployments where bandwidth is constrained. However, the framework's reliance on a small clean root dataset at the server, its limitation to cosine-similarity-based aggregation (extending to rank-based methods like KRUM is acknowledged as challenging), and the absence of differential privacy guarantees remain open questions that limit its applicability in settings where the server cannot be trusted to hold any clean data.
