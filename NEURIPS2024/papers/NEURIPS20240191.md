## Problem

Two common estimators of the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) — the score-based and Hessian-based — have different variances that determine their accuracy, yet practitioners lack computable, parameter-wise bounds to decide which to use. Prior work derived variance expressions as 4D tensors whose norms were bounded but individual entries were not, making it impossible to quantify estimator quality for specific parameters or parameter groups in deep networks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a neural network parameterized by θ, input samples x drawn from a parameter-free marginal q(x), and output samples y drawn from an exponential family p(y|x;θ) specified by a log-partition function F and sufficient statistics t(y). It produces diagonal FIM entries I(θᵢ), their estimator variances Vⱼ(θᵢ|x), and tight upper/lower bounds that factor into a product of NN derivative norms (‖∂ᵢh(x)‖₂ or ‖∂ᵢ²h(x)‖₂) and eigenvalues of the sufficient-statistic moment matrices — a decomposition that scales linearly in dim(θ) rather than quadratically. The joint FIM variance further decomposes via the law of total variance into an input-randomness term (over x) and an output-randomness term (over y|x), with sample complexity O(1/Nₓ + 1/(Nₓ·N)).

## Method

The paper derives closed-form conditional variances for the diagonal entries of both FIM estimators (Lemma 3.1), showing V₁ depends on first-order gradients and the 4th central moment of t(y), while V₂ depends on second-order gradients and only the 2nd central moment. The key technical contribution is Theorem 4.1, which bounds these variances by separating NN derivative norms from eigenvalues of the sufficient-statistic covariance and 4th-moment tensors, using variational (Z-eigenvalue) definitions that mirror the Courant-Fischer min-max theorem. Corollary 4.2 and Proposition 4.3 further bound the tensor eigenvalues via matrix eigenvalues when t(y) is bounded (as in classification). Theorem 4.7 extends all bounds from conditional to joint FIMs via the law of total variance, and Lemma 4.8 makes the input-randomness term computable. The framework is instantiated for isotropic Gaussian regression (where all bounds become equalities) and categorical classification (where a tension between σ_max and 1−‖σ‖₂² governs the bound tightness), and validated empirically on MNIST with 4-layer MLPs across 20 training epochs.

## Results

For regression (isotropic Gaussian), the eigenvalue bounds in Theorem 4.1 become exact equalities: λ_min = λ_max = 1 for I(h|x) and λ̃_min = λ̃_max = 2 for the 4th-moment tensor, meaning the conditional FIM reduces to a Gauss-Newton matrix. For classification (categorical), the maximum eigenvalue is bounded by m(x) = min(σ_max(x), 1 − ‖σ(x)‖₂²), which transitions from ~1/C at initialization to ~0 as the network becomes confident. On MNIST (4-layer MLP, sigmoid, C=10, N=5000), the first-layer variances are ~5×10⁻⁵; the upper bound for V₁ is ~50× the true variance (log-ratio ≈ 4) while the UB for V₂ is ~10× tighter. Critically, V₂(θᵢ|x) = 0 for all parameters in the last layer, making Î₂ unconditionally preferred there.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines; instead it contrasts the two standard FIM estimators Î₁ (outer-product of score) and Î₂ (negative Hessian) on variance. In the first three layers of the MNIST MLP, V₁(θᵢ|x) < V₂(θᵢ|x), so Î₁ provides a more accurate diagonal FIM estimate. In the last layer, V₂(θᵢ|x) = 0 identically (Remark 4.4) while V₁ remains the largest across all parameter groups, making Î₂ strictly superior. For the second-last layer, V₂ can vanish when the pre-activation of a sigmoid neuron is zero (Remark 4.5), again favoring Î₂. The paper concludes that uniformly using one estimator is suboptimal: the choice depends on the layer position, activation function, and the spectrum of the exponential family's sufficient-statistic moments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the authors' prior work [37] (Soen & Sun, NeurIPS 2021), which derived closed-form variance expressions for full FIM estimators but was limited by intractable 4D tensor computations and norm-only bounds. By restricting to diagonal entries, the current work makes these variance quantities computable via automatic differentiation and provides element-wise upper/lower bounds that cleanly separate the neural network's derivative structure from the exponential family's moment structure. The paper also situates itself within the broader FIM approximation literature (unit-wise FIM, block-diagonal layer-wise FIM, GGN) and the empirical Fisher literature, clarifying how the unbiased estimators relate to the biased empirical Fisher used in optimizers like Adam.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a principled, computable criterion for choosing between the two standard FIM estimators (score-based vs. negative-Hessian-based) on a per-parameter basis, rather than defaulting to one universally. The layer-dependent trade-off discovered empirically (I1 preferred for early layers, I2 with zero variance for the last layer) has direct implications for natural gradient descent and FIM-based optimization in practice. Open questions include extending the analysis to block-diagonal FIM approximations (e.g., K-FAC) and incorporating estimator variance into adaptive optimizers, which the authors identify as future directions. The analysis is limited to diagonal entries and two specific exponential families (Gaussian, categorical), leaving the general multi-dimensional case partially open.
