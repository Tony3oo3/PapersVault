## Problem

Existing video restoration methods either target a single known degradation type or handle unknown but time-invariant degradations across all frames. In practice, degradations in real-world videos change over time (e.g., motion blur appears when objects start moving, noise emerges in low-light scenes), creating the time-varying unknown degradations (TUD) challenge that no prior unified method addresses. This paper is the first to study and tackle TUD with a single all-in-one model.

## Input / Output

The method takes a corrupted video clip (12 frames at 256×256 during training; full-resolution 854×480 or 960×540 at test time) along with pre-computed optical flow between consecutive frames (via SPyNet) and produces restored video frames. Degradations are synthesized by sampling from seven types (Poisson/Gaussian/speckle noise, resizing/Gaussian blur, JPEG/video compression) with random levels, applied in shuffled order to clips of fixed interval t (6, 12, or 24 frames) to simulate time-varying corruption. The model is trained on DAVIS (90 sequences) and evaluated on DAVIS-test (30 sequences) and Set8 (8 sequences).

## Method

AverNet employs an alternating bidirectional recurrent propagation scheme (four passes: backward, forward, backward, forward) with two prompt-based core modules. The Prompt-Guided Alignment (PGA) module addresses the larger pixel shifts caused by time-varying degradations by generating input-conditioned prompts via a Prompt Generation & Integration (PGI) block—attention-weighted combinations of learnable prompt embeddings—that guide the estimation of deformable convolution offsets and masks for frame alignment. The Prompt-Conditioned Enhancement (PCE) module handles multiple unknown degradations by extracting degradation-specific prompts from shallow features at sparsely selected key frames (interval T=6) and using them as conditions to enhance propagated features through residual blocks, effectively recasting multi-degradation restoration as a conditional restoration problem. Training uses Charbonnier loss with Adam optimizer over 600K iterations at batch size 1, with the optical flow network frozen for the first 5K iterations.

## Results

On DAVIS-test with t=12 (the standard evaluation setting), AverNet achieves 34.09 dB PSNR and 0.9339 SSIM, outperforming the strongest video restoration baseline RVRT (33.98 dB / 0.9311) and the best all-in-one image restoration method AirNet (32.46 dB / 0.8887). On Set8 with t=12, AverNet reaches 31.47 dB PSNR and 0.9145 SSIM, surpassing RVRT (31.39 dB / 0.9113) and AirNet (30.40 dB / 0.8806). AverNet also maintains the best efficiency among all methods, with a runtime of 6.93 s per 48-frame video—less than half of RVRT (15.07 s) and roughly one-sixth of Shift-Net (45.54 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AverNet is compared against four all-in-one image restoration methods (WDiffusion, TransWeather, AirNet, PromptIR) and four video restoration methods (EDVR, BasicVSR++, Shift-Net, RVRT). Against the strongest video baseline RVRT, AverNet wins by 0.08–0.18 dB PSNR on DAVIS-test and 0.08–0.20 dB on Set8 across all variation intervals, while running at less than half the runtime (6.93 s vs. 15.07 s). Against Shift-Net, the margin is much larger: up to 0.99 dB on DAVIS-test and 0.65 dB on Set8, with a 6.6× speed advantage. Against the best AIR method AirNet, AverNet gains at least 1.53 dB PSNR and 0.0428 SSIM on DAVIS-test and 1.02 dB / 0.0345 SSIM on Set8, demonstrating the critical importance of temporal modeling. EDVR performs worst among video methods (28.37 dB on DAVIS t=12), confirming that older architectures struggle with TUD. Overall, AverNet achieves the best PSNR and SSIM on nearly all test configurations while maintaining the highest inference efficiency among all compared methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

AverNet extends the bidirectional recurrent propagation paradigm of BasicVSR/BasicVSR++ to the all-in-one video restoration (AVR) setting, while adapting the learnable prompt mechanism from PromptIR (originally for all-in-one image restoration) to encode degradation-specific information in a video context. It departs from prior AVR methods (ViWS-Net, CUDN, Diff-TTA) that assumed a single unknown but time-invariant degradation type per video, by explicitly modeling degradations that change type and level across frames. The Prompt-Guided Alignment module repurposes deformable convolution (as in EDVR) but conditions its offset/mask estimation on input-conditioned prompts to handle the larger pixel shifts caused by time-varying degradations, while the Prompt-Conditioned Enhancement module selectively applies prompt-conditioned refinement at key frames to control computational cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AverNet addresses a practical gap: real-world videos rarely suffer from a single fixed degradation, yet prior AVR methods assume time-invariant corruption. The data synthesis pipeline for generating TUD training pairs is a useful contribution given the absence of real-world TUD benchmarks. However, the method is limited to seven synthetic degradation types (noise, blur, compression) and has not been validated on real-world videos or on additional degradations like rain, haze, or snow, which the authors acknowledge as future work. The modest performance gains over RVRT (0.08–0.34 dB) and the reliance on a fixed SPyNet optical flow estimator leave open questions about scalability to longer sequences and more complex real-world corruption.
