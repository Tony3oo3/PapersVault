## Problem

Existing DNA foundation models rely on tokenization strategies borrowed directly from NLP (single nucleotide, k-mer, BPE), which are ill-suited to genomic sequences that lack natural delimiters and whose biologically meaningful units are discontinuous, overlapping, and ambiguous. The optimal way to tokenize DNA remains unknown to humans, yet no prior work has allowed a model to autonomously discover an effective tokenization strategy through end-to-end learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242111/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

MxDNA takes a single-nucleotide DNA sequence (length l, hidden dimension d) as input and produces a shorter sequence of learnt tokens (length k ≈ 101.6 for l = 512) that are discontinuous, overlapping, and ambiguous by design. During pretraining, a cross-attention mechanism maps the token-level output back to single-nucleotide resolution so the model can reconstruct 15% masked nucleotides; during finetuning, a [CLS] token from the token-level output is used for classification. The tokenization is stochastic during training (via multiplicative jitter noise) but deterministic at inference.

## Method

MxDNA inserts a learnt tokenization module (replacing the 5th transformer block of a Nucleotide Transformer v2 100M backbone) that operates in two stages. First, a sparse Mixture of Convolution Experts scores the probability of basic units of 10 different lengths (kernel sizes 1–10) at every nucleotide position via a linear gating layer, applies 1-D non-maximum suppression to select non-overlapping units, and embeds each selected unit with its corresponding convolution expert. Second, a 1-D deformable convolution with learned offsets and modulation factors assembles the basic units into final tokens, explicitly enabling discontinuous and overlapping token composition. A multiplicative jitter noise (±1%) on the gating scores introduces ambiguity during training. The entire module is trained end-to-end via gradient descent on masked language modeling over the Human Reference Genome, with an auxiliary balancing loss to prevent expert collapse.

## Results

On the Nucleotide Transformer Benchmarks (18 datasets), MxDNA achieves a state-of-the-art average of 78.14±0.12, outperforming the second-best model DNABERT2 (76.66±0.20) by 1.48 points, with best performance on 10/18 datasets and top-2 on 16/18. On the Genomic Benchmarks (8 regulatory element classification tasks), MxDNA achieves 89.13±0.13 average Top-1 accuracy, beating DNABERT2 (88.29±0.19) by 0.84 points, with best performance on 5/8 datasets. Notably, MxDNA is pretrained only on the Human Reference Genome (~3 days on 4 A100 GPUs), while some competitors use multispecies data and longer training. The model reduces sequence length from 512 nucleotides to ~102 tokens via its learnt tokenization module, with only 0.914 GFLOPs additional overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MxDNA outperforms all four baselines on average across both benchmarks. Against DNABERT2 (the closest competitor, using BPE tokenization with 4096 vocabulary), MxDNA gains +1.48 points on NT Benchmarks (78.14 vs 76.66) and +0.84 on Genomic Benchmarks (89.13 vs 88.29), with the largest margins on histone marker tasks (e.g., H3K4me3: 63.68 vs 57.90). Against Nucleotide Transformer v2 100M (same backbone, single-nucleotide tokenization), MxDNA improves by +7.44 on NT Benchmarks and +1.00 on Genomic Benchmarks, demonstrating that the learnt tokenization module alone provides substantial gains. Against DNABERT (K-mer tokenization), MxDNA leads by +9.53 on NT Benchmarks and +1.63 on Genomic Benchmarks. Against HyenaDNA (single-nucleotide, long-range architecture), MxDNA achieves +2.18 on NT Benchmarks and +1.96 on Genomic Benchmarks, despite using a standard transformer with quadratic attention rather than a sub-quadratic architecture. MxDNA is the only model to achieve top-2 on 16/18 NT Benchmark tasks and 7/8 Genomic Benchmark tasks, and it does so with pretraining on only the Human Reference Genome rather than multispecies corpora.

## Relation to Prior Work

MxDNA repurposes the sparse Mixture of Experts paradigm (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022) — typically used for model scaling — as a mechanism for variable-length basic unit recognition in DNA, and adapts deformable convolution (Dai et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2019) from computer vision to model discontinuous and overlapping token assembly. It departs from the dominant practice in DNA foundation models (DNABERT, DNABERT2, Nucleotide Transformer, HyenaDNA) of borrowing fixed tokenization rules from NLP (single nucleotide, k-mer, BPE), instead letting the model learn tokenization end-to-end via gradient descent. The closest prior work is VQDNA, which shares the motivation of learned tokenization but adopts a vector-quantized codebook approach rather than the convolutional expert + deformable assembly used here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a model can autonomously discover a tokenization strategy superior to all human-designed rules challenges the assumption that genomic segmentation must be manually specified, and the t-SNE visualization showing functional clustering at the token level without fine-tuning suggests the learned tokens capture biologically meaningful structure. However, direct biological validation of the tokenization decisions remains absent, evaluation on long-range tasks is lacking due to quadratic self-attention cost, and pretraining was limited to the human reference genome. Open questions include whether the learned tokenization generalizes across species, whether it can be combined with sub-quadratic architectures for longer contexts, and whether the discovered segmentation patterns correspond to known genomic features.
