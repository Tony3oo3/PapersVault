## Problem

Existing spatial GNN methods, including SGCNs, do not truly generalize the CNN convolution layer to graphs: they fail to reduce to an equivalent CNN when trained on array data, breaking the powerful local inductive bias that makes CNNs successful. The fundamental obstacle is that CNN neighborhoods have fixed sizes and fixed node ordering, a convenience absent in general graphs. This paper asks whether a GNN layer can be constructed that is formally and empirically identical to a 2D CNN on image data while extending naturally to arbitrary graph structures.

## Input / Output

The QGCL layer consumes graph node feature vectors, the adjacency structure, and optionally (pseudo-)positional descriptors to produce updated node feature vectors of the same dimensionality. When applied to 2D image data converted to positional graphs (edges between adjacent pixels), the layer produces outputs bit-identical to a standard 2D convolutional layer with matched parameters. For the FEM benchmark, inputs are fluid velocity components on an adaptive mesh graph; for the EEG application, inputs are spectral power features across 32 electrodes in 4 frequency bands arranged as a fully-connected graph, with outputs used for both signal reconstruction and valence classification.

## Method

The Quantized Graph Convolution Layer (QGCL) decomposes the CNN convolution into a set of non-overlapping sub-kernels, each paired with a binary mask that selects which neighbor nodes contribute to that sub-kernel's weighted sum. For graphs with positional information, a 'satisficing mapping' divides the angular space around each center node into H non-overlapping bins, assigning each neighbor to exactly one sub-kernel; the minimum H ensuring injective assignment is computed per dataset. For arbitrary graphs without positional information, QuantNet—an MLP that projects node features to a high-dimensional space, computes pairwise differences, and applies Gumbel-Softmax hard sampling—learns the sub-kernel assignment end-to-end as a multinomial classification. The QGCL is then wrapped in a Quantized Graph Residual Layer (QGRL) with two residual blocks (one around the QGCL, one around the message-passing MLP) to mitigate vanishing gradients and over-smoothing. A formal proof (Theorem 1) and empirical validation on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10 confirm that the satisficing-mapping QGCL is exactly equivalent to a 2D CNN convolutional layer on image data.

## Results

On graph classification benchmarks, QGRN achieves 99.50% on AIDS, 75.58% on Frankenstein, 99.99% on Mutag, and 80.20% on Proteins, outperforming or matching all 8 comparison GNNs (GCNConv, ChebConv, GraphConv, SGConv, GENConv, GeneralConv, GATv2Conv, TransformerConv). On the novel Navier-Stokes FEM datasets, QGRN matches SGCN on NS-Binary (99.67%) and outperforms it on NS-Denary-1 (97.47% vs 94.23%) and NS-Denary-2 (95.13% vs 93.33%). On node classification, QGRN is competitive on homophilic datasets (89.02% Cora, 89.11% PubMed) but trails GeneralConv on heterophilic ones (74.15% vs 78.11% on Chameleon; 56.17% vs 66.80% on Squirrel). QGCN empirically matches CNN accuracy on image data (98.98% vs 98.92% MNIST, 92.39% vs 92.56% Fashion-MNIST, 79.59% vs 80.21% CIFAR-10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

QGRN outperforms all 8 graph classification baselines on Frankenstein (75.58% vs. next-best GENConv at 66.74%, a 8.8-point margin) and Enzymes (72.50% vs. GENConv at 67.22%), and on Coil-Del (94.14% vs. GENConv at 81.91%). On AIDS, Mutag, and Proteins, QGRN matches or narrowly exceeds the strongest baselines (GENConv, GATv2Conv, TransformerConv) by 0.3–1.6 points. Against SGCN specifically (the closest architectural competitor), QGRN wins on all three Navier-Stokes denary/binary FEM tasks and on all Letter and AIDS positional datasets, while using fewer FLOPs (535M vs. 800M on NS datasets). On node classification, QGRN is competitive on homophilic graphs (89.02% Cora, 89.11% PubMed) but underperforms GeneralConv on heterophilic graphs (74.15% vs. 78.11% Chameleon; 56.17% vs. 66.80% Squirrel), acknowledging a known limitation of message-passing GNNs on heterophilic data. In the EEG supervised autoencoder task, QGRN improves AUC over SGCN (0.59 vs. 0.56) and reduces generative MSE (1787 vs. 2169).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a formal generalization of the CNN convolution layer to graphs, departing from prior spatial GNNs (GCNs, SGCNs, KerGNNs, LGCLs) that the authors argue do not truly reduce to equivalent CNNs on array data. The core idea decomposes the convolution into non-overlapping sub-kernels with binary masks, then extends this to graphs via an angular 'satisficing mapping' (for positional graphs) or a learnable multinomial assignment network (QuantNet, for arbitrary graphs). The residual architecture (QGRL) adapts the ResNet framework [He et al., 2015] to the QGCL layer, and the overall design draws inspiration from dilated convolutions with learnable spacings for the QuantNet component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formal equivalence proof between QGCL and 2D CNN on image graphs provides a clean theoretical bridge that prior spatial GNNs lack, and the QuantNet mechanism offers a principled way to handle graphs without positional information. However, the practical impact is tempered by a significant computational cost: QGRN inference latency is roughly 5–10× higher than competing GNNs (e.g., ~12–25 ms vs. ~1–6 ms on TPU), and the performance gains over strong baselines like GENConv are often modest (1–5% on most benchmarks). Open questions include whether parallelized sub-kernel operations can close the efficiency gap, whether the framework extends to odd kernel sizes or stride > 1, and whether deeper QGRNs can overcome the observed performance degradation with depth.
