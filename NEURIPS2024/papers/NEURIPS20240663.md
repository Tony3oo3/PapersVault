## Problem

Semantics and pragmatics are typically studied in isolation, with pragmatic models assuming a fixed shared lexicon and emergent communication work focusing on utility maximization without information-theoretic constraints. The paper tackles the under-explored question of how a shared, human-like lexicon can co-emerge from local, context-sensitive pragmatic interactions, rather than being given a priori.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240663/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, agents receive 512-dimensional ResNet-18 features extracted from two objects (a target and a distractor) within the same naturalistic image from ManyNames, with the target identity randomly assigned. The speaker produces a discrete communication signal drawn from a 3000-vector codebook; the listener uses it to identify the target (pragmatic setting) or reconstruct the speaker's mental representation without context (semantic setting). At test time, the emergent lexicon is evaluated against ~36 free-naming labels per object produced by native English speakers, using metrics including Normalized Information Distance, lexicon size, and complexity.

## Method

The paper extends the VQ-VIB architecture (Tucker et al., 2022) by training speaker-listener agent pairs in a pragmatic reference game where both observe a shared visual context of two objects, and the speaker must communicate which is the target. The training objective jointly optimizes three terms weighted by λ_U, λ_I, λ_C: task utility (listener discrimination accuracy), informativeness (negative MSE between speaker and listener mental representations), and complexity (a variational bound on mutual information between meanings and words). Agents are trained via unsupervised self-play with parameter annealing across 270 λ combinations, and target-distractor pairs are selected from the same image using Bottom-Up object detection to encourage natural semantic partitions. The key finding is that only a non-trivial tradeoff (λ_Eng* = 0.005, 0.98, 0.015) yields a lexicon matching English in size, complexity, and semantic alignment, while any single-objective extreme produces degenerate, non-human-like systems.

## Results

The best-aligned model (λ_Eng* with λ_U=0.005, λ_I=0.98, λ_C=0.015) achieves the lowest Normalized Information Distance (NID) of 0.55±0.00 with English, the smallest lexicon size difference (ΔLexSize = 22±35), and the lowest complexity difference (ΔCompl. = 1.48±0.08), while maintaining good pragmatic utility (0.72±0.01) and low reconstruction error (MSE = 0.23). In contrast, the utility-only extreme (λ_U=1) achieves the highest utility (0.95) but the worst semantic alignment (NID = 0.84) and reconstruction error (MSE = 20). The informativeness-only extreme (λ_I=1) achieves the best MSE (0.13) but produces an overly large lexicon (ΔLexSize = 1687) and high complexity (ΔCompl. = 2.80). The complexity-only extreme (λ_C=1) fails entirely, yielding the worst utility (0.58) and NID (0.99).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead ablates the three components of its own training objective (utility, informativeness, complexity) by setting each to 1 in turn, serving as baselines for the proposed balanced tradeoff (λ_Eng*). The balanced model wins on all three human-likeness metrics: it reduces NID from 0.84 (utility-only) and 0.99 (complexity-only) to 0.55, shrinks the lexicon size gap from 559 (utility-only) and 1687 (informativeness-only) to just 22, and reduces the complexity gap from 2.80 (informativeness-only) and 5.21 (complexity-only) to 1.48. However, it trades off some pragmatic utility (0.72 vs. 0.95 for utility-only) and reconstruction quality (MSE 0.23 vs. 0.13 for informativeness-only). The key finding is that no single extreme achieves human-like properties: utility-only agents overfit to the discrimination task, informativeness-only agents produce overly complex lexicons, and complexity-only agents collapse to a single uninformative signal. Only the non-trivial combination of all three pressures yields a lexicon that simultaneously matches English in size, complexity, and semantic partitioning while supporting successful pragmatic interaction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VQ-VIB framework of Tucker et al. (NeurIPS 2022), which integrated utility maximization with information-bottleneck objectives for emergent communication, by adapting it to a pragmatic training setting where agents observe shared conversational context. It departs from the standard assumption in both pragmatic models (RSA, rate-distortion views of pragmatics) and prior emergent communication work that the lexicon is either given a priori or studied only through utility-driven training. The key conceptual move is training agents in a pragmatic (context-sensitive) setting and then evaluating their emergent lexicon in a separate semantic (context-free) setting, thereby directly probing the co-evolution of semantics and pragmatics rather than treating them independently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a computational framework for a long-standing open question in cognitive science: how a shared, human-like lexicon can emerge from local pragmatic interactions without any human supervision. The finding that informativeness pressure dominates (λ_I ≈ 0.98) while very small amounts of complexity and utility pressure suffice for human-like properties offers a concrete, testable account of the relative weights of different communicative pressures in language evolution. Key limitations include evaluation on a single language (English), focus on lexical items only (no syntax or morphology), and a moderate NID alignment score (0.55) that leaves a gap to full human-like semantics. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes across languages, whether the pre-trained visual features introduce hidden linguistic bias, and how the approach scales to compositional and syntactic structure.
