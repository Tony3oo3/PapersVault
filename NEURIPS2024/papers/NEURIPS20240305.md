## Problem

Optimizing graph diffusion probabilistic models (DPMs) for arbitrary, often non-differentiable reward signals (e.g., docking scores, graph validity) is a critical need in drug design and structured data generation, yet directly applying the policy gradient methods developed for continuous-variable DPMs (DDPO, DPOK) to discrete graph DPMs empirically fails to converge, especially as graph size grows. The root cause is the vast discrete trajectory space combined with the high variance of REINFORCE-style gradient estimators, where the weighted sum over all possible clean graphs can be dominated by terms promoting low-reward predictions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained discrete graph DPM (DiGress with a graph transformer backbone) and an arbitrary black-box reward function r(G_0) that maps a single generated graph to a scalar, and produces a fine-tuned DPM whose sampling distribution is shifted toward high-reward graphs. The reward is queried only on the final denoised graph G_0 (not intermediate noisy states), and can be any non-differentiable signal such as AutoDock Vina docking scores, QED, synthetic accessibility, Tanimoto-based novelty, or binary validity checks. Graphs are represented with categorical one-hot node and edge attributes, and the method operates on graphs of varying sizes (16–200 nodes in experiments).

## Method

GDPO formulates the T-step reverse denoising process of a graph DPM as a Markov decision process where the state is the noisy graph G_t, the action is the next denoised graph G_{t-1}, and the reward r(G_0) is received only at the terminal step. The key technical contribution is the 'eager policy gradient,' which replaces the standard REINFORCE summand ∇_θ log p_θ(G_{t-1}|G_t) with ∇_θ log p_θ(G_0|G_t), thereby skipping the intermediate weighted sum over all possible clean graphs and directly promoting the probability of predicting the high-reward final graph from every noisy state. This biased estimator optimizes in equivalence classes (grouped by G_0) rather than the full trajectory space, dramatically reducing gradient variance at the cost of a small bias. In practice, GDPO samples K trajectories, selects a random subset of |T| timesteps per trajectory for gradient accumulation, normalizes rewards to [−5, 5], and fine-tunes only the attention layers of a pre-trained DiGress model with a small learning rate (1e-5) and gradient clipping.

## Results

On ZINC250k, GDPO achieves an average Hit Ratio of 14.01% across five target proteins (parp1: 9.81%, fa7: 3.45%, 5ht1b: 34.36%, braf: 9.04%, jak2: 13.41%), representing a 5.72% average improvement over the best SOTA baseline (MOOD). For DS(top 5%), GDPO records an average of -10.46 kcal/mol, a 1.48% improvement over baselines. On general graph generation, GDPO achieves an 81.97% average reduction in distribution distance (Deg, Clus, Orb) on Planar and 41.64% on SBM compared to DiGress, while also improving V.U.N from 70.02% to 73.83% (Planar) and 60.94% to 80.08% (SBM). On MOSES, GDPO achieves a 12.94% average Hit Ratio improvement on four of five proteins and a 5.54% DS(top 5%) improvement over MOOD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDPO decisively outperforms DDPO (the direct REINFORCE application to graph DPMs) across all datasets and metrics; on ZINC250k, DDPO achieves Hit Ratios of only 0.34–5.49% versus GDPO's 3.45–34.36%, and the toy experiment (Fig. 2) shows DDPO fails to converge entirely beyond 4 nodes while GDPO remains stable. Against DiGress (the base graph DPM without RL), GDPO improves Hit Ratio by 10–80× depending on the protein target, and reduces distribution distance by 81.97% (Planar) and 41.64% (SBM). Compared to MOOD (the strongest guidance-based graph DPM baseline), GDPO wins on 4 of 5 ZINC250k proteins for Hit Ratio (e.g., 34.36% vs 18.67% on 5ht1b) and on all 5 for DS(top 5%), and on MOSES achieves a 5.54% average DS improvement. Against older RL-based methods (GCPN, REINVENT, FREED), GDPO shows large margins, with FREED being the closest competitor (e.g., 16.77% vs 34.36% Hit Ratio on 5ht1b). DiGress-guidance (property-predictor-based guidance) is consistently outperformed, confirming that direct RL optimization bypasses the inaccuracy of learned predictors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL-based optimization of diffusion models (established for continuous-variable image DPMs by DDPO and DPOK) to the discrete-variable setting of graph DPMs. It builds directly on DiGress's categorical diffusion framework and the REINFORCE policy gradient, but identifies that the standard REINFORCE estimator fails empirically on graph DPMs due to the high variance introduced by discrete, discontinuous trajectories. The key departure is the 'eager policy gradient,' which replaces the one-step denoising gradient ∇log p(G_{t-1}|G_t) with the x₀-prediction gradient ∇log p(G_0|G_t), effectively optimizing over equivalence classes of trajectories rather than individual paths. This positions the work at the intersection of graph DPMs, RL-based alignment, and controllable generation, filling a gap where prior RL methods (GCPN, REINVENT, FREED) used simpler policy networks and prior DPM alignment methods (DDPO, DPOK) were limited to continuous variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GDPO provides the first effective method for aligning graph DPMs with arbitrary, potentially non-differentiable reward signals (e.g., docking scores from physical simulations), bypassing the need for property predictors that often fail on complex objectives. The practical impact is significant for drug discovery and materials design, where reward signals are inherently black-box. Key open questions include: (1) the theoretical gap between the biased eager policy gradient and the true policy gradient, and whether an unbiased estimator exists; (2) scalability to large graphs (500+ nodes); and (3) mitigating overoptimization/collapse, as demonstrated by the failure case with high novelty weights. The paper also notes that importance sampling (used in DDPO) does not further improve GDPO, suggesting the bias-variance tradeoff is not yet fully understood.
