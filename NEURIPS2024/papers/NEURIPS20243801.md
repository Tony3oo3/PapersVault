## Problem

While LLMs are empirically evaluated on planning tasks, there is no theoretical account of how the autoregressive next-token prediction mechanism gives rise to planning capability in Transformer architectures. Specifically, it remains unclear what graph-structural information (adjacency, reachability) a Transformer actually learns from path data, and whether it can generalize to reachability relationships not directly present in the training set.

## Input / Output

The model receives a token sequence beginning with a source node token followed by a target node token (format 's t'), and must autoregressively generate the remaining tokens to form a valid directed path from s to t, terminated by a newline token. Training sequences follow the format 's t s a b c t \n', where the path portion lists intermediate nodes. The underlying graph is a randomly generated DAG with n nodes and edge probability p=0.1, and the model must produce syntactically valid paths that correspond to actual directed paths in the graph.

## Method

The paper provides a two-part theoretical analysis of a simplified 1-layer, 1-head Transformer trained with cross-entropy loss on path data. Theorem 2 constructs a Transformer whose FFN weights encode the adjacency matrix and whose attention value weights encode the reachability matrix, proving expressiveness for path-finding. Theorem 3 then analyzes gradient descent dynamics, showing that the model learns the observed adjacency matrix in W^M (current-node information via FFN) and the observed reachability matrix in W^V (target-node information via attention to the second token), with the next-node prediction computed as softmax(W^M_{(current,k)} + W^V_{(target,k)}). The key finding is a fundamental limitation: the model only learns reachability pairs directly observed in training data and cannot deduce transitive reachability (reachability through concatenation of path segments), causing accuracy to drop sharply for source-target pairs requiring multi-hop reasoning. Empirical validation on synthetic DAGs (100–500 nodes) and the Blocksworld benchmark confirms that attention concentrates on the target node, W^{M'} aligns with the adjacency matrix, W^{V'} aligns with observed (but not full) reachability, and accuracy degrades for high-degree pairs.

## Results

On the Blocksworld benchmark (73-node state graph), all Transformer models (embedding sizes d=30, 60, 120) reach near-100% path-finding accuracy after training. On synthetic DAGs (100–500 nodes, d=120), accuracy is high for 1-layer/1-head models when the graph is small (~95%+ at 100 nodes) but degrades as node count increases; embedding size is the dominant hyperparameter, with even 6-layer/6-head models failing when d is too small relative to graph size. The critical finding is a significant accuracy drop for source-target pairs of degree ≥2 (requiring transitive reachability): these pairs show substantially lower accuracy than degree-0 and degree-1 pairs across all configurations, confirming the theoretical prediction that Transformers learn only observed (non-transitive) reachability. The linear approximation of the FFN output achieves cosine similarity of 0.926 (100 nodes) to 0.870 (400 nodes), validating the interpretability analysis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; it is a theoretical and empirical study of the standard GPT/Transformer architecture on a controlled path-finding task. The key internal comparison is between source-target pairs of different 'degree' (how many path-segment concatenations are needed): degree-0 and degree-1 pairs (where a single observed edge + observed reachability suffices) achieve high accuracy, while degree-2 and degree-3+ pairs (requiring transitive reasoning over unobserved reachability) show a consistent and significant accuracy drop across all model sizes (1-layer to 6-layer, 1-head to 6-head) and graph sizes (100 to 500 nodes). This gap persists even with more layers and heads, indicating a fundamental architectural limitation rather than a capacity issue. On Blocksworld, the W^{M′} matrix almost perfectly aligns with the true adjacency matrix, and the W^{V′} matrix captures observed reachability (first 24 columns noticeably darker), but no test pairs of degree ≥2 exist in that benchmark, so the transitive-reachability limitation is not directly stress-tested there.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard GPT/Transformer architecture (Radford et al., 2018) and positions itself at the intersection of three prior research threads: (1) empirical evaluations of LLM planning (CogEval, Blocksworld) that reported mixed results without mechanistic explanation; (2) theoretical expressiveness results showing Transformers can simulate algorithms in TC⁰; and (3) the 'physics of language models' line of work that uses synthetic data to reverse-engineer internal mechanisms. The key departure is providing the first joint theoretical analysis of both expressiveness (Theorem 2) and gradient-descent learning dynamics (Theorem 3) specifically for the path-finding/planning task, bridging the gap between 'Transformers can in principle do this' and 'here is exactly what gradient descent on cross-entropy loss will and will not learn.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic explanation for why LLMs fail on certain planning tasks: gradient descent on next-token prediction loss fundamentally prevents learning of transitive reachability, so the model cannot compose path segments it has seen individually. This reframes prior negative empirical results (e.g., GPT-3's 5% on Blocksworld) as an architectural limitation rather than a data or scale issue. Open questions include whether alternative training objectives (path accuracy), architectural modifications (explicit transitivity), or enriched training data can overcome this limitation, and whether the finding generalizes beyond simple DAGs to the more complex state spaces in real planning problems.
