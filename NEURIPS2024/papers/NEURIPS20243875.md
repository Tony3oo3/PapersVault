## Problem

As object detection models become increasingly lightweight, non-maximum suppression (NMS) has emerged as the dominant post-processing bottleneck, yet its intrinsic computational structure has never been formally characterized. Existing speedup methods rely on parallelization without reducing actual computational overhead, making them hardware-dependent, and the field lacks a unified benchmark for fair comparison of NMS algorithms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243875/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The methods take a set of n candidate bounding boxes with associated confidence scores and a fixed IOU threshold (typically 0.7) as input, and produce a binary retention sequence of length n indicating which boxes survive suppression. The input boxes are pre-extracted from well-trained detectors (YOLOv5, YOLOv8, Faster R-CNN) on MS COCO 2017 and Open Images V7, decoupling inference from post-processing so that NMS validation runs in minutes rather than requiring full model forward passes. NMS-Bench packages 273,100 images of pre-computed boxes with standardized latency and mAP evaluation.

## Method

The paper's central insight is that NMS induces a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each box is a node and an arc (v,u) exists iff box v has higher confidence and IOU > threshold with box u, making the NMS result computable via dynamic programming in topological order. Two structural properties of this graph drive the proposed algorithms: (1) it contains many weakly connected components (WCCs), enabling a quicksort-inspired divide-and-conquer (QSI-NMS) that recursively partitions by centroid distance, with its extended form (eQSI-NMS) achieving optimal O(n log n) complexity by recognizing the recursion tree as a Cartesian tree solvable via a monotone stack; (2) most WCCs are very small (over 75% have size < 10), enabling BOE-NMS to exploit geometric locality—proving that if a box's centroid lies outside a scaled version of the current box, their IOU is bounded below the threshold—so only nearby boxes need IOU computation, yielding exact results with no mAP loss.

## Results

On MS COCO 2017 with YOLOv8-N, QSI-NMS achieves 6.2× speedup over original NMS (146.8μs vs 906.9μs) with only 0.1% mAP decrease (37.1 vs 37.2), while eQSI-NMS reaches 10.7× speedup (85.0μs) with 0.3% mAP decrease (36.9). BOE-NMS provides 5.1× speedup (176.8μs) with zero mAP loss (37.2). On YOLOv5-N, the speedups are even more pronounced: eQSI-NMS achieves 29.6× speedup (339.0μs vs 10034.2μs) with 0.4% mAP decrease, and BOE-NMS achieves 14.0× speedup (719.6μs) with no mAP loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against original NMS [7], all three proposed methods achieve substantial speedups: eQSI-NMS is the fastest at 10.7× (YOLOv8-N) up to 29.6× (YOLOv5-N), QSI-NMS at 6.2×, and BOE-NMS at 5.1×, with mAP losses of 0.3%, 0.1%, and 0% respectively. Compared to Fast NMS [4], which parallelizes IOU computation but still requires O(n²) IOU calculations, eQSI-NMS is 4.3× faster and QSI-NMS is 2.2× faster on YOLOv8-N, while also matching or exceeding its mAP. Against Cluster-NMS [46], which iterates Fast NMS until convergence, eQSI-NMS is 8.9× faster and QSI-NMS is 4.6× faster on YOLOv8-N, with BOE-NMS at 4.8×. Versus PSRR-MaxpoolNMS [43], eQSI-NMS achieves lower latency across all tested models while PSRR-MaxpoolNMS suffers 1–2% mAP degradation on Faster R-CNN and YOLOv5; PSRR-MaxpoolNMS's speedup is highly dependent on parallelism degree and bounding box count. Even against the GPU-accelerated CUDA NMS in TorchVision [28], eQSI-NMS remains faster (85.0μs vs 343.4μs on YOLOv8-N), demonstrating that the methods reduce actual computational work rather than relying on hardware parallelism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by reframing NMS as a directed acyclic graph problem solvable via dynamic programming in topological sorting, a perspective absent from all prior NMS work. Prior efficiency-focused methods (Fast NMS, Cluster-NMS) rely on parallelization without reducing computational complexity, while anchor-based methods (MaxpoolNMS, ASAP-NMS, PSRR-MaxpoolNMS) exploit detector-specific structure and are limited to two-stage detectors. The divide-and-conquer (QSI-NMS) and geometric exclusion (BOE-NMS) strategies here are novel applications of the discovered graph properties—many small weakly connected components and sparse arc structure—rather than extensions of any single prior algorithm. The NMS-Bench benchmark also departs from prior practice where NMS methods were evaluated only through full model inference on heterogeneous hardware.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As model inference times shrink due to lightweighting, NMS is increasingly the dominant post-processing cost, making algorithmic (rather than parallel) speedups critical for edge deployment. The O(n log n) complexity of eQSI-NMS via the Cartesian tree connection is theoretically optimal, and BOE-NMS's zero-mAP-loss guarantee provides a safe drop-in replacement. NMS-Bench addresses a real gap in the field by enabling fair, rapid comparison of NMS methods without full model inference. Open questions include whether the graph-theoretic framework can be combined with accuracy-enhancing variants (e.g., Soft-NMS), whether further parallelization of the divide-and-conquer structure yields additional gains, and how the WCC size distribution changes across different detector architectures and datasets.
