## Problem

Zero-shot models like CLIP inherit label distribution biases from their web-scale pretraining data, causing systematic misclassification when downstream tasks have different class balances. Existing remedies—fine-tuning or standard label shift adaptation—require either labeled downstream data or access to the pretraining label distribution, both unavailable in true zero-shot deployment. The paper asks whether predictions can be corrected at inference time using only an estimate of the downstream label distribution.

## Input / Output

OTTER consumes a batch of n inference points (images or text), the pretrained model's per-class prediction scores for each point, and a label distribution specification ν̂ ∈ ℝ^K (a probability vector over K classes). It outputs a transport plan π ∈ ℝ^{n×K} from which each data point's predicted class is read as argmax_j π_{ij}. A key constraint is that OTTER requires the full batch to be available simultaneously (it enforces the marginal constraint Σ_i π_{ij} = n·ν̂_j), making it unsuitable for sequential online prediction without the R-OTTER reweighting variant.

## Method

OTTER formulates zero-shot classification as a discrete optimal transport problem: the cost matrix is C_{ij} = −log P_θ(Y=j|X=x_i) (negative log prediction score), the source marginal is uniform over the n data points, and the target marginal is the label distribution specification ν̂. Solving the resulting linear program yields a transport plan that minimizes total classification cost while exactly matching the specified label distribution. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.2) shows that under label shift (P_s(X|Y)=P_t(X|Y)) and well-calibrated source predictions, OT with the true target distribution recovers the Bayes-optimal classifier without ever accessing P_s(Y), because the source-to-target posterior difference decomposes into row and column additive perturbations that leave the OT solution invariant. Perturbation sensitivity is bounded via Hoffman constants from linear programming, decomposing error into label distribution estimation error, cost matrix calibration error, and solution suboptimality.

## Results

On 17 image classification datasets using CLIP ViT-B/16, OTTER achieves an average 4.8% accuracy improvement over zero-shot baselines (e.g., 91.7% on CIFAR-10 vs. 88.3%, 88.7% on Caltech-101 vs. 79.8%, 62.9% on ImageNet vs. 60.2%). On 4 text classification datasets using BERT, OTTER yields a 15.5% average improvement (e.g., 91.7% on Amazon Reviews vs. 74.0%, 81.4% on CivilComments vs. 48.4%). OTTER outperforms the Prior Matching baseline in 17 out of 21 datasets, with particularly large margins on class-imbalanced datasets where Prior Matching collapses (e.g., 87.0% vs. 9.5% on Caltech-256, 54.1% vs. 6.7% on SUN397).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OTTER is compared against two baselines: Prior Matching (PM) [Liusie et al., 2023], which optimizes score-weighting parameters to match a label distribution specification, and CLIPPR [Kahana et al., 2022], which trains adapter layers using label distribution priors. Against Prior Matching, OTTER wins on 17 of 21 datasets; the critical failure mode of PM is its extreme sensitivity to hyperparameters (temperature and learning rate), causing it to collapse on class-imbalanced datasets—e.g., 9.5% on Caltech-256, 6.7% on SUN397, and 16.7% on ImageNet-R—while OTTER achieves 87.0%, 54.1%, and 72.4% respectively without any tuning. OTTER's only losses to PM are marginal (e.g., STL-10: 98.6 vs. 98.4; EuroSAT: 42.0 vs. 41.6; CivilComments: 81.4 vs. 57.2 where PM actually wins). Against CLIPPR, OTTER consistently outperforms across all CLIP architectures (RN50 through ViT-L/14) and text embedding models, while requiring no adapter training or additional loss functions—CLIPPR degrades severely on fine-grained datasets (e.g., 20.5% on CUB vs. OTTER's 50.4% with ViT-B/16). OTTER's key practical advantage is being entirely tuning-free at inference time, whereas both baselines require either hyperparameter grid search (PM) or additional training (CLIPPR).

## Relation to Prior Work

OTTER repurposes the optimal transport framework—previously applied to pseudo-labeling (Tai et al.), imbalanced training (Guo et al.), and partial-label learning (Wang et al.)—as a purely inference-time post-processing step for zero-shot classifiers. It departs from standard label shift adaptation methods (BBSE, importance sampling) that require access to the source distribution, and from Prior Matching [37] and CLIPPR [31] that require hyperparameter tuning or adapter training. The R-OTTER variant explicitly adopts the logit-adjustment reweighting formulation from Zhu et al. [71], connecting OT-based rebalancing to the calibration literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OTTER offers a tuning-free, training-free correction for a pervasive failure mode of zero-shot models (CLIP, BERT), with theoretical guarantees of Bayes-optimal recovery under label shift and explicit perturbation bounds. Its practical impact is broad: it improves zero-shot image and text classification by 4.8% and 15.9% on average, and extends naturally to LLM selection bias mitigation. Key limitations include the batched-prediction requirement (partially addressed by R-OTTER), dependence on a label distribution specification that is unavailable in truly zero-shot settings, and performance degradation under noisy specifications—leaving open the question of how to obtain reliable distribution estimates without any labeled target data.
