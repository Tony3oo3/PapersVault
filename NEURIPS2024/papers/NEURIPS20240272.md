## Problem

Existing Fourier-based LTSF methods map frequency coefficients in the frequency domain without involving basis functions, leading to two fundamental issues: inconsistent starting cycles (real and imaginary parts lack explicit meaning without their associated basis functions) and inconsistent series length (the physical frequency in Hz depends on the series length, making frequency components ambiguous across different look-back windows). Consequently, these methods cannot capture time-frequency relationships, which are essential for accurate long-term forecasting.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate time series of length T (look-back window, assumed even, set to 336 in experiments) and produces a forecast of L future steps (96, 192, 336, or 720, also assumed even). The input is first normalized, then transformed via DFT into a frequency spectrum; the real and imaginary parts are each multiplied by their corresponding orthogonal basis functions (cosine and sine) to produce a time-frequency feature matrix G of shape (T/2+1) × T, which is then mapped to the L-step forecast. This intermediate representation preserves both temporal structure and explicit frequency-level information simultaneously.

## Method

FBM reinterprets the DFT through a basis functions lens: the real and imaginary parts of the frequency spectrum are the coefficients of T/2+1 orthogonal cosine and T/2−1 orthogonal sine basis functions, respectively. The method multiplies H_R by the cosine basis matrix C and H_I by the sine basis matrix S (Eq. 5), then sums over the frequency dimension to form G, which recovers the original time series while encoding explicit per-frequency-level temporal patterns. This time-frequency feature G is then mapped to the forecast via one of three lightweight decoders: a single linear layer (FBM-L), a three-layer MLP (FBM-NL), or a PatchTST-style transformer with fewer patches applied after the basis expansion (FBM-NP). The key distinction from prior Fourier-based methods is that the mapping operates in a joint time-frequency space rather than purely in frequency space, and the basis functions resolve both the phase-shift ambiguity and the series-length-dependent frequency interpretation.

## Results

On the eight LTSF datasets (ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2, Electricity, Traffic, Weather, Exchange), FBM-L reduces the average MSE and MAE from 0.333/0.349 (NLinear) to 0.323/0.339, with the largest gains on ETTh1 and ETTh2 due to richer frequency content and higher noise. FBM-NL and FBM-NP outperform their respective architecture counterparts (TimeMixer and PatchTST) on most datasets and horizons. On the four PEMS short-term forecasting datasets (L=12), FBM-NL achieves the best MSE of 0.053 on PEMS07 and 0.060 on PEMS03/PEMS08, while FBM-NP matches or nearly matches these results with a transformer-based architecture using fewer patches than PatchTST.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FBM-L (a single linear layer) outperforms NLinear on all eight LTSF datasets and all four forecast horizons, reducing average MSE from 0.333 to 0.323 and MAE from 0.349 to 0.339, with the most notable gains on ETTh1 and ETTh2. FBM-NL (three-layer MLP) consistently beats TimeMixer, and FBM-NP (patched transformer) beats PatchTST on most datasets, despite using fewer patches than PatchTST. Against Fourier-based baselines (N-BEATS, FreTS, CrossGNN, FITS), all three FBM variants show significant improvements, which the authors attribute to the inconsistent starting cycles and series length issues in those methods. On the PEMS STSF benchmark, FBM-NL achieves the best MSE on PEMS07 (0.053) and PEMS03/PEMS08 (0.060), while FBM-NP achieves the best MAE on PEMS04 (0.170); both substantially outperform FiLM (MSE 0.101–0.118) and TimesNet (MSE 0.079–0.094). FBM-L matches NLinear on PEMS07 (both 0.073 MSE) but wins on the other three PEMS subsets, confirming that even the simplest variant benefits from Fourier basis expansion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the discrete Fourier transform through the lens of orthogonal cosine and sine basis functions, drawing a direct analogy to the N-BEATS seasonal block design. It departs from existing Fourier-based LTSF methods (FEDformer, FreTS, FITS, CrossGNN, FiLM) that feed raw real/imaginary frequency coefficients into mapping networks, arguing these methods suffer from 'inconsistent starting cycles' and 'inconsistent series length' issues because the coefficients lack explicit temporal grounding. The FBM method embeds the DFT with basis functions to produce time-frequency features, then applies a lightweight mapping network (linear, MLP, or PatchTST-style) to forecast, positioning itself as a plug-and-play enhancement orthogonal to the choice of downstream architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of two concrete failure modes in frequency-domain mapping (phase ambiguity without basis functions, and Hz ambiguity when series length changes) provides a useful diagnostic framework for evaluating future Fourier-based time series methods. The practical finding that a single linear layer operating on time-frequency features can match or exceed complex Transformer and MLP architectures suggests that feature engineering in the frequency domain may be more valuable than architectural depth for LTSF. Limitations include the assumption of even-length series, reduced effectiveness on monotonic (non-periodic) trends, and the fact that improvements over the strongest baselines (e.g., iTransformer, TimeMixer) are often modest. Open questions include whether the approach extends to CNN/RNN backbones, anomaly detection, and irregularly sampled series.
