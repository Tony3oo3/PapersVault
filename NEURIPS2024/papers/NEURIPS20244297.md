## Problem

The classic prophet inequality assumes each reward distribution is an inanimate object that fully discloses its realized value to the searcher. In many real-world applications (hiring, venture capital, real estate), each distribution is governed by a strategic player who can selectively reveal information to maximize their probability of being selected. This paper asks how robust the well-known 1/2-approximation threshold stopping policies are when all players optimally signal their rewards, and whether the searcher can still guarantee strong competitive ratios under this strategic manipulation.

## Input / Output

The searcher takes as input a finite sequence of N known reward distributions H_{1:N} and, after players commit to their signaling schemes, observes a sequence of information signals (not the realized rewards directly). The searcher pre-commits to a threshold T computed as a function of H_{1:N} and then accepts the first player whose posterior mean reward (given the signal) meets or exceeds T. The output is the expected accepted reward, evaluated against the prophet value OPT = E[max_i X_i], with the guarantee expressed as a competitive ratio α such that E[X^(q)] ≥ α · OPT in both the strategic and non-strategic settings simultaneously.

## Method

The key technical insight is a clean characterization of each player's optimal information strategy under a threshold policy: when T > λ_i, player i's optimal signaling scheme pools all rewards above a cutoff t_i (where E[X_i | X_i ≥ t_i] = T) into one signal and all lower rewards into another, equivalently represented as a binary-support mean-preserving contraction G_i of H_i. This reduces the strategic prophet inequality to a related problem with binary reward supports. For arbitrary distributions, the Kleinberg-Weinberg threshold T_KW = E[max X_i]/2 achieves (1−1/e)/2-robustness, shown to be tight among all thresholds in the known 1/2-approximation spectrum. For IID distributions, the threshold T* (satisfying T* = Σ E[(X_i − T*)⁺]) achieves full 1/2-robustness, which is tight. For log-concave heterogeneous distributions satisfying regularity conditions (bounded density at 1 and bounded derivative), any threshold between 2·T_KW and T_SC also achieves 1/2-robustness, proven via convexity of the product distribution H̄(x) = ∏ H_i(x).

## Results

For arbitrary reward distributions, the Kleinberg-Weinberg threshold (T_KW = E[max X_i]/2) achieves (1−1/e)/2 ≈ 0.316-strategic robustness, and this ratio is tight among all thresholds in the 1/2-approximation spectrum (Theorem 4.1, Proposition 4.2). For IID distributions, the threshold T* (satisfying T* = Σ E[(X_i − T*)⁺]) achieves full 1/2-robustness, which is optimal among all threshold policies (Theorem 5.1, Proposition 5.2). For log-concave heterogeneous distributions satisfying regularity conditions (f(1) ≥ α, f′(1) ≥ −β, N ≥ 1 + β/α²), any threshold in [2·T_KW, T_SC] also achieves 1/2-robustness (Theorem 5.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between the classic (non-strategic) prophet inequality guarantee of 1/2 and the degraded guarantee under strategic reward signaling. For arbitrary distributions, the T_KW threshold retains 1/2-approximation in the non-strategic world but degrades to (1−1/e)/2 ≈ 0.316 in the strategic world, and Proposition 4.2 shows no threshold in the known 1/2-approximation spectrum can do better. For IID distributions, the T* threshold maintains the full 1/2 guarantee in both worlds, and Proposition 5.2 establishes that no threshold policy (even outside the spectrum) can exceed 1/2 in the strategic setting. For log-concave distributions, the Samuel-Cahn threshold T_SC and the T_KW threshold both yield 1/2-robustness under the stated regularity conditions. The paper also notes (Corollary 5.3) that if one abandons the 1/2 non-strategic requirement, a (1−1/e) ≈ 0.632 approximation is achievable in the strategic setting for IID distributions, but this does not satisfy the robustness criterion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of prophet inequalities (optimal stopping) and Bayesian persuasion (information design). It builds directly on the classic prophet inequality framework of Krengel-Sucheston and Samuel-Cahn, and the modern threshold spectrum of Kleinberg-Weinberg, while importing the signaling/experiment framework of Kamenica-Gentzkow to model how each reward distribution is governed by a strategic player. The key departure from prior work is the decentralized, multi-player strategic setting: unlike Hahn et al. [30], who study a single centralized information designer, here each player independently chooses a signaling scheme to maximize their own selection probability, creating a constant-sum competition. The paper also connects to competitive information design and strategic machine learning, but applies these ideas to the optimal stopping problem rather than classification or regret minimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that well-studied threshold policies for prophet inequalities are 'intrinsically robust' to strategic manipulation—provides a clean worst-case guarantee ((1-1/e)/2 ≈ 0.316 for arbitrary distributions, 1/2 for IID) that practitioners in sequential decision-making (hiring, VC investment, real estate) can rely on even when information providers are strategic. The tightness results rule out improvement within the known threshold spectrum, leaving open whether thresholds outside that spectrum or dynamic (player-dependent) policies can close the gap to full 1/2-robustness for arbitrary distributions. The clean characterization of optimal signaling as a mean-preserving contraction with a single pooling cutoff is a reusable analytical tool for future work on strategic information in sequential settings.
