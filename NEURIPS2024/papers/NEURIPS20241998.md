## Problem

Transformers trained on k-th order Markov processes (k > 1) without tokenization empirically fail to learn beyond a unigram model, incurring cross-entropy loss far from optimal. Despite tokenization being ubiquitous in practice, there is no theoretical framework explaining why it enables transformers to model Markovian data near-optimally, nor a principled way to compare tokenizers on end-to-end cross-entropy loss.

## Input / Output

The framework takes raw character sequences drawn from a k-th order Markov source (or real text corpora like Wikitext-103, GLUE, Yelp, Rotten Tomatoes) and produces a token sequence via a tokenizer's encoding function, followed by a likelihood model (unigram or transformer) that outputs a next-token probability distribution. The key evaluation metric is the end-to-end cross-entropy loss L(Q ∘ enc(·)), where the expectation is over a fresh test sequence of length m. The tokenizer is defined as a tuple (Dict, enc, dec) with a consistency constraint dec(enc(·)) = identity, and the unigram model family Q_{1-gram} assigns probability Q_#(j)·∏Q_tok(t_i) to a token sequence of length j.

## Method

The paper introduces a statistical formulation for tokenization and proves that with a dictionary of size d, there exists a tokenizer such that the best unigram model over tokens achieves cross-entropy loss within a factor 1/(1−ε) of the optimal model, where ε = log(1/δ)/(0.99·log(d)³). The key mechanism is a 'heavy-hitting dictionary' property: tokenizers that assign all high-probability substrings as tokens ensure the greedy encoder emits tokens with P(t) ≤ 1/d^β, making H(Q_MLE, P) ≥ β·log(d) and invoking a generic reduction (Theorem 3.4) that bounds the unigram cross-entropy loss. The LZW tokenizer is proven to produce such dictionaries with d ≈ 1/δ² tokens (vs. exponential for a naive length-r chunking tokenizer), and a sequential BPE variant achieves the same bound up to a factor of 2. The core insight is that tokenizers which learn frequent co-occurring patterns as single tokens make the induced token distribution approximately i.i.d., rendering a unigram model over tokens near-optimal for Markovian sources.

## Results

The paper's main empirical result (Table 1) shows that unigram models trained on pretrained subword tokenizers (BERT/Wordpiece, GPT-neox/BPE, Phi-2/BPE, etc.) achieve cross-entropy losses of 1.45–1.84 across four datasets (Rotten Tomatoes, Wikitext-103, Yelp, GLUE), consistently outperforming the character-level ByT5 tokenizer with a bigram model, which achieves 2.38–2.46. Theoretically, Theorem 3.1 proves that with a dictionary of size d, the best unigram model under LZW or sequential BPE tokenization achieves cross-entropy loss within a factor of 1/(1−ε) of the optimal, where ε = log(1/δ)/(0.99·log(d)³). Empirically, transformers trained on order-2 Markov data without tokenization fail to improve beyond the unigram loss floor, while with BPE tokenization (dictionary size 10) they reach the optimal cross-entropy bound within a small number of iterations.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| BERT (Wordpiece) | 1.58 | 1.55 | 1.60 | 1.50 |
| Tinyllama (BPE) | 1.75 | 1.84 | 1.82 | 1.70 |
| GPT-neox (BPE) | 1.57 | 1.64 | 1.66 | 1.48 |
| Mistral (BPE) | 1.69 | 1.80 | 1.75 | 1.66 |
| Phi-2 (BPE) | 1.54 | 1.62 | 1.64 | 1.45 |
| Character / ByT5 (bigram) | 2.40 | 2.45 | 2.46 | 2.38 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares unigram models under five pretrained subword tokenizers against a character-level baseline (ByT5) with a more powerful bigram model. The character-level ByT5 tokenizer is consistently and substantially outperformed: on GLUE, the best subword tokenizer (Phi-2) achieves 1.45 versus 2.38 for the character-level bigram, a gap of nearly 1.0 nats. Among subword tokenizers, BERT (Wordpiece) and GPT-neox (BPE) perform best on Wikitext-103 (1.55 and 1.64 respectively), while Phi-2 (BPE) achieves the lowest loss on Rotten Tomatoes (1.54) and GLUE (1.45). Tinyllama and Mistral (both BPE) show slightly higher losses, suggesting that tokenizer design and training data matter. The key takeaway is that even the simplest unigram likelihood model, when paired with a data-learned subword tokenizer, matches or exceeds the performance of a more complex bigram model on raw characters, providing empirical support for the paper's theoretical claim that tokenization enables unigram models to approach optimal cross-entropy on Markovian sources.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work studying transformers on Markov data (Makkuva et al., 2024; Edelman et al., 2024) by introducing tokenization as the critical missing ingredient that explains why transformers succeed in practice despite failing on raw character sequences. It departs from the formal tokenization analysis of Zouhar et al. (2023a,b), which focused on compression properties of BPE, by studying end-to-end cross-entropy loss and the generalization of tokenizers to unseen sequences. The theoretical framework builds on the digital search tree analysis of LZW parsing (Jacquet et al., 2001; Drmota et al., 2021) and the REPAIR/BPE compression literature (Larsson and Moffat, 2000; Navarro and Russo, 2008) to establish the first near-optimality guarantees for practical tokenizers under Markov sources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical justification for why subword tokenization is essential in practice, showing that even a simple unigram model over tokens can achieve near-optimal cross-entropy on Markov sources when the tokenizer is well-designed. Key limitations include the strong Assumption 3.2 (all transitions bounded away from zero), the restriction to Markov sources, and the gap between the analyzed sequential BPE variant and standard BPE. Open questions include whether the factor-of-2 gap for BPE can be closed, whether results extend to arbitrary Markov chains without the connectivity assumption, and how the dictionary-encoder interaction generalizes to more complex data distributions beyond Markov processes.
