## Problem

Existing differentiable surrogate losses for the predict-then-optimize framework (SPO+, Fenchel-Young loss, DBB) tacitly rely on the true cost function f* belonging to the hypothesis class or on centrally symmetric noise, and fail to approximate the true decision loss under misspecification—precisely the regime where decision-aware learning offers the most benefit over decision-blind approaches. No differentiable surrogate had previously been shown to yield a best-in-class policy asymptotically in misspecified settings, leaving a gap between the practical need for robust decision-aware learning and the theoretical guarantees available.

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. training pairs (X_i, Y_i) where X_i is a context vector and Y_i is a realized cost vector, and produces a learned cost function f(X) from a user-specified hypothesis class F. The learned f is plugged into the downstream problem min_{z∈Z} f(X)^T z to obtain a decision z, where Z can be any known feasible region (convex, polyhedral, or combinatorial). The only requirement on the optimization layer is a black-box oracle that solves the plug-in problem for any given cost vector, making the approach compatible with custom solvers for vehicle routing, portfolio allocation, or other structured problems without requiring differentiability of the solver itself.

## Method

The key insight is that by Danskin's theorem, the decision loss ℓ(t,y) = y^T π̂(t) equals the directional derivative of the concave plug-in objective V(t) = min_{z∈Z} t^T z in direction y at t. The paper approximates this derivative via zeroth-order finite differences—backward differencing (PGB) and central differencing (PGC)—yielding surrogate losses that are Lipschitz continuous, bounded, and expressible as the difference of concave functions. Unlike the original decision loss whose gradient is zero or undefined almost everywhere, the PG losses have 'informative' gradients: the gradient at a single sample is an unbiased estimate of the gradient of the expected loss, enabling direct application of first-order methods like Adam out-of-the-box. The central theoretical contribution is proving that the uniform approximation error between the PG surrogate and the true decision loss vanishes at rate Õ_p(√(ℜ^n) + n^{-1/2}) for general Z (or Õ_p(n^{-1/2}√(ν log|Z_∠|)) for polyhedral Z), implying the empirical minimizer achieves best-in-class performance asymptotically even when f* ∉ F.

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical result (Theorem 3.8) shows that the empirical minimizer of the PGB loss achieves excess regret of Õ_p(√(ℜⁿ(𝓕))) + O_p(n^{-1/2}) for general feasible regions 𝓩, reducing to Õ_p((dp/n)^{1/4}) for linear hypothesis classes, and Õ_p(n^{-1/2}√(ν log|𝓩_∠|)) for polyhedral 𝓩 — all vanishing as n→∞ even under misspecification (f*∉𝓕). Empirically, in the most misspecified setting (α=1, m=0), PGB and PGC are the only methods whose normalized excess regret converges to zero with increasing n, while SPO+, FYL, DBB, and the decision-blind ETO baseline all plateau at non-zero regret. In the portfolio optimization experiment on Fama French 12 Industry Portfolios, both PGB and PGC are described as 'notably stronger' than all baselines despite the low signal-to-noise ratio. In the harder planted shortest-path experiment, PG losses offer a 'significant benefit' over all compared surrogates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SPO+ [Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022], PG losses substantially outperform under misspecification: in the m=0, α=1 setting, SPO+ regret increases rapidly as misspecification grows, while PGB/PGC regret increases much more slowly and vanishes asymptotically. SPO+ also degrades significantly as noise becomes asymmetric (α→1), whereas PG losses remain stable. Against FYL [Berthet et al., 2020], PG losses achieve comparable performance in well-specified and random-arc shortest-path settings but clearly outperform under misspecification. Against DBB [Pogančić et al., 2019], PG losses dominate; the paper attributes DBB's weaker performance to its use of forward differencing with large h, which introduces optimistic bias by overestimating the derivative of the concave plug-in objective. Against the LTR losses [Mandi et al., 2021], PG losses again outperform in misspecified settings. Against the decision-blind ETO baseline (minimize MSE then plug-in), PG losses match or slightly underperform in well-specified settings (m=−4) but substantially outperform as misspecification increases, confirming that decision-aware learning offers its greatest benefit precisely when the hypothesis class cannot represent f*.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the predict-then-optimize framework initiated by Elmachtoub and Grigas (SPO+) by constructing surrogate losses through zeroth-order gradient approximations (backward and central finite differences) of the directional derivative of the plug-in objective, leveraging Danskin's theorem. It departs from prior surrogates—SPO+ (duality-based), FYL (randomized smoothing + conjugate duality), and DBB (linearization)—by accepting a non-convex, difference-of-concave structure in exchange for the first vanishing-excess-regret guarantee in misspecified settings. The uniform error bounds for polyhedral feasible regions build directly on VC-dimension results from Hu, Kallus, and Mao, while the general-case bound uses multivariate Rademacher complexity in the style of Liu and Grigas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that misspecified settings—precisely where decision-aware learning offers the greatest advantage over decision-blind methods—are the regime in which all prior differentiable surrogates lose their theoretical justification and empirical performance degrades sharply. The PG losses provide the first principled, gradient-optimizable surrogate with asymptotic best-in-class guarantees under misspecification, opening a path to decision-aware learning with low-complexity hypothesis classes (linear, shallow) where prior methods fail. Open questions include identifying the optimal zeroth-order gradient scheme, understanding the bias-computational-complexity tradeoff governed by the step size h, and extending guarantees beyond the Lipschitz log-density assumption. The non-convexity (DC structure) of the loss is a practical limitation, though the authors argue it is unavoidable for any method seeking best-in-class recovery and is empirically manageable with standard first-order methods.
