## Problem

Fine-tuning aligned LLMs on benign downstream datasets (e.g., GSM8K, ChatDoctor) can significantly degrade safety alignment, causing models to respond helpfully to harmful queries. While prior work documented this degradation, the role of prompt templates in mediating the safety loss was unexplored, and no practical strategy existed to preserve alignment while still improving downstream task performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243765/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-aligned chat model and fine-tunes it on task-specific data (GSM8K math problems, ChatDoctor medical conversations, or OpenOrca reasoning traces) using a chosen prompt template, then enforces a potentially different template at inference. Safety is quantified as Attack Success Rate (ASR) — the fraction of harmful queries (from AdvBench, DirectHarm4, or GSM-Danger) receiving a score of 5 on a GPT-4 Likert scale — while helpfulness is measured by task-specific metrics such as exact match on GSM8K, BERTScore on ChatDoctor, or exact match on ARC-Easy/Challenge.

## Method

The core contribution is the Pure Tuning, Safe Testing (PTST) strategy: fine-tune the model without a safety prompt (e.g., using chat:vanilla or chat:alpaca templates) but prepend a safety system prompt at test time (e.g., chat:llama). This intentional distribution shift prevents the model from associating the safety prompt with the fine-tuning distribution, so harmful behaviors inadvertently learned during fine-tuning are less likely to be triggered when the safety prompt is present at inference. The paper demonstrates that PTST outperforms both using the same template throughout and early stopping, and remains effective even when safety examples are mixed into training data — particularly for out-of-distribution harmful queries (GSM-Danger) that stylistically differ from the safety examples. The strategy is validated across Llama 2-Chat, Mistral 7B Instruct, and GPT-3.5 Turbo, and is shown to generalize to alternative safety prompts (Self-Reminder, In-context Defense, MPT system prompt).

## Results

On Llama 2-Chat fine-tuned on GSM8K, using the same prompt template for training and testing (e.g., chat:vanilla for both) increases the Attack Success Rate (ASR) on DirectHarm4 from 2.75% (no fine-tuning) to 11.00%, while the PTST strategy (fine-tune with chat:vanilla, test with chat:llama safety prompt) reduces ASR to 1.08% while retaining 30.00% GSM8K helpfulness (vs. 33.39% for same-template). Surprisingly, using the Llama safety prompt (chat:llama) for both training and testing yields the worst safety, with ASR of 18.08% on DirectHarm4. On GPT-3.5 Turbo, PTST reduces DirectHarm4 ASR from 22.75% (same template) to 4.50%, and on GSM-Danger (OOD harmful queries), PTST achieves 5% ASR versus 32–59% for same-template fine-tuning even with safety data mixed in.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PTST against two lightweight safety-prompt defenses: Self-Reminder (SR, Xie et al. 2023) and In-context Defense (ICD, Wei et al. 2023). When SR or ICD is used as both the training and test template, safety degrades severely: SR:SR yields 21.50% ASR on DirectHarm4 and ICD:ICD yields 27.58%, both far worse than the no-safety-prompt baseline (2.75%). In contrast, applying PTST with these defenses (fine-tune with chat:vanilla, test with SR or ICD) reduces ASR to 0.17% and 0.42% respectively, while maintaining helpfulness of 31.26% and 28.86% on GSM8K. The paper also shows that using two *different* safety prompts (e.g., fine-tune with chat:llama, test with chat:mpt or chat:llama-short) still leads to noticeable safety degradation, confirming that the key factor is omitting the safety prompt during fine-tuning rather than simply switching prompts. Overall, PTST consistently outperforms same-template fine-tuning across all three models (Llama 2-Chat, Mistral 7B Instruct, GPT-3.5 Turbo) and all three fine-tuning datasets (GSM8K, ChatDoctor, OpenOrca).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Qi et al. (2024), who first demonstrated that benign fine-tuning degrades safety alignment, and on the catastrophic forgetting literature that provides a mechanistic explanation for why utility-oriented fine-tuning can erode safety behaviors. It also engages with existing lightweight defenses (Self-Reminder, In-context Defense) and the Llama 2 paper's practice of using safety system prompts at inference time. The key departure is reframing the distribution shift between training and test inputs—typically treated as harmful to generalization—as a deliberate safety mechanism, yielding the PTST strategy where the safety prompt is omitted during fine-tuning but restored at inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PTST is a zero-cost, implementation-trivial strategy that any model creator can adopt immediately, and it is validated across three model families (Llama 2, Mistral, GPT-3.5) and multiple fine-tuning tasks (math, medical, general reasoning). However, the underlying mechanism remains unexplained—the paper acknowledges that PTST's success suggests an uncharacterized compositional generalization ability in LLMs—and the strategy's robustness to adversarial attacks (e.g., GCG) is only partially tested. Open questions include whether alignment training itself can be made more robust to template changes, and whether the findings extend to larger models or multi-turn settings.
