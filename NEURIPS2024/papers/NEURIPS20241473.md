## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks primarily test factual knowledge or simple image understanding, leaving a critical gap in evaluating MLLMs' higher-order perceptual capabilities—specifically their ability to decode metaphors, symbolism, and implicit emotional meanings embedded in complex visual content. This paper addresses the absence of a dedicated benchmark for image implication understanding, a skill that requires multi-hop reasoning and theory of mind to bridge surface-level visual content with the deeper human emotions and cultural narratives artists intend to convey.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241473/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a single RGB image (illustration, meme, poster, multi-panel or single-panel comic, logo, or painting) with a multiple-choice question of ~17 words and six answer options of ~14 words, where only one option correctly captures the image's implied meaning. The model outputs a single letter (A–F), and accuracy is computed via regex-based answer extraction from the free-text response. Optionally, the prompt can be augmented with the image's emotional polarity (positive/neutral/negative), domain (life/art/society/psychology/environment/others), or rhetorical device (metaphor, symbolism, personification, etc.), allowing ablation of how much contextual scaffolding the model needs.

## Method

II-Bench is constructed through a rigorous pipeline: 20,150 raw images are collected from illustration websites, then filtered through three stages (pixel-based deduplication, OCR-based text-to-image ratio thresholding, and exhaustive human visual inspection for implication presence), reducing the pool to under 2,000 images. Fifty undergraduate annotators then label each image with domain, difficulty, emotion, and rhetoric tags, write a human interpretation of the implication, and author 1–3 fine-grained multiple-choice questions with five carefully crafted distractors; quality assurance requires at least 4 of 5 independent reviewers to concur on each question's accuracy and difficulty. The final benchmark contains 1,222 images and 1,434 questions split into 1,399 test and 35 dev/validation items. Evaluation benchmarks 20 open- and closed-source MLLMs under eight prompt settings (zero-shot, 1/2/3-shot, CoT, Domain, Emotion, Rhetoric), revealing a substantial human–model gap (90.3% vs. 74.8% best), a consistent performance drop on abstract domains (Art, Psychology), and a notable accuracy boost when emotional polarity hints are provided—evidence that current MLLMs lack intrinsic sentiment understanding of images.

## Results

On II-Bench (1,399 test questions), the best closed-source model Qwen-VL-MAX achieves 74.8% overall accuracy, while the best open-source model LLaVA-1.6-34B scores 73.8%. Human participants achieve a mean accuracy of 90.3% (best: 98.2%), revealing a 15–23 percentage-point gap between state-of-the-art MLLMs and humans. Performance drops most sharply in the Art (best: 74.1%) and Psychology (best: 73.0%) domains, and models consistently score lower on negative-emotion images than positive ones, suggesting a positive-emotion bias in training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, LLaVA-1.6-34B (73.8%) leads, narrowly ahead of CogVLM2-Llama3-Chat (70.3%) and MiniCPM-Llama3-2.5 (69.4%), while the weakest performer InstructBLIP-T5-XL (47.3%) trails by over 26 points, largely due to its weaker multi-hop reasoning backbone (FLAN-T5-XL). Among closed-source models, Qwen-VL-MAX (74.8%) edges out Gemini-1.5 Pro (73.9%) and GPT-4o (72.6%), with GPT-4V (65.9%) lagging behind several top open-source models. The gap between the best open-source (LLaVA-1.6-34B, 73.8%) and best closed-source (Qwen-VL-MAX, 74.8%) model is only ~1%, indicating that open-source MLLMs have largely closed the gap on implication understanding. All models struggle most on Art and Psychology domains (e.g., GPT-4V drops to 59.9% on Psychology vs. 76.5% on Environment), and adding emotion-polarity hints to prompts consistently improves scores (e.g., Qwen-VL-MAX: 74.8% → 75.5%), confirming that models lack intrinsic emotional comprehension of images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out new ground in the MLLM benchmarking landscape by introducing a task—image implication understanding—that prior benchmarks (MMBench, SEED, MMMU, CMMMU, MMStar) explicitly do not target. While those benchmarks evaluate factual knowledge, common-sense reasoning, or simple visual perception, II-Bench focuses on higher-order perceptual capabilities: decoding metaphors, symbolism, and emotional narratives embedded in abstract images such as comics, posters, and paintings. The paper draws conceptually on cognitive science notions of theory of mind and higher-order perception, but repurposes them as a formalized multiple-choice evaluation protocol for MLLMs. It does not build on a specific prior method; rather, it positions itself as the first systematic benchmark for this capability gap, evaluating 20 existing MLLMs (BLIP-2, LLaVA, Qwen-VL, GPT-4V, etc.) as baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~15-point gap between the best MLLM (74.8%) and human average (90.3%) provides a concrete, reproducible measure of how far current models are from human-level implicit meaning comprehension, a capability the authors argue is essential for AGI. The finding that providing emotion polarity hints in prompts consistently improves scores reveals a specific, actionable deficiency: models lack intrinsic emotional grounding for images, likely due to training-data bias toward positive content. Open questions include whether the multiple-choice format adequately captures the open-ended nature of implication understanding, whether the subjective annotation process introduces irreducible variance, and whether scaling model size or incorporating richer cultural context can close the gap. The benchmark is publicly available and likely to become a standard probe for higher-order multimodal reasoning.
