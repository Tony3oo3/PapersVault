## Problem

Active preference learning for ordering items typically ignores shared structure between items (limiting sample efficiency and out-of-sample generalization) or treats comparison noise as uniform across pairs, despite noise being informative of item similarity. No prior work provides a theoretical bound on ordering error as a function of which specific comparisons have been collected, nor an active sampling criterion jointly justified by both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty for the full-ordering task.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pool of items with d-dimensional context attributes (e.g., DenseNet features for X-rays, ResNet34 embeddings for images, Sentence-BERT embeddings for text) and an annotation budget T of adaptively chosen pairwise comparisons drawn from a Bradley-Terry logistic model. It outputs a binary comparison function h(i,j) that induces a total ordering over all items, including unseen ones, by evaluating the learned scoring function f_θ(x_i, x_j) = θ^T(x_i − x_j) (or the hybrid variant with per-item offsets ζ_i − ζ_j) at zero. The active learner selects which pair to compare at each round based on the current model estimate, making the process sequential and adaptive rather than batch.

## Method

The paper derives the first upper bound on expected ordering error (Theorem 1) for a logistic preference model, showing it decays exponentially with T at a rate governed by max_{i,j} σ̇(z_ij^T θ_T)·||z_ij||_{H_T^{-1}(θ_T)} — a product of aleatoric uncertainty (annotation noise under the MLE) and epistemic uncertainty (parameter confidence in the z_ij direction). This motivates GURO, a greedy deterministic algorithm that at each round samples the pair maximizing this product, thereby reducing uncertainty where it most affects the ordering guarantee. A Bayesian variant (BayesGURO) replaces the MLE with a MAP estimate and samples by maximizing posterior predictive variance, which equals the GURO criterion to first order via Taylor expansion. To address model misspecification, a hybrid scoring function f_{θ,ζ}(x_i,x_j) = θ^T(φ(x_i)−φ(x_j)) + (ζ_i−ζ_j) adds per-item parameters that absorb residual noise while the contextual term preserves out-of-sample generalization, combining the low variance of contextual models with the low bias of per-item fitting.

## Results

The paper evaluates GURO and GURO Hybrid across four ordering tasks using Kendall's Tau distance (in-sample) and holdout comparison error (human data). On the synthetic X-ray task (200 images, 2000 comparisons), GURO and BayesGURO achieve the lowest in-sample ordering error and the smallest generalization gap (R_I_E − R_I_D) compared to all baselines. On the three human-annotated datasets (ImageClarity, WiscAds, IMDB-WIKI-SbS), GURO and GURO Hybrid consistently match or outperform all baselines, with the hybrid model combining fast initial convergence of contextual models with continued improvement of non-contextual models. In a continual-learning experiment (3000→6072 images), TrueSkill's error spikes dramatically upon adding new items, while GURO Hybrid is only moderately affected.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BALD (Houlsby et al., 2011), GURO wins on all tasks: BALD initially converges at a similar rate but plateaus at a higher error, attributed to its tendency to avoid high-aleatoric-uncertainty pairs where annotators disagree—precisely the pairs that need multiple comparisons to resolve. Against CoLSTIM (Bengs et al., 2022), GURO substantially outperforms, confirming that regret-minimization objectives are poorly aligned with recovering a complete ordering. Against TrueSkill (Herbrich et al., 2006), GURO Hybrid achieves faster initial convergence (matching contextual models) while avoiding the plateau that fully contextual methods exhibit; TrueSkill converges more slowly but steadily, and in the few-shot continual-learning setting its error increases drastically when 3072 new items are added, whereas GURO Hybrid's increase is much smaller due to contextual generalization. The NormMin variant (which ignores the aleatoric term) performs significantly worse than even Uniform sampling on certain instances, underscoring the importance of accounting for annotation noise in the sampling criterion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends active preference learning from the non-contextual setting (TrueSkill, Hamming-LUCB) to a fully contextual framework where item attributes enable out-of-sample generalization. The theoretical analysis builds directly on concentration results from logistic bandits (Filippi et al., 2010; Faury et al., 2020), but repurposes them to derive the first upper bound on expected ordering error as a function of the specific set of comparisons collected. The key conceptual departure from prior contextual active learning (BALD, CoLSTIM) is the explicit inclusion of aleatoric uncertainty (via the σ̇ term) in the sampling criterion, motivated by the observation that annotator noise is informative of item similarity and that regret-minimization objectives do not translate to full-ordering recovery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical justification for an active sampling strategy in the contextual ordering setting, bridging a gap between bandit theory and practical preference learning where annotations are expensive (e.g., medical imaging). The hybrid model combining contextual features with per-item parameters offers a practical recipe for balancing generalization and in-sample accuracy, with demonstrated benefits for continual learning when new items arrive. Open questions include whether the greedy sampling criterion is optimal (no matching lower bound exists), whether the approach scales to online settings with truly adaptive annotation, and whether representation learning can be integrated throughout the active learning loop rather than being fixed a priori.
