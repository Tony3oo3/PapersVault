## Problem

Existing deepfake detection benchmarks focus on binary classification with discriminative networks, and the only prior VQA-based work relies on costly human captions, targets a single manipulation region per image, and omits the open-ended setting. There is no unified, multi-stage protocol to systematically evaluate VLLMs on the multi-label fine-grained task of identifying which facial areas are manipulated, nor a fair matching strategy for comparing free-text model responses against multi-label ground truth.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a face image (frame-level, from seven public deepfake datasets plus one pseudo-fake splicing dataset) paired with a text prompt that frames detection as a VQA question. The prompt uses one of seven synonyms for 'deepfake' (e.g., 'manipulated', 'synthetic', 'altered') to probe instruction robustness. Outputs are autoregressive natural-language sentences: a 'Yes'/'No' for the binary stage, a subset of named regions for the multiple-choice stage, or a free-form description of manipulated areas for the open-ended stage. Because fine-grained labels are multi-label (multiple regions can be altered simultaneously), evaluation requires matching strategies beyond exact match—specifically a 'contains' check (with synonym expansion) and a CLIP text-embedding cosine similarity scored through a sigmoid with temperature 0.5.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-stage VQA evaluation protocol that progressively increases task difficulty: (1) binary detection with synonym-varying prompts scored by exact match, (2) multiple-choice fine-grained detection where the model selects from a fixed list of manipulation regions, and (3) open-ended fine-grained detection where the model freely names manipulated areas, matched to ground truth via both a contains-with-synonyms strategy and a CLIP text-embedding cosine similarity passed through a sigmoid (adapted from Conti et al.'s vocabulary-free classification). Four open-source VLLMs (BLIP-2, InstructBLIP with T5-xl and T5-xxl, LLaVA-1.5) are evaluated zero-shot across nine binary and three fine-grained benchmarks, with CLIP retrieval as a lower-bound baseline and GPT-4V as an upper bound. A supplementary experiment fine-tunes the LLaVA vision encoder with LoRA adapters (rank 32, 4-bit quantization) using a sigmoid loss over cosine similarity between image embeddings and text-embedding prototypes of positive/negative synonyms, yielding ~4% F1 improvement within-dataset without touching the LLM weights. Qualitative assessment uses BertScore against formatted ground-truth references and a 1–5 human rating protocol on a 100-sample R-Splicer subset, with Krippendorff's alpha of 0.75 confirming strong inter-annotator agreement.

## Results

Across nine binary benchmarks, no VLLM clearly dominates; BLIP-2 (3.74B params) is the most robust to prompt synonym variation, while LLaVA-1.5 with a LoRA-fine-tuned CLIP vision encoder gains up to +3.73% F1 on FF++ (76.83 vs. 73.10) and ~2% cross-dataset. In the novel open-ended fine-grained multi-label setting, LLaVA-1.5 achieves the best F1 (40.0 on SeqDeepFake attributes, 41.6 on R-Splicer with contains matching; 74.1 and 66.5 with CLIP-distance matching), though all models remain well below task-specific discriminative networks (e.g., LAA-Net reaches 91.93 mAP on CelebDF). All VLLMs struggle on in-the-wild datasets, with CelebDF F1 scores ranging only from 41.8% to 43.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLaVA-1.5 outperforms BLIP-2 on fine-grained F1 by a wide margin (40.0 vs. 20.4 on SeqDeepFake attributes; 41.6 vs. 31.3 on R-Splicer), while BLIP-2 is the most robust to binary prompt synonym variation despite being the smallest model (3.74B params). InstructBLIP-xxl (12.31B params) does not consistently beat its T5-xl counterpart, suggesting that additional language-model parameters introduce more hallucination on this abstract task rather than improving reasoning. Against the CLIP retrieval baseline, the VLLMs are competitive on binary F1 (e.g., LLaVA-1.5 achieves 66.15 vs. CLIP's 69.63 on SeqDeepFake attributes) but CLIP cannot perform fine-grained multi-label detection at all since it produces no natural-language output. GPT-4V, used as an upper bound on a 5k-sample subset, vastly outperforms all open-source VLLMs on three benchmarks and is comparable on the rest, confirming that the gap to proprietary models remains significant. The fine-tuned LLaVA-1.5 vision encoder (LoRA, 50 epochs on FF++) yields consistent but modest gains (~4% F1 within-dataset, ~2% cross-dataset), indicating that task-specific visual features help but the bottleneck remains the lack of deepfake-specific language supervision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the emerging line of work that frames deepfake detection as a Visual Question Answering task (Zhang et al. [87], Jia et al. [26]) by moving from binary yes/no questions to a multi-label fine-grained setting that identifies specific manipulation areas. It builds on CLIP-based text matching (Conti et al. [14]) and contains-matching strategies (Xu et al. [76]) to evaluate open-ended VLLM responses against ground-truth manipulation labels, and departs from prior VQA benchmarks that assume a single correct class by explicitly handling the multi-label nature of stacked manipulations. The evaluation protocol is positioned as a complement to discriminative deepfake detection benchmarks (DeepfakeBench [82], SHIELD [65]) that are ill-suited for generative, language-producing models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first systematic, open-source, and extendable multi-label VQA evaluation protocol for deepfake detection, filling a gap that prior binary-only benchmarks cannot address as VLLMs gain traction in forensics. Key open questions remain: zero-shot VLLMs still trail task-specific discriminative models by wide margins (especially on in-the-wild datasets like CelebDF), current datasets lack fine-grained language annotations, and the scarcity of deepfake-specific vision-language pre-training data limits performance. The finding that a simple LoRA fine-tune of the vision encoder yields meaningful gains (~4% F1 within-dataset) without any LLM modification suggests a practical path forward, but the broader challenge of building explainable, generalisable deepfake detectors via language reasoning remains largely unsolved.
