## Problem

Existing Graph Injection Attacks (GIAs) on Text-Attributed Graphs operate exclusively at the embedding level, injecting numerical node features rather than actual textual content. This is unrealistic because attackers in practice can only add raw text (e.g., fake papers) to public datasets, cannot replicate defenders' proprietary embedding pipelines, and produce uninterpretable embeddings that are trivially detectable. The paper asks whether coherent, interpretable text can be injected to achieve comparable adversarial effectiveness.

## Input / Output

The attacker receives a text-attributed graph (nodes with raw text, edges), a target node set, and a surrogate GNN, and must produce injected nodes whose features are raw text (titles and abstracts) plus edges connecting them to existing nodes. For WTGIA specifically, the BoW vocabulary partitions into specified words (must appear) and prohibited words (must be absent), and the generated text is constrained to a length limit derived from the 95th percentile of original document lengths. The output is a perturbed graph where injected nodes carry semantically coherent text that, when embedded by the defender's chosen method, degrades GNN classification accuracy on target nodes.

## Method

The paper introduces three text-level GIA designs. ITGIA generates adversarial embeddings via PGD on the unit sphere (with optional HAO regularization) and inverts them to text using the GTR/Vec2Text inversion model, but the injected embeddings fall outside the narrow interpretable region, yielding incoherent text with high perplexity. VTGIA prompts GPT-3.5 with heterophily, random, or mixing instructions to generate adversarial text, then optimizes graph structure via sequential injection, but sacrifices attack strength for interpretability. WTGIA, the strongest design, first applies a row-wise constrained FGSM to produce binary BoW embeddings under a sparsity budget, then formulates a 'word-to-text' generation task for LLMs (GPT-3.5 or Llama3) that must include specified words and exclude prohibited words (enforced via logit masking for open-source models), with multi-round correction to maximize word usage rate. Theorem 1 formalizes the trade-off: under a cosine-similarity unnoticeability constraint, the maximum number of harmful words (from other classes) that can be injected is bounded by m − c√(mk), where m is the interpretability budget and k is the target's word count, revealing that stronger attacks require more specified words, which in turn degrades text coherence.

## Results

On Cora, ITGIA (the inversion-based text-level GIA) achieves a best accuracy of 65.77% (with HAO, SeqGIA/MetaGIA) compared to 31.14% for the best embedding-level GIA, revealing a substantial performance gap due to information loss during embedding-to-text inversion. WTGIA with BoW embeddings achieves 35.33% accuracy (ATDGIA variant) on Cora, closely matching the embedding-level best of 31.14%, but degrades to 76.88% when transferred to GTR embeddings. On PubMed, WTGIA with GTR reaches 80.20% under LLM-as-predictor defense, showing that LLM-based classifiers can largely neutralize text-level injection attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three text-level GIAs (ITGIA, VTGIA, WTGIA) against five embedding-level GIA baselines (SeqGIA, MetaGIA, TDGIA, ATDGIA, AGIA) and the best embedding-level result. ITGIA substantially underperforms embedding-level attacks: on Cora, the best ITGIA variant reaches 65.77% vs. 31.14% for the best embedding-level GIA, a gap of ~35 percentage points caused by information loss during embedding-to-text inversion (average cosine similarity drops to 0.14 without HAO). VTGIA is even weaker, with the best Cora result at 80.78% (Mixing prompt, MetaGIA), far from the 31.14% embedding-level best. WTGIA with BoW embeddings is the only text-level method that matches or exceeds embedding-level performance (35.33% ATDGIA on Cora vs. 31.14% Best Emb.), but this advantage vanishes when transferred to GTR embeddings (76.88% vs. 31.14%). Against LLM-as-predictor defenses, WTGIA's impact is minimal on PubMed (89.30% vs. 89.80% clean), indicating that simple LLM-based classifiers can effectively neutralize text-level injection attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established line of Graph Injection Attacks (GIAs) — which have been studied at the embedding level since Wang et al. (2018) through AFGSM (2020), TDGIA (2021), and GIA-HAO (2022) — to the text level, where attackers must generate actual textual content rather than numerical vectors. It repurposes the Vec2Text inversion framework (Morris et al., 2023) for the ITGIA design and adapts the row-wise constrained FGSM from AFGSM to binary BoW embeddings for WTGIA, while introducing LLM-based text generation (GPT-3.5, Llama3) as a new mechanism for producing interpretable injected content. The key conceptual departure is that all prior GIAs assume the attacker controls the embedding directly, whereas this work forces the attacker to work through the text-to-embedding pipeline, exposing a previously invisible interpretability constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that text interpretability imposes a hard, quantifiable trade-off on attack effectiveness that is entirely absent at the embedding level, fundamentally complicating the design of realistic graph attacks. The finding that LLM-based predictors (bypassing GNNs entirely) provide strong defense against text-level GIAs suggests that the graph structure — the very mechanism injection attacks exploit — may be unnecessary for robust node classification, pointing toward a paradigm shift in how defenders should approach TAG security. However, the practical impact is limited by poor transferability across embedding methods and the fact that current text-level GIAs remain substantially weaker than their embedding-level counterparts, leaving open whether truly effective and interpretable text-level attacks are achievable at all.
