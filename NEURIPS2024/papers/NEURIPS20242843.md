## Problem

Training a diffusion model directly on full 3D CT volumes (e.g., 256×256×500) is computationally infeasible due to prohibitive memory, data, and training-time requirements. Existing diffusion-based 3D CT reconstruction methods either apply hand-crafted total-variation regularization between independently reconstructed 2D slices (sacrificing z-axis consistency) or train separate 2D priors on orthogonal planes, neither of which properly captures the joint 3D image distribution.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242843/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a CT sinogram (2D projection measurements at a set of view angles) and a linear forward model (system matrix) as input, and produces a full 3D CT volume as output. Volumes are 256×256 in the axial plane with 500 slices (AAPM) or 256 slices (LIDC) in the z-direction. The diffusion prior operates on 3D patches of k=3 consecutive slices (3-channel input/output) rather than the full volume, and the reconstruction supports sparse-view settings (4–100 views) and limited-angle (90°) acquisition geometries.

## Method

DiffusionBlend++ trains a 2D U-Net diffusion model (fine-tuned from a natural-image checkpoint) on 3D patches of k=3 adjacent axial slices, with a relative positional encoding input that encodes the slice separation distance, so the network learns the joint distribution of neighboring slices rather than treating them independently. At reconstruction time, the volume is randomly partitioned into 3D patches at each diffusion step using two complementary schemes: an adjacency partition (consecutive slices) and a cross partition (jumping slices, e.g., {1,4,7},{2,5,8},{3,6,9}), and the full-volume score is approximated by summing the per-patch score predictions. This random partitioning stochastically averages over different slice groupings, enforcing cross-slice consistency without any external regularizer. Data consistency with the measurements is enforced via a Krylov-subspace (conjugate-gradient) step applied to the Tweedie estimate at each DDIM sampling iteration, yielding a 32-minute wall-clock reconstruction for 200 NFEs on a 256×256×500 volume.

## Results

On the AAPM dataset (256×256×500 volumes), DiffusionBlend++ achieves PSNR of 35.69/34.68/32.93 dB and SSIM of 0.966/0.960/0.952 for 8/6/4 sparse views (axial), outperforming the next-best method DDS by 2.5/2.7/3.7 dB. On the larger LIDC-IDRI dataset (357 training volumes), DiffusionBlend++ reaches 34.46/33.03/30.98 dB PSNR for 8/6/4 views, again leading all baselines. For limited-angle CT (90° arc), DiffusionBlend++ achieves 35.86/36.03/37.45 dB PSNR on AAPM axial/sagittal/coronal views, a ~7 dB margin over the best baseline. Reconstruction takes 32 minutes with 200 NFEs, compared to 48 min for DDS and ~1400 min for DiffusionMBIR (2000 NFEs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffusionBlend++ outperforms all five baselines across every view count and both datasets. Against the closest competitor DDS [15], the gap is 2.5–3.7 dB PSNR on AAPM and 2.8–3.8 dB on LIDC; notably, DDS 2D (with the TV regularizer removed) drops 0.3–0.5 dB below full DDS, confirming that DDS relies on the hand-crafted TV term rather than a learned 3D prior. DiffusionMBIR [13] is competitive on LIDC (32.67 vs. 34.46 dB at 8 views) but requires 2000 NFEs and ~20 hours of wall time versus 32 minutes for DiffusionBlend++. TPDM [14] performs poorly, especially under extreme sparsity (21.99 dB at 4 views on LIDC), likely due to its requirement for cubic volumes and alternating axial/coronal updates. FBP-UNet [42] is a supervised 2D method that must be retrained per task and lags by 9–10 dB PSNR. The ablation (Table 6/7) further shows that the adjacency-slice blending adds +1.17 dB and cross-slice blending adds another +1.63 dB over a fixed partition, validating both components of the score-blending strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of diffusion-based 3D CT reconstruction initiated by DiffusionMBIR [13] and accelerated by DDS [15], both of which learn 2D slice priors and enforce cross-slice consistency via a hand-crafted TV regularizer. It also departs from TPDM [14], which trains separate 2D diffusion models on orthogonal planes and alternates between them. The key conceptual departure is learning a joint 3D-patch distribution (multiple adjacent slices as a single input) with positional encoding, then approximating the full-volume score via random partitioning and score blending — eliminating the need for any external regularizer. The score-matching theory in Appendix A.1 directly extends Song & Ermon [1] to decomposable distributions, and the reconstruction pipeline reuses the Krylov-subspace data-consistency step from DDS [15] and DDIM sampling [29].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that a learned 3D-patch diffusion prior can replace hand-crafted cross-slice regularizers for large-volume CT reconstruction (256×256×500), achieving 2–3 dB PSNR gains over the closest baseline (DDS) while being ~33% faster in wall time. The practical impact is significant for reducing patient radiation dose in sparse-view and limited-angle CT. Key limitations include reliance on noiseless simulated measurements, evaluation on only two public datasets, and the use of a pretrained natural-image checkpoint (k=3) due to medical data scarcity. Open questions include robustness to real measurement noise, extension to other 3D modalities (MRI, ultrasound), and whether latent-space variants can further reduce the 200-NFE inference cost.
