## Problem

Despite the growing adoption of Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs), there is no clear theoretical understanding of how the properties of a concept set—its size and its degree of misspecification relative to the true predictor—govern model performance. This paper asks: what makes a concept set good, and under what conditions (sample size, distribution shift) do CBMs actually outperform their non-bottleneck counterparts?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243177/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (resized to 224×224, center-cropped, and normalized) and produces a class label via a linear probe. The key intermediate step is a fixed-dimensional concept vector: each image is embedded by CLIP ViT-B/32, and its concept representation is computed as the cosine similarity between the image embedding and the text embeddings of a curated concept set (generated by GPT-4). The output is a one-hot class prediction from a linear regression trained on these concept activations, making the bottleneck dimensionality equal to the number of selected concepts rather than the full CLIP embedding dimension.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical risk decomposition (Theorem 1) showing that the expected loss of a CBM equals a sample-efficiency term scaling with (d − |C|) plus a misspecification term ε = ‖ω* C⊥‖²_F whose coefficient grows with n, establishing that small concept sets win in low-data regimes while large, complete sets win asymptotically. A second result (Theorem 2) proves that confounder-induced bias in the linear estimator is proportional to the inner products between concept directions and spurious-feature directions, so excluding confound-related concepts from the set reduces OOD bias. Practically, the authors propose a three-step concept-set generation pipeline: (1) sample candidate concepts from GPT-4 conditioned on the task context, (2) discard concepts whose activation entropy falls below a threshold (non-informative), and (3) greedily add concepts only if they reduce cross-validated empirical risk beyond a margin, thereby balancing expressiveness against redundancy. The full pipeline is validated on CIFAR-10 (data efficiency), MetaShift (subpopulation and domain shifts), and CINIC-10 (OOD generalization), consistently showing CBMs outperform the vanilla CLIP linear probe, especially in low-data and shifted settings.

## Results

On CINIC-10 OOD generalization, the CBM achieves 78.8±0.7% accuracy versus 70.4±1.2% for the vanilla CLIP linear probe, an 8.4-point improvement. On the 12 MetaShift OOD tasks, the CBM outperforms the non-bottleneck baseline by typically more than 10% accuracy, with gains exceeding 50% in some settings. In the low-data regime on CIFAR-10 (n < 100), smaller concept sets (20 concepts) yield the best sample efficiency, while larger sets (50 concepts) dominate in the high-data regime (n > 200) due to reduced misspecification. Against the Label-Free CBM baseline on CIFAR-10, the proposed concept curation method maintains a consistent accuracy gap of over 7.3% across all training sizes, reaching up to 17.7%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline throughout the paper is a linear probe trained directly on CLIP ViT-B/32 image embeddings (the 'vanilla' embedding). The CBM consistently outperforms this baseline in low-data regimes across CIFAR-10, CUB-200-2011, Food-101, and DTD, but the baseline catches up and surpasses the CBM in the large-sample regime (n > 200 on CIFAR-10) due to the misspecification term ε dominating. On distribution shift (MetaShift subpopulation imbalance), the CBM shows larger accuracy gains as the shift becomes more pronounced, confirming Theorem 2's prediction that excluding confounder-aligned concepts reduces bias. On the 12 MetaShift OOD tasks, the CBM beats the vanilla baseline by >10% accuracy on every task, with some gaps exceeding 50%. The only direct method-to-method comparison is against the Label-Free Concept Bottleneck Model (LF-CBM, Oikarinen et al. 2023) on CIFAR-10, where the paper's concept curation procedure outperforms LF-CBM by more than 7.3% across all training set sizes and up to 17.7%, demonstrating that principled concept set selection (minimizing |C| while controlling ε) is critical for CBM performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first systematic theoretical analysis of how concept set properties (size and misspecification) govern Concept Bottleneck Model performance, building directly on the CBM architecture of Koh et al. (2020) and the CLIP-based concept identification paradigm of Oikarinen et al. (2023). The risk decomposition in Theorem 1 follows the mathematical backbone of Radhakrishnan et al. (2023) on transfer learning with kernel methods, adapted to the bottleneck setting. Prior CBM work focused on practical flexibility (post-hoc concepts, residual fitting, LLM-guided identification) without formalizing *when* and *why* a concept set helps; this paper fills that gap by identifying expressiveness and model-aware inductive bias as the two core desiderata and deriving explicit risk bounds that separate sample-efficiency gains from misspecification costs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper gives CBM practitioners a principled recipe for concept set design: minimize |C| while keeping misspecification ε small, with the clear prediction that CBMs shine in low-data and distribution-shift regimes but can underperform strong baselines in large-data settings when concepts are incomplete. The errors-in-variables analysis (Section 4.1) is practically important because it warns that noisy concept identification degrades *all* coefficients, not just the noisy one, which has direct implications for how carefully concept identifiers must be validated. Key open questions include the interaction of confounding, noise, and in-domain generalization simultaneously, the scalability of the greedy concept-selection procedure to large datasets, and whether the isotropic-distribution assumption in Theorem 1 holds for real CLIP embeddings. The CBM assumption itself—that human concepts transfer predictive power to ML models—remains an empirical bet that the paper validates only on a handful of vision tasks.
