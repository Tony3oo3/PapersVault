## Problem

Human experts naturally develop the ability to skip intermediate reasoning steps through practice, improving efficiency without sacrificing accuracy. Language models, however, lack any intrinsic motivation to minimize reasoning steps, and no prior work has investigated whether they can learn this human-like step-skipping behavior or whether such behavior helps or hurts generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241440/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During the initialization and iteration phases, the model receives a text question concatenated with an explicit step-count instruction (e.g., 'Solve it in n steps'), and produces a stepwise reasoning chain of exactly n steps ending in a final answer. In the standard model evaluation phase, the step-count instruction is removed, so the model receives only the raw question and freely decides how many steps to use. The three synthetic tasks (Analog of Algebra, Multi-digit Addition, Directional Reasoning) have well-defined intermediate steps, enabling precise measurement of how many steps the model actually takes versus the full-step baseline.

## Method

The framework has two phases. In initialization, the model is fine-tuned on full-step reasoning data (cold start) or full-step plus manually created skipped-step data (warm start), with each example paired with a step-count instruction to teach the model to produce exactly n steps. In the iteration phase, the model is prompted to solve the same problems in fewer steps (n−1 or n−2); responses that are both correct and meet the reduced step count are filtered and mixed with the original full-step data to form an expanded dataset for the next round of training. This self-generated skipping data is accumulated over iterations, and at each iteration a separate standard model is trained on the mixed dataset without step instructions to solidify the skipping behavior. The key insight is that mixing complete and skipped reasoning paths acts as a form of easy-to-hard generalization: the model learns to solve problems more efficiently while maintaining or even improving out-of-distribution performance, rather than developing harmful shortcut biases.

## Results

Across three synthetic reasoning tasks, iterative step-skipping training reduces the average number of reasoning steps while maintaining or improving accuracy. On Analog of Algebra, phi-3-mini improves OOD-hard accuracy from 4.05% (cold start) to 11.13% (Iter 5) while reducing average steps from 10.01 to 7.98. On Multi-digit Addition, Llama2-7B achieves a dramatic 13.91% gain on OOD-easy (0.06% → 13.97%) and 4.75% on OOD-hard (0.00% → 4.75%), with average steps dropping from 2.86 to 1.46. On Directional Reasoning, Llama2-7B improves OOD-hard accuracy by 9.8% (42.00% → 51.80%) while phi-3-mini gains 11.2% (62.67% → 73.87%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it compares three internal phases of its own framework: cold start (trained only on full-step data), warm start (augmented with human-created skipped steps), and iterative training (model-generated skipped data mixed with full-step data over 5 iterations). The cold start model serves as the primary baseline. Iterative step-skipping training consistently outperforms cold start on OOD-easy and OOD-hard across all three tasks and both models, while maintaining near-perfect in-domain accuracy. The largest gains appear on Multi-digit Addition, where cold start models nearly fail on OOD (0.06% for Llama2, 35.93% for phi-3-mini on OOD-easy), but iterative training lifts these to 13.97% and 46.61% respectively. Warm start provides a modest boost over cold start but is consistently outperformed by iterative training, suggesting that model-generated skipping data is more effective than human-crafted shortcuts. An ablation (Table 8) further confirms that mixing full-step and skipped data outperforms training on skipped data alone, as the latter risks shortcut bias that harms OOD generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of chain-of-thought reasoning, easy-to-hard generalization, and System 1/System 2 cognitive science. It repurposes the iterative self-training paradigm (where a model generates its own training data) for a new purpose: teaching models to produce shorter, step-skipped reasoning paths rather than merely improving answer quality. Conceptually, it draws on Blessing & Anderson's (1996) cognitive science of step-skipping in algebra and connects to recent work on distilling System 2 reasoning into System 1 (Deng et al., Yu et al.), but is the first to operationalize step-skipping as a controllable, learnable behavior in LMs through a structured initialization-iteration framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key finding that mixing full-step and step-skipped reasoning data improves (rather than degrades) out-of-distribution generalization challenges the common assumption that shorter reasoning paths are merely biased shortcuts. This provides a concrete mechanism for easy-to-hard generalization: training on simpler, partially-skipped reasoning can transfer to harder problems. However, the evidence is primarily on synthetic tasks with limited compositional complexity, and the GSM8K experiments show the method struggles with genuinely complex multi-step problems, leaving open whether step-skipping generalizes to real-world reasoning. The lack of a principled stopping criterion and the modest gains on harder OOD settings suggest this is a promising but preliminary direction.
