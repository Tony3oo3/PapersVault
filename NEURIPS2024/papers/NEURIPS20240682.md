## Problem

Prior BFSD benchmarks suffer from six major deficiencies—train/test leakage within the same binary, cross-binary function duplication, under-specified labeling, disabled compiler optimizations, near-exclusive use of small Linux-only datasets, and artificial pool-size restrictions—that collectively prevent models from generalizing to real-world deployment on large-scale Windows binaries and malware. The paper asks whether the sophisticated feature engineering and complex architectures (GNNs, transformers over CFGs) currently dominating the field are actually necessary, or whether simpler approaches can match or exceed them when evaluated fairly.

## Input / Output

The model consumes the raw byte sequence of a single binary function with no disassembly, decompilation, or other preprocessing. It outputs a 128-dimensional embedding vector, which is then used with Faiss HNSW approximate nearest-neighbor search to rank all candidate functions in the corpus by cosine distance, producing a similarity ordering from which MRR and Recall@k are computed. Functions are padded or truncated to a fixed window size of 8 bytes per convolutional step, and the training set contains ~83M functions across 8.9M unique labels.

## Method

REFuSe adapts the MalConv architecture to produce embeddings rather than classifications: each input byte is mapped to a learned 8-dimensional feature vector, passed through two 1-D convolutional layers (window 8, stride 8), a temporal max-pooling layer, and a single fully-connected layer yielding the 128-dim embedding. Training uses triplet loss with cosine distance and margin α=0.2, employing semi-hard online triplet mining where each anchor is paired with at most one positive and the hardest available semi-hard negative from a batch of 600 functions (300 unique labels × 2 functions each). The key finding is that this minimal raw-byte CNN, trained on the 83M-function Assemblage dataset, achieves state-of-the-art MRR on 4 of 5 benchmark datasets, outperforming GNNs over ACFGs, jump-aware transformers, and Ghidra's BSim by large margins—suggesting that existing complex feature extractors are not being leveraged to their full potential. The REFUSE-BENCH benchmark itself contributes five datasets (Assemblage, MOTIF, Common Libraries, Marcelli Dataset-1, BinaryCorp) totaling over 32M functions, with careful train/test partitioning by source project, common-function filtering, and duplicate removal to eliminate the six identified leakage and realism issues.

## Results

REFuSe, a simple CNN over raw function bytes with no disassembly or feature engineering, achieves MRR of 0.61 on Assemblage (24.5M functions), 0.68 on MOTIF (real malware), 0.69 on Common Libraries, and 0.68 on Marcelli Dataset-1, making it the top-scoring model on 4 of 5 datasets. Its performance is notably stable across domains, with a range of only 0.22 between its highest and lowest MRR, compared to 0.53 for the GNN and 0.59 for jTrans. The only dataset where REFuSe does not lead is BinaryCorp (MRR 0.47), where jTrans scores 0.693, though this comparison is confounded by jTrans's use of a restricted pool size of 10,000 versus REFuSe searching all 4.79M functions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REFuSe decisively outperforms the GNN [32] on all five datasets, with the largest gaps on Common Libraries (0.69 vs 0.11 MRR) and Marcelli Dataset-1 (0.68 vs 0.05–0.08), suggesting the GNN's ACFG-based features do not generalize beyond its original small Linux training set. Against jTrans [54], REFuSe wins on Assemblage (0.61 vs 0.26), MOTIF (0.68 vs 0.10), and Marcelli (0.68 vs 0.08), but jTrans leads on BinaryCorp (0.693 vs 0.47), a result the authors attribute to jTrans's pool-size restriction (10,000 functions) versus REFuSe's full 4.79M-function search. Even when jTrans is fine-tuned on Assemblage data (jTrans-F), it still trails REFuSe on Assemblage (0.475 vs 0.61) and MOTIF (0.069 vs 0.68), indicating the gap is architectural rather than purely data-driven. BSim, Ghidra's hand-engineered p-code similarity tool, is outperformed by REFuSe on every dataset while requiring ~24 minutes of CPU time per file for feature extraction, versus REFuSe's direct byte-level inference. The paper's central finding is that these complex, feature-engineered baselines appear over-engineered: a lightweight CNN on raw bytes matches or exceeds them in most realistic settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

REFuSe directly adapts the MalConv architecture (Raff et al., 2017/2021), originally designed for raw-byte malware classification, by replacing its classification head with an embedding layer trained via triplet loss. This departs sharply from the dominant BFSD lineage—GNNs over attributed control-flow graphs (Gemini, Li et al.) and transformer-based assembly models (jTrans, BinBert, PalmTree)—all of which require expensive disassembly, decompilation, or intermediate-representation extraction as a preprocessing step. The paper also introduces REFUSE-BENCH, a benchmark that explicitly corrects six methodological deficiencies (data leakage, missing Windows data, pool-size restrictions, etc.) identified across the prior BFSD evaluation literature, positioning itself as a corrective to the field's evaluation practices rather than a new modeling paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a lightweight raw-byte CNN matches or exceeds state-of-the-art GNN and transformer models on four of five datasets challenges the field's core assumption that sophisticated feature engineering (disassembly, CFGs, p-code) is a prerequisite for competitive BFSD performance. REFUSE-BENCH, with its 135K-binary Assemblage test set, real malware (MOTIF), and cross-version library evaluation, provides the first large-scale, Windows-centric benchmark that mirrors deployment conditions, and will likely become the standard reference for future BFSD work. Open questions remain around whether domain-knowledge features can eventually surpass raw-byte baselines at even larger scale, how to formalize the spectrum of function similarity beyond binary same/different labels, and whether the labeling heuristics (size thresholds, name-length rules) generalize beyond C/C++.
