## Problem

State-of-the-art dysfluency modeling approaches (2D-Alignment, H-UDM) rely on non-monotonic alignment that fails to scale—increasing training data yields no further improvement. Additionally, no large-scale dysfluency corpus exists, and no end-to-end learning framework unifies articulatory representation, alignment, and dysfluency detection. The paper tackles all three gaps simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243229/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a 16 kHz raw speech waveform and a reference text as input. The waveform is first inverted to 50 Hz EMA articulatory features (upsampled to 200 Hz) via a pretrained UAAI model, then decomposed into 40 gestural scores at 4× downsampled resolution matching WavLM features. The output is a structured per-word dysfluency annotation (type: repetition, block, replacement, prolongation, missing; with timestamps), produced by an LLaMA-7B language model conditioned on CSA alignment pairs (C_i^S, γ(C_i^S)) and word-level timestamps.

## Method

SSDM combines three novel components trained sequentially then end-to-end via curriculum learning. First, a Neural Variational Gestural VAE decomposes articulatory data into learned gesture kernels and gestural scores using convolutive matrix factorization, with Gumbel-Softmax for discrete duration, Sigmoid for intensity, online sparse sampling, and a multi-scale decoder; gestural scores are refined via self-distillation from WavLM acoustic posteriors and text-conditioned priors through a Glow flow. Second, the Connectionist Subsequence Aligner (CSA) imposes a Longest Common Subsequence constraint on the CTC forward-backward algorithm, yielding a differentiable monotonic aligner that maps each reference phoneme to a consecutive subsequence in the dysfluent phonetic alignment—enabling per-phoneme dysfluency detection without non-monotonic search. Third, LLaMA-7B (fine-tuned with LoRA and Vicuna instruction tuning) consumes the alignment pairs and reference text to produce typed, timestamped dysfluency labels. The key insight is that monotonic subsequence alignment (LSA) is both scalable and semantically sufficient for dysfluency localization, and that physically grounded gestural scores outperform SSL units in scaling behavior.

## Results

On simulated dysfluency detection (Table 2), SSDM achieves 81.4% F1 and 70.4% Matching Score (MS) when trained on the full Libri-Dys dataset, outperforming the prior state-of-the-art H-UDM (75.9% F1, 61.1% MS) by 5.5 and 9.3 points respectively. Crucially, SSDM demonstrates positive scalability (SF1=0.76, SF2=0.19), meaning performance improves with more training data, whereas H-UDM shows negative scalability (SF1=-0.61). On real nfvPPA clinical speech (Table 3), SSDM also outperforms large-scale speech understanding systems (LTU-AS-13B, SALMONN-13B, ChatGPT), though detection remains challenging due to the gap between simulated and real disordered speech.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSDM outperforms H-UDM [2] (the prior SOTA dysfluency detection model) by 5.5 F1 points and 9.3 MS points on Libri-Dys-trained evaluation, and by 6.5 F1 / 3.6 MS on VCTK++ evaluation. Against HuBERT [108] SSL units used for phonetic transcription (Table 1), SSDM's gestural scores with self-distillation achieve 90.8% F1 vs. 87.8% F1, and critically show positive scalability (SF1=0.56) where HuBERT shows near-zero (SF1=0.18). On real nfvPPA clinical speech (Table 3), SSDM surpasses large-scale speech understanding systems LTU-AS-13B [24] and SALMONN-13B [27], as well as ChatGPT [109], which the authors note show 'limited performance in dysfluent speech detection.' Ablations confirm that the Connectionist Subsequence Aligner (CSA) and gestural scores are the primary contributors, while the LLaMA-7B language model component adds only marginal gains (81.4% vs. 80.7% F1), suggesting the alignment and representation modules carry most of the signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gestural phonology tradition (Browman & Goldstein) and the neural convolutive matrix factorization line (Lian et al. 2022, 2023) by proposing Neural Variational Gestural Modeling as a scalable speech representation for dysfluency tasks. It departs from the authors' own prior 2D-Alignment work [1, 2] by replacing non-monotonic alignment with a monotonic Local Subsequence Aligner (CSA), arguing that per-reference-token monotonic subsequence mapping is both sufficient and more scalable for dysfluency detection. The system also builds on CTC-style forward-backward alignment, self-supervised speech features (WavLM, HuBERT) for distillation, and instruction-tuned LLMs (LLaMA-7B via LTU-style prompting) for end-to-end dysfluency annotation, while introducing the large-scale Libri-Dys simulated corpus to address the data scarcity that has constrained prior dysfluency research.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical scalability bottleneck in dysfluency modeling: prior non-monotonic alignment methods plateau with additional training data, whereas the proposed gestural-score + CSA pipeline shows consistent gains as data scales from 30% to 100% of Libri-Dys. The open-sourced Libri-Dys corpus (3,983 hours, 7× larger than LibriTTS) is a significant practical resource for the field. However, the gap between simulated and real disordered speech (nfvPPA) remains substantial, the LLM component contributes marginally (ablation shows near-identical performance without it), and the authors acknowledge that data scale is still inadequate and that learnable WFSTs or real-time MRI-based representations may offer more natural solutions. The interpretability of gestural scores (e.g., GradCAM revealing incorrect tongue movement direction) opens a path toward clinically meaningful explainability in speech therapy tools.
