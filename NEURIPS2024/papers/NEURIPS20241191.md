## Problem

Perceptual learning exhibits two seemingly contradictory phenomena: specificity (improvement restricted to trained retinal locations or features) and transfer (improvement generalizing to untrained conditions). Existing computational models can account for one phenomenon but not both, leaving a unified explanation lacking. This paper asks how the brain balances fast task adaptation against slow feature refinement to produce both effects under different training paradigms.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single-channel Vernier discrimination image (400×200 or 800×200 pixels) containing two Gabor patches of orthogonal orientation on a Gaussian noise background, with a variable pixel offset between the patches encoding difficulty. It produces a binary decision (upper orientation more leftward vs. rightward) via a sigmoid output, and the discrimination threshold is extracted by fitting a cumulative-normal psychometric function across 10 difficulty levels to find the offset yielding 80% accuracy. Testing is performed at trained and untrained retinal locations and orientations to quantify specificity versus transfer.

## Method

The dual-learning model is a three-stage hierarchical network: (1) a HyperBF feature-extraction layer using 40×18 Gabor basis functions to form retinotopic representations; (2) a feedforward layer with competitive Hebbian learning (slow rate η_f) that strengthens connections at locations where stimuli are repeatedly presented, inducing location-specific feature refinement; and (3) a three-layer CNN with global max pooling (fast rate η_t) that reads out task-relevant information for the binary decision via cross-entropy backpropagation. The key mechanism is the rate asymmetry: task-based learning dominates early in training and enables transfer through the translation-invariant max pooling, while feature-based learning accumulates slowly and only becomes dominant after excessive repetition of the same stimulus condition, producing specificity. This interplay naturally generates the observed transition from transfer to specificity as training sessions increase, and the double-training effect where a second training phase at a new condition restores transferability by modifying the feature representations locked in by the first phase.

## Results

The dual-learning model successfully replicates four classical perceptual learning phenomena: specificity under condition-specific training, transfer under varied training, transition from transfer to specificity with increasing training sessions, and transfer restoration via double training. In the ablation study, combined feature- and task-based learning reaches 90% accuracy in 81.3 epochs versus 84.7 epochs for task-based learning alone (t=3.90, p<0.001, 50 repetitions). In the transition experiment (Fig. 6), the model reproduces the experimental finding that transfer performance degrades monotonically as training sessions increase from 2 to 12, with all pairwise comparisons statistically significant (p-values ranging from 0.02 to 2.1×10⁻²¹, Table A4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against other computational models in a head-to-head quantitative benchmark. Instead, it validates the dual-learning model against four independent sets of psychophysical experimental data: specificity (Xiao et al. 2008, ref. [28]), transfer via varied training (Xie & Yu 2020, ref. [34]), transition from transfer to specificity (Jeter et al. 2010, ref. [26]), and double-training-enabled transfer (Xiao et al. 2008, ref. [28]). In each case, the model's predicted learning curves and threshold improvements qualitatively and quantitatively match the human data. The ablation studies (Sec. C.4) further confirm that the relative learning rates are critical: accelerating feature-based learning by 10× destroys the transfer phenomenon and the transition effect (p-values increase substantially, e.g., T8 vs. T12 goes from p=0.02 to p=0.61), while slowing task-based learning by 10× eliminates all significant differences between conditions (all p-values > 0.48), demonstrating that the specific rate ratio is essential for the model to reproduce the observed phenomena.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a computational implementation of the two-stage model theory of perceptual learning (Shibata et al., 2014), which posits coexisting feature-based and task-based plasticity but lacked a concrete neural network realization. It departs from earlier specificity-focused models (HyperBF, reweighting) that could not explain transfer, and from later transfer-focused models (Dosher et al.'s translation-invariant reweighting, Li et al.'s CNN) that could not account for specificity. The key architectural departure is the explicit separation of a slow Hebbian feature-refinement stage from a fast supervised CNN readout stage, with the relative learning rates governing the specificity-transfer balance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual-learning framework offers a principled, minimal explanation for why perceptual learning exhibits specificity under repetitive training but transfer under varied training, and crucially, the gradual transition between the two as stimulus repetitions increase. The 'to learn or not to learn' framing elevates this from a perceptual-learning curiosity to a general principle about how learning agents balance task adaptation cost against representational refinement. However, the model is deliberately simple (1D position, single Vernier task, no biological details like lateral inhibition or cortical layers), and the authors acknowledge that extending it to richer tasks, 2D retinotopy, and AI applications remains open. For AI practitioners, the fast/slow dual-learning decomposition is a potentially transferable design principle for continual learning systems.
