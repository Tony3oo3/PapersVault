## Problem

Training node embedding models on large directed graphs requires sampling because the full adjacency matrix is infeasible to observe, yet standard uniform sampling ignores structural priors that could drastically reduce the number of entries needed. For transitively-closed DAGs (hierarchies), no formal characterization existed of the minimal subset of adjacency matrix entries sufficient to uniquely identify the graph, nor a sampling strategy that exploits this structure to improve both efficiency and performance.

## Input / Output

The method takes a transitively-closed DAG as input (represented by its adjacency matrix) and first computes a 'sidigraph' — a signed digraph specifying exactly which positive edges (from the transitive reduction) and which negative edges (the minimal elements of a poset over the edge complement) are needed to uniquely distinguish the graph. The trained model then produces 64-dimensional box embeddings (two 64-dim corner vectors per node) whose containment relationships encode the full transitive closure. The key unusual aspect is that training consumes only the reduced edge set (e.g., 99.78% fewer negatives on MeSH) rather than the full adjacency matrix, yet recovers the complete graph structure.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal framework using signed digraphs (sidigraphs) to identify the minimal set of adjacency matrix entries that uniquely distinguish a graph among all transitively-closed DAGs. It defines a partial order on negative edges where (a→b) ≼ (a→c) if (c→b) is a positive edge, proves this forms a poset for DAGs, and shows that the minimal elements of this poset constitute the provably minimal negative edge set (Theorem 1). An explicit algorithm, FINDMINDISTINGUISHER, computes this set by iteratively pruning negative edges that are implied by transitivity. The second key contribution is proving that box embeddings (GT-BOX) possess a 'transitivity bias' — their energy function's threshold-based edge prediction is automatically transitive — which, combined with the minimal sidigraph, guarantees that training on the reduced edge set suffices to recover the full graph. This yields a 'hierarchy-aware sampling' procedure that replaces uniform negative sampling with sampling from the pruned set, achieving up to 99% reduction in training examples while improving convergence for models with transitivity bias.

## Results

On synthetic transitively-closed DAGs (Balanced Trees, nCRP, Price's model with ~8K nodes), GT-BOX with hierarchy-aware negative sampling (E_H*⁻) achieves AUF1C scores of 0.658, 0.775, and 0.602, matching or exceeding the full-edge-complement baseline (0.568, 0.796, 0.582) while reducing the negative training pool by 86–99%. On the real-world MeSH 2020 taxonomy (~30K nodes), GT-BOX with E_H*⁻ outperforms sampling from the full edge complement while using only 0.22% of the negative edges (a 99.78% reduction). In contrast, SIM-VEC (which lacks transitivity bias) degrades severely under hierarchy-aware sampling, dropping from AUF1C 0.158 to 0.336 on Price's model and failing to converge above 0.4 F1 on representative graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard GT-BOX training with random uniform negative sampling over the full edge complement E̅ and positive sampling over the transitive closure E^tc, as established in Boratko et al. (2021a). GT-BOX with hierarchy-aware sampling (reduced negative set E_H*⁻) matches or beats this baseline on all three synthetic graph types: AUF1C improves from 0.568→0.658 on Balanced Trees and 0.582→0.602 on Price's model, while remaining comparable on nCRP (0.796 vs. 0.775). On MeSH 2020, GT-BOX with E_H*⁻ converges to the highest F1 while using 99.78% fewer negative examples than the full-edge-complement baseline. The critical contrast is with SIM-VEC: when the same reduced negative set E_H*⁻ is used, SIM-VEC's performance collapses (e.g., AUF1C drops from 0.508 to 0.409 on nCRP, and F1 fails to exceed 0.4 on representative graphs), confirming that the synergy between transitivity bias and hierarchy-aware sampling is essential and not merely a sampling artifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GT-BOX model (Boratko et al., NeurIPS 2021), which introduced a smooth, trainable box embedding for directed graphs, by proving it possesses a formal 'transitivity bias' property that makes it compatible with a drastically reduced training set. The hierarchy-aware sampling strategy departs from the standard random uniform negative sampling paradigm in graph representation learning (Yang et al. 2020, Mikolov et al. 2013) and from structure-aware variants (Ahrabian et al. 2020, Chen et al. 2021) by leveraging a provably minimal set of negative edges derived from a novel signed-digraph framework. The theoretical contribution—characterizing the minimal distinguishing sidigraph for transitively-closed DAGs via a poset on negative edges—connects to classical graph theory (transitive reduction, Aho et al. 1972) but applies it in a new context of representation learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 99% reduction in training examples while maintaining or improving F1 on both synthetic hierarchies and the ~30K-node MeSH taxonomy is practically significant for large-scale ontology and taxonomy embedding, where the full adjacency matrix is infeasible to enumerate. The clean theoretical separation—GT-BOX succeeds while SIM-VEC catastrophically fails under the same reduced training set—provides strong evidence that transitivity bias is a necessary (not merely helpful) inductive bias for hierarchy representation, sharpening the design criteria for future embedding models. Key limitations include restriction to strictly transitively-closed DAGs (near-closed graphs are unaddressed) and the open question of whether the signed-digraph framework generalizes to other graph properties (e.g., partial orderings, bipartite structures) paired with corresponding inductive biases.
