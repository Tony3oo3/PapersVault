## Problem

Skeleton-based action recognition suffers severe accuracy degradation (20%+) in cross-dataset settings due to temporal mismatch — the same action is cropped or defined differently across datasets, making training samples partial observations of complete action sequences. Existing approaches (warping-based alignment, self-supervised contrastive learning, general domain generalization) fail to adequately address this structural temporal gap, leaving a critical challenge for real-world deployment where target domain data is unavailable.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D skeleton sequences uniformly resized to 64×25×3 (T frames × J joints × 3 coordinates) with camera rotation and trajectory removed, plus action labels during training. It produces two outputs: (1) standard action class predictions via a modified AGCN backbone, and (2) augmented skeleton sequences generated by the recover-and-resample pipeline. The augmentation framework additionally learns and stores a small set of boundary pose clusters (10 poses of shape J×3) and linear transform matrices (20 matrices of shape T×T) via k-means clustering, which are the only new parameters (~90K) added to the system.

## Method

The core contribution is a recover-and-resample augmentation framework built on a novel 'complete action prior' — the observation that per-frame latent feature diversity in large datasets increases from low (rest poses) to high (action poses) over time, implying most samples are partial segments of complete actions. The recovering stage is a two-step stochastic completion: first, a nonlinear transform F_N extrapolates the sequence by assigning a clustered boundary pose (from k-means on first frames) and infilling new frames via linear interpolation with a Beta-distributed length; second, a linear transform F_L reorganizes existing frames using context-aware smooth linear transforms learned by clustering similarity matrices from partial-to-full sequence pairs, capturing global patterns like shifting, scaling, and symmetry. The resampling stage then randomly crops a segment from the recovered complete action. Training uses standard cross-entropy on both original and augmented samples (75% augmentation ratio) with a lightweight 4-block AGCN backbone, requiring no auxiliary networks or pre-training.

## Results

On the proposed four cross-dataset settings (N→E, N→P, E_A→N, E_A→P) with 18 shared action classes, the method achieves an average balanced accuracy of 60.1%, improving over the ERM baseline (54.4%) by 5.7% and outperforming the strongest baseline ST-Cubism (57.1%) by 3.0%. The largest gains appear in the N→P setting (75.8% vs. 71.7% for ST-Cubism) and E_A→P (57.8% vs. 52.4%), where temporal mismatch is most pronounced. In the 51-class N51→P51 setting, the method reaches 72.2% accuracy, surpassing ST-Cubism (70.5%) and Skeleton-MAE (70.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all 13 baselines on average accuracy across the four cross-dataset settings. Against the closest competitor ST-Cubism [46] (57.1%), the gain is +3.0% on average, with the largest margin in E_A→P (+5.4%) and N→P (+4.1%); however, ST-Cubism slightly edges out the proposed method in the P51→N51 setting (53.8% vs. 53.3%) with the HCN backbone, which the authors attribute to PKU already containing mostly complete actions. Self-supervised methods Skeleton-MAE [28] (56.4%) and HICLR [62] (56.3%) trail by 3.7–3.8%, confirming that handcrafted or contrastive augmentations are less flexible than data-learned transforms. Among augmentation baselines, CropResize [47] (56.0%) and Uniform sampling [8] (55.7%) are the strongest, yet both fall short by 4.1–4.4% because they only resample observed frames without recovering missing temporal context. Alignment-based OTAM+kNN [3] (55.2%) and aggregation-based TSN [54] (54.0%) / Multiple-crop (56.1%) are outperformed by 4.0–4.9%, supporting the paper's argument that warping-based matching is suboptimal when sufficient training data is available. General domain generalization methods CCSA [35] (55.9%) and ADA [52] (54.7%) show only marginal gains over ERM, underscoring that generic feature alignment or adversarial perturbation does not address the temporal mismatch specific to skeleton sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the skeleton-based action recognition literature (AGCN, ST-GCN, CTR-GCN backbones) and the domain generalization problem, but departs from both handcrafted temporal augmentations (CropPad, CropResize, Mixup) and self-supervised auxiliary-task approaches (ST-Cubism, HICLR) by proposing a data-driven recover-and-resample framework grounded in a novel 'complete action prior.' The prior—that human actions in large datasets exhibit a statistical pattern of low per-frame diversity at boundaries (rest poses) transitioning to high diversity mid-sequence—is, to the authors' knowledge, the first time action completeness has been explicitly exploited for skeleton-based domain generalization. The method also contrasts with warping-based temporal alignment (DTW, OTAM) used in few-shot settings, arguing that learned linear transforms via clustering are more appropriate when sufficient training data is available.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Cross-dataset generalization is a practical bottleneck for deploying skeleton action recognition in real-world settings where target data is unavailable, and this paper provides a lightweight (clustering-based, ~90K new parameters) augmentation strategy that addresses the temporal mismatch between datasets without requiring target-domain knowledge. The complete action prior offers a reusable statistical lens for understanding how human motion data is structured across datasets, potentially informing future augmentation and representation learning designs. Key limitations include reliance on clean skeleton data and daily-routine action patterns (failing on noisy sources like Kinetics-12), and the resampling stage can introduce semantic ambiguity for reversible actions (e.g., standing up vs. sitting down), which the authors flag for future work via positional encoding.
