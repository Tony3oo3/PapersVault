## Problem

Existing physical adversarial patch attacks against person detection assume a fixed, known camera system and ignore the physical-to-digital transition performed by the camera's Image Signal Processing (ISP) pipeline. This oversight causes attack performance to vary dramatically across different imaging hardware, making attacks unstable and difficult to reproduce in real-world scenarios where the target camera is unknown.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240259/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes source images (INRIAPerson/COCO), a pre-trained YOLOv5 person detector (fine-tuned on INRIAPerson, processing 640×640 inputs), and a trained differentiable U-Net-based ISP proxy network as inputs. The ISP proxy consumes scene measurements plus six normalized conditional hyperparameters (from Color/Tone Correction and Denoising modules) concatenated to encoder features. The output is a 300×300 adversarial patch designed to remain effective when physically deployed and captured by any of six unseen cameras (two DSLRs and four smartphones).

## Method

The core contribution is a GAN-style adversarial optimization framework that treats the camera ISP as a natural defender. A differentiable U-Net ISP proxy network (trained on 2,270 pairs from the fast-openISP simulator and COCO) simulates arbitrary ISP configurations via six conditional hyperparameters. In the attacker phase, the adversarial patch P is optimized to maximize the detector's loss (Eq. 1) while ISP parameters are frozen; in the defender phase, the ISP conditional parameters Θ are optimized to minimize the same loss (Eq. 2) while the patch is frozen. The two modules alternate with k₁=k₂=20 update steps per cycle over 100 epochs, and NPS loss handles the digital-to-physical printing transition. This zero-sum game forces the patch to remain effective across the full range of ISP configurations, yielding cross-camera robustness without requiring knowledge of the target camera.

## Results

On the INRIAPerson dataset with YOLOv5 as the victim detector, CAP achieves the highest Attack Success Rate (ASR) across all five camera ISP settings in digital space, ranging from 54.4% (Original) to 73.8% (ISP2), consistently outperforming all seven baselines. In physical space, CAP achieves over 90% ASR on all six tested cameras (Sony, Canon, iPhone, Redmi, Huawei, Samsung), reaching 100% ASR on iPhone and Huawei. The method also demonstrates superior stability: while baselines like AdvPatch fluctuate between 0% and 35% ASR across cameras, CAP maintains consistent high ASR regardless of imaging hardware.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAP outperforms all seven baselines on ASR across every ISP setting. Against the strongest digital-space competitor T-SEA [15], CAP leads by 9.9–21.1 percentage points in ASR (e.g., 73.8% vs. 52.7% on ISP2), though T-SEA achieves lower AP due to a multi-box false-positive artifact rather than genuine person concealment. Against AdvPatch [29], the most widely cited physical attack, CAP's ASR advantage ranges from 34.7 pp (Original: 54.4% vs. 19.7%) to 36.2 pp (ISP2: 73.8% vs. 30.4%). The stealthiness-oriented methods NAP [11] and LAP [28] are the weakest, with ASR below 17% in all settings, trailing CAP by over 37 pp. In physical space, AdvPatch [29] achieves 35% ASR on Canon but 0% on iPhone, while T-SEA [15] largely fails across all six cameras; CAP maintains >90% ASR uniformly. AdvCloak [34] and AdvT-shirt [35] show moderate but inconsistent digital-space ASR (12–30%), and TC-EGA [14] remains below 21% ASR in all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established line of physical adversarial patch attacks on person detection (AdvPatch, AdvT-shirt, AdvCloak, NAP, LAP, TC-EGA, T-SEA) by identifying and modeling a previously overlooked domain transition: the physical-to-digital conversion performed by the camera's Image Signal Processing (ISP) pipeline. While prior work addressed the digital-to-physical transition (via NPS loss, color transformers) and physical-domain transformations (TPS deformation, scale/angle augmentation), none systematically modeled the camera as a black-box imaging device. The method repurposes the differentiable ISP simulation literature (DeepISP, hyperparameter-optimized ISP proxies) and the observation from Zhang et al. (2022) that camera ISP attenuates adversarial perturbations, embedding the ISP proxy as a GAN-style 'defender' in an adversarial optimization loop with the patch generator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a methodological gap in the physical adversarial attack literature: the assumption of a fixed, known camera pipeline undermines reproducibility and real-world transferability of attacks. By demonstrating that existing patches (especially T-SEA) degrade or fail entirely across different camera hardware, the work reframes cross-camera stability as a first-class design objective. Limitations include the ISP proxy being trained on a software simulator (fast-openISP) rather than real camera RAW data, only six ISP hyperparameters being modeled, and evaluation restricted to person detection with YOLOv5. Open questions include whether a full end-to-end differentiable camera model (exposure, aperture, ISO) would further improve transfer, and whether the adversarial ISP-as-defender paradigm generalizes to other detection or recognition tasks.
