## Problem

Neural PDE solvers trained with classical empirical risk minimization fail to generalize across varying PDE parameter configurations (coefficients, forcing terms, domains), particularly when data is scarce. While adaptive conditioning mechanisms can address this, existing meta-learning approaches (gradient-based inner loops, hypernetworks) do not scale effectively with the number of environments or the dimensionality of the adaptation parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242257/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The solver takes a spatio-temporal field state (1D or 2D grid) as input—either a single initial condition or a short history of past states—and autoregressively rolls out the full trajectory over a temporal horizon using a Neural ODE time-stepper. For out-of-distribution adaptation, a single context trajectory from the new environment is consumed to optimize the compact context vector before prediction. In the hybrid physics-aware setting, the output additionally includes estimated PDE coefficients for the known physical component.

## Method

GEPS introduces a low-rank adaptation rule ΔW_{L_i} = A_{L_i} diag(c^e) B_{L_i} applied to each layer of the neural solver, where a single compact context vector c^e (shared across all layers) encodes environment-specific information while A, B, and base weights W remain shared. Training is a first-order meta-learning procedure with no inner loop: shared parameters and per-environment context vectors are jointly optimized via standard gradient descent. At inference, all shared parameters are frozen and only c^e is optimized on one trajectory from the new environment, initialized to the training-average context. The framework is instantiated in two forms: a purely data-driven solver (MLP, CNN, or FNO backbone) and a physics-aware hybrid that composes a differentiable PDE solver for the known physics with the data-driven component for unknown terms, with PDE coefficients also adapted via the context vector.

## Results

On the data-driven setting (Table 2), GEPS achieves the best or near-best relative L2 loss across all four PDEs: 2.22e-2 (In-d) / 1.86e-2 (Out-d) on Gray-Scott, 2.59e-3 (In-d) / 52.9e-3 (Out-d) on Burgers, and 2.94e-1 (In-d) / 5.10e-1 (Out-d) on Kolmogorov flow. In the hybrid physics-aware setting, GEPS-Phy dramatically outperforms APHYNITY on Pendulum (8.04e-2 vs 67.2e-2 In-d) and Burgers (5.43e-3 vs 31.9e-3 In-d), though APHYNITY remains best on Gray-Scott (0.14e-2 vs 0.67e-2 In-d). On the large-scale Combined equation (Table 3), GEPS achieves 7.52e-3 (In-d) / 8.29e-2 (Out-d) with only 4M parameters, versus CoDA's 9.32e-2 / 1.78e-1 with 35M parameters — a 12× parameter reduction with ~12× lower In-d loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among adaptation-based baselines, GEPS outperforms CoDA (the SOTA hypernetwork method) on Burgers (2.59e-3 vs 4.98e-3 In-d; 52.9e-3 vs 74.5e-3 Out-d) and Kolmogorov (2.94e-1 vs 4.02e-1 In-d; 5.10e-1 vs 6.34e-1 Out-d), while matching or slightly beating it on Gray-Scott and Pendulum. GEPS substantially outperforms gradient-based meta-learning methods CAVIA and FOCA, which suffer from high variance and poor Out-d performance (e.g., CAVIA reaches 225e-3 on Burgers Out-d vs GEPS's 52.9e-3). LEADS, a multi-task method requiring one model per environment, is matched or slightly beaten by GEPS on most datasets. In the hybrid setting, GEPS-Phy dominates APHYNITY on Pendulum (8.04e-2 vs 67.2e-2 In-d) and Burgers (5.43e-3 vs 31.9e-3 In-d), though APHYNITY retains an edge on Gray-Scott (0.14e-2 vs 0.67e-2 In-d). In the ERM comparison (Section 3), GEPS outperforms Transolver, FNO, MP-PDE, and the Poseidon foundation model across all tested configurations, with the gap widening for Out-d generalization where non-adaptive methods fail to capture new dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

GEPS sits at the intersection of meta-learning for dynamical systems (CAVIA, FOCA, CoDA, LEADS) and low-rank adaptation from NLP (LoRA), but departs from both in key ways. Unlike LoRA, GEPS learns all parameters jointly from scratch rather than adapting a frozen pre-trained model; unlike CoDA's hypernetwork-based adaptation, GEPS uses a single shared low-rank context vector across all layers, avoiding the parameter explosion of hypernetworks. The paper also extends the hybrid physics-informed line (APHYNITY) by jointly adapting both the PDE coefficients and the neural correction module through the same context vector, and makes the stronger claim that adaptive conditioning is *necessary* (not merely beneficial) for parametric PDE solvers, in contrast to the ERM paradigm that dominates the field (FNO, MP-PDE, Transolver, Poseidon).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear empirical argument that the standard ERM recipe—train one model on many sampled PDE instances—fundamentally fails for initial-value-problem forecasting, even with foundation models like Poseidon, and that a minimal low-rank context vector (4M params) can outperform a 35M-parameter hypernetwork (CoDA) on a 1200-environment benchmark. This reframes how practitioners should approach parametric PDE solvers: the bottleneck is not model capacity or data volume but the absence of an explicit per-environment adaptation mechanism. Open questions remain about whether the approach scales to genuinely complex, high-dimensional, or 3D real-world dynamics (the authors acknowledge the tested PDEs are relatively simple), and whether one-shot adaptation suffices for systems with high intra-environment variability (e.g., Kolmogorov flow).
