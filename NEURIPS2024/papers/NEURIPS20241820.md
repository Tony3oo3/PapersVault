## Problem

Graph transformers lack strong inductive biases for capturing multi-level hierarchical structures, community structures, and long-range dependencies that are prevalent in molecular graphs, social networks, and citation networks. Existing positional encodings such as shortest path distance (SPD) are insufficient to distinguish nodes that share the same pairwise distance but differ in their hierarchical context, and the quadratic cost of global attention prevents scaling to graphs with millions or billions of nodes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241820/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G=(V,E,X) with node features and a pre-computed graph hierarchy (a sequence of coarsened graphs G^0,...,G^K produced by algorithms like METIS, Louvain, or Newman). For each node pair (i,j), it constructs a (K+1)-dimensional vector of hierarchy distances, clipped to a maximum length L (default 30). For large-scale node classification, the high-level HDSE variant computes distances between all input nodes and the |V^c| clusters at level c, yielding a |V^0|×|V^c| bias matrix that replaces the standard |V^0|×|V^0| attention bias, enabling linear-complexity attention.

## Method

HDSE defines a Graph Hierarchy Distance (GHD) at each coarsening level k as the shortest-path distance between the images of two nodes under the composition of coarsening maps φ_{k-1}∘...∘φ_0, and concatenates GHD^0 through GHD^K into a per-pair encoding vector. This vector is fed through a small MLP with learnable distance embeddings (one per level, indexed by the clipped integer distance) to produce a scalar bias H_{i,j} that is added directly to the attention logits, making the integration backbone-agnostic and composable with other positional encodings. For scalability, the high-level HDSE substitutes the random projection matrix in linear transformers (GOAT/Linformer) with the coarsening partition matrix P, so that attention is computed between all input nodes and a small set of super-nodes at level c, with the bias computed from distances at levels c through K. Theoretically, the paper proves that GD-WL with HDSE is strictly more expressive than GD-WL with SPD (distinguishing the Dodecahedral and Desargues graphs), that graph transformers with HDSE match GD-WL expressiveness, and that a one-layer transformer with HDSE achieves generalization guarantees on hierarchical core neighborhoods where SPD-based or unencoded transformers cannot.

## Results

On graph-level tasks, integrating HDSE into GT yields the largest gains: ZINC MAE drops from 0.226 to 0.159, MNIST accuracy rises from 90.8% to 94.4%, and CIFAR-10 accuracy from 59.8% to 64.7%. GraphGPS+HDSE achieves 76.2% on CIFAR-10 (vs. 72.3% baseline) and 0.062 MAE on ZINC (vs. 0.070). On the Long-Range Graph Benchmark, GraphGPS+HDSE reaches 0.7156 AP on Peptides-func, a 6.21% improvement over GraphGPS and matching the best baseline DRew (0.7150). On 11 large-scale node classification datasets (up to 111M nodes), GOAT+HDSE consistently outperforms GOAT, e.g., 83.4% on ogbn-products (vs. 82.0%) and 66.04% on ogbn-papers100M (vs. 65.05%), while matching or exceeding the fastest baseline SGFormer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HDSE consistently improves every transformer backbone it is integrated into: GT+HDSE beats GT on all five graph-level benchmarks (e.g., ZINC MAE 0.159 vs. 0.226, a 29.6% reduction), and GraphGPS+HDSE surpasses GraphGPS on four of five datasets (CIFAR-10: 76.2% vs. 72.3%). On Peptides-func, GraphGPS+HDSE (0.7156 AP) edges out the strongest baseline DRew (0.7150) and substantially outperforms GRIT (0.6988) and Exphormer (0.6527). For large-scale node classification, GOAT+HDSE dominates GOAT across all 11 datasets (e.g., ogbn-products: 83.38% vs. 82.00%; ogbn-papers100M: 66.04% vs. 65.05%) and matches or exceeds SGFormer, the fastest scalable transformer (ogbn-products: 83.38% vs. 75.36%; ogbn-100M: 66.04% vs. 66.01%). On the synthetic Community-small dataset, GT+HDSE (88.6%) dramatically outperforms GT+SPD (81.5%) and vanilla GT (64.7%), confirming that hierarchical distance captures community structure that shortest-path distance cannot. GRIT+HDSE shows the smallest gains (only MNIST improves), which the authors attribute to GRIT's relative random walk probabilities already encoding distance information.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the biased self-attention framework of Graphormer [86] to inject structural information into graph transformers, but replaces the commonly used shortest-path or Laplacian-based positional encodings with a multi-level hierarchical distance (GHD) derived from graph coarsening algorithms (METIS, Louvain, Newman, etc.). For large-scale graphs, it extends the linear-attention architecture of GOAT [45] by substituting its learned projection matrix with coarsening partition matrices, enabling high-level HDSE at linear complexity. The generalization guarantee (Proposition 3) is a direct extension of the SPD-based analysis in Li et al. [53], generalized from one-dimensional shortest-path neighborhoods to multi-dimensional hierarchical core neighborhoods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HDSE provides a theoretically grounded (strictly more expressive than SPD under GD-WL, with generalization bounds) and practically simple drop-in module that consistently improves diverse graph transformer backbones, including state-of-the-art models like GraphGPS and GRIT, across 18 datasets. The ability to scale to billion-node graphs (ogbn-papers100M) with linear complexity while retaining hierarchical structural bias is practically significant for real-world networks. Open questions include: the optimal coarsening algorithm remains dataset-dependent (Newman for small molecular graphs, METIS for large-scale), higher hierarchy levels (K≥2) show only marginal gains suggesting current coarsening methods may be insufficient, and whether the approach transfers to other structural encodings beyond distance-based ones.
