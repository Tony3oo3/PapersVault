## Problem

Most time series models assume all variables are continuous, but real-world data frequently contains mixed time series (MiTS) with both continuous variables (CVs) and discrete variables (DVs). This creates a spatial-temporal heterogeneity problem: DVs exhibit sudden changes or steady states with Bernoulli distributions, while CVs show smooth autocorrelated patterns with Gaussian distributions, leading to insufficient and imbalanced representation learning when both are treated uniformly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240569/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multivariate mixed time series where half the variables are binary discrete (0/1, generated by MinMax-normalizing and thresholding at 0.5) and half are continuous, with sequence lengths ranging from 24 to 1751 timesteps and up to 963 variables. It produces task-specific outputs: class labels (classification), scalar regression values (extrinsic regression), per-timestep anomaly scores (anomaly detection), imputed CV values at masked positions (imputation), or future CV and DV sequences (long-term forecasting up to 720 steps). A key design choice is that the model internally recovers latent continuous variables (LCVs) from DVs before any downstream processing, so all spatial-temporal modeling occurs in a unified continuous space.

## Method

MiTSformer addresses spatial-temporal heterogeneity through two synergistic inductive biases. First, a Latent Continuity Recovery network (residual dilated 1D convolutions with increasing dilation rates) hierarchically aggregates multi-scale temporal context from DVs to produce LCVs, guided by an adversarial variable-modality discriminator (trained with a gradient reversal layer to make LCVs indistinguishable from CVs) and a temporal smoothness constraint that penalizes LCV changes at DV transition points. Second, cascaded spatial-temporal attention blocks model dependencies: intra-modality self-attention captures correlations within CVs or within LCVs, while symmetric inter-modality cross-attention (CV queries attending to LCV keys/values and vice versa) bridges information across the two modalities. The overall training objective combines self-supervision (smoothness + reconstruction + adversarial discrimination losses) with task-specific supervision, enabling a single architecture to handle five distinct analysis tasks by swapping only the task head.

## Results

MiTSformer achieves state-of-the-art performance across all five mixed time series analysis tasks. On classification (10 UEA datasets), it reaches 71.9% average accuracy, outperforming the strongest baselines ModernTCN (70.1%) and iTransformer (68.9%). On long-term forecasting (10 datasets, 96–720 horizons), it achieves the best MAE on 76% of tasks, with notable margins on ETTh2 (0.430 vs. 0.442 for iTransformer) and ILI (0.779 vs. 0.995 for iTransformer). On anomaly detection, it attains the highest F1-score on all five benchmarks (e.g., 87.84 on SMD, 96.83 on PSM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MiTSformer consistently outperforms all 11 time-series baselines (CNN, Transformer, and MLP families) on average across all five tasks. In classification, it leads by 1.8 points over ModernTCN (71.9% vs. 70.1%) and by 3.0 points over iTransformer (71.9% vs. 68.9%), with the largest margins on Handwriting (+4.4 over ModernTCN) and PEMS-SF (+5.2 over iTransformer). In long-term forecasting, it achieves the best MAE on 76% of dataset-horizon combinations, with particularly large gains on ETTh2 (0.430 vs. 0.442 for iTransformer) and ILI (0.779 vs. 0.995 for iTransformer), while ModernTCN and iTransformer are the closest competitors on ETTm1 and Weather. For anomaly detection, MiTSformer attains the highest F1 on all five benchmarks (SMD 87.84, MSL 85.30, SMAP 96.83, SWaT 96.15, PSM 96.83), with iTransformer as the second-best. In imputation, MiTSformer achieves the lowest MAE on 5 of 6 datasets (e.g., 0.116 on ETTm2 vs. 0.145 for PatchTST), though ModernTCN edges it out slightly on ETTm1 (0.135 vs. 0.156). The mixed-data-specific baselines HVM and VAMDA (Appendix E.1) are substantially outperformed, e.g., 94.6% vs. 86.4% on JapaneseVowels, confirming that task-general time-series backbones without modality-aware design are inadequate for MiTS.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formulating general mixed time series analysis as a unified problem, departing from two existing lines of work: (1) general time series frameworks (TimesNet, iTransformer, ModernTCN, PatchTST) that assume all variables are continuous and model spatial-temporal patterns uniformly, and (2) mixed data methods (Mv.RBM, mixed naive Bayes, variational inference) that handle heterogeneous variables but are restricted to specific tasks or non-temporal tabular data. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of latent continuous variables (LCVs) as an intermediate representation behind discrete variables, recovered via dilated convolutions and aligned to continuous variables through adversarial variable-modality discrimination. The spatial-temporal modeling then proceeds in a unified continuous space using cascaded self- and cross-attention, repurposing standard Transformer components for the mixed-modality setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Mixed time series (combining continuous and discrete variables) are ubiquitous in meteorology, industrial monitoring, and healthcare, yet prior methods either discard discrete variables or treat all variables uniformly, leading to imbalanced representation learning. The LCV recovery framework provides a principled and task-general solution that could be adopted whenever discrete sensors coexist with continuous ones. Key limitations include the assumption that discrete variables originate from latent continuous factors (inapplicable to categorical variables like color or gender), the focus on binary DVs in main experiments, and the artificial creation of mixed data by discretizing continuous benchmarks. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-state or categorical discrete variables, whether pre-training and LLM-based architectures can further enhance scalability, and whether the LCV concept generalizes beyond the adversarial alignment strategy used here.
