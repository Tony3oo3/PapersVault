## Problem

Previous representational alignment studies have focused on static tasks such as object recognition and pairwise similarity judgments, which do not capture how humans use their representations to continuously generalise over novel stimuli. This paper asks what determines whether a neural network's internal representations support the same kind of few-shot, continuous generalisation that humans demonstrate when learning functional relationships over naturalistic images, and which model properties drive alignment in such cognitively demanding settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243889/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unique naturalistic images from the THINGS database (no image is repeated across trials, forcing continuous generalisation) and extracts representation vectors from 86 pretrained networks—penultimate-layer activations for classifiers, [CLS] tokens for transformers, and category-label embeddings for language models. These vectors feed into incrementally-trained linear probes (ℓ₂-regularised logistic regression for the 120-trial category task, Bayesian linear regression with spherical Gaussian priors for the 60-trial reward task), whose per-trial predictions are then regressed onto human choices via mixed-effects logistic regression in leave-one-trial-out cross-validation, with alignment summarised as McFadden's R².

## Method

The core contribution is a systematic evaluation of 86 pretrained representations (supervised CNNs/ViTs, self-supervised DINO/SimCLR/MoCo/SwAV/VICReg/Barlow Twins, multimodal CLIP variants, and language models including BERT, RoBERTa, GPT-3, fastText) on two novel learning paradigms—category learning and reward learning—where the task rule is defined over a single THINGS embedding dimension and every stimulus is unique. For each trial, a linear probe is retrained from scratch on all previously observed image-target pairs, and its output is used as a predictor in mixed-effects logistic regression to model participant choices, enabling a trial-by-trial comparison of model and human learning trajectories. The paper then identifies which model properties (parameter count, training data volume, class-separation R², linear CKA similarity to the generative task embedding, and TwoNN intrinsic dimensionality) predict alignment, and runs a controlled comparison using Mu et al.'s models trained on identical YFCC15M data with CLIP, SimCLR, or combined losses to isolate the effect of the contrastive multimodal objective from dataset size. Finally, three purpose-built human-alignment methods (Harmonization, DreamSim, gLocal) are tested to determine whether explicit alignment transfers to these continuous learning tasks.

## Results

Across 86 pretrained neural network representations tested on two naturalistic few-shot learning tasks (category learning, n=91; reward learning, n=82), CLIP models occupied the top 7 (category) and top 6 (reward) positions in McFadden's R² alignment with human choices. In total, 16 (7) of 86 representations outperformed the ground-truth THINGS embedding used to generate the tasks, and 14 (6) of those were CLIP variants. Key factors driving alignment were similarity to the generative task features (CKA, ρ=0.67, p<.001), training dataset size (ρ=0.52, p<.001), model size (ρ=0.48, p<.001), class separation (ρ=0.29, p=.01), and lower intrinsic dimensionality (ρ=−0.32, p=.03, strongest for multimodal models). In a controlled comparison on YFCC15M, CLIP loss consistently outperformed SimCLR loss in alignment even when architecture and data size were held constant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP models (Radford et al. [45], OpenCLIP [54], Cherti et al. [55]) dominate the alignment rankings, occupying the top 7 of 86 models for category learning and top 6 for reward learning, substantially outperforming supervised (ResNet [32], ViT [31], Swin [33], ConvNeXt [34]), self-supervised (DINO [36], SwAV [38], SimCLR [39], MoCo [37], VICReg [40], Barlow Twins [41]), and language models (BERT [47], RoBERTa [49], GPT-3 [50], fastText [51]). In the controlled YFCC15M comparison (Mu et al. [52]), CLIP loss beat SimCLR loss in alignment and task accuracy despite SimCLR having higher class separation, better CKA with the task embedding, and lower intrinsic dimensionality—properties that individually predicted better alignment in the broader analysis. Among explicit human-alignment methods, DreamSim (Fu et al. [12]) actually *hurt* alignment in both tasks, while gLocal (Muttenthaler et al. [10]) and Harmonization (Fel et al. [11]) showed no consistent improvement in McFadden's R², though both improved task accuracy on average. A human-aligned DINOv2 variant (Oquab et al. [53]) was the only non-CLIP model to beat the ground-truth task embedding in category learning. Compared to prior alignment benchmarks, the tasks correlate most strongly with THINGS odd-one-out judgements (ρ=0.54 zero-shot, ρ=0.61 probing; Muttenthaler et al. [3]), moderately with Peterson et al. [23] similarity judgements (ρ=0.35), show no correlation with a fine-grained 2AFC task, and exhibit a strong *negative* correlation with ClickMe pixel-level alignment (ρ=−0.48), suggesting global semantic alignment and localised pixel alignment are in tension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representational alignment literature (Muttenhaler et al. 2023, Conwell et al. 2023, Sucholutsky & Griffiths 2023) from static similarity judgements and object recognition to active learning trajectories, where humans and networks must generalise across unique naturalistic images without repetition. It builds on the THINGS database and its 49-dimensional human-interpretable embedding (Hebart et al. 2020) to define task structure, and adapts established cognitive psychology paradigms (category learning, reward learning) to naturalistic stimuli. The modelling approach draws on linear probing with Bayesian and regularised regression, and the intrinsic dimensionality analysis uses the TwoNN method (Facco et al. 2017), positioning the work at the intersection of alignment research, cognitive modelling, and representation geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a new, cognitively richer benchmark for measuring alignment that captures generalisation and information integration over time rather than single-trial similarity, and demonstrates that off-the-shelf pretrained networks can serve as representations for cognitive models in naturalistic domains. The finding that explicit alignment methods (DreamSim, gLocal, Harmonization) do not consistently improve alignment in these learning tasks is a practically important negative result, suggesting that alignment is task-dependent and that methods optimised for similarity judgements may not transfer. Open questions include whether findings generalise to nonlinear task rules, other paradigms beyond the THINGS embedding, and whether behavioural alignment observed here corresponds to neural alignment (the authors note MRI replication as a key next step).
