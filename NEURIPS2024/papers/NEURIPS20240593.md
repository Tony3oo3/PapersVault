## Problem

Large multimodal models have demonstrated strong performance across many domains, but their ability to interpret scientific figures—a dense, compressed source of complex information central to research—remains poorly characterised. No established benchmark exists for quantitatively evaluating this capability, and constructing one is difficult because scientific figures lack obvious objective evaluation metrics and manual annotation at scale requires domain expertise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240593/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark presents 2000 five-way multiple-choice questions in two complementary tasks: Figure→Caption (one scientific figure image plus five caption options, select the matching caption) and Caption→Figure (one caption plus five figure images, select the matching figure). Negatives are adversarially selected via joint CLIP embedding nearest-neighbours rather than random sampling, making them semantically close to the correct answer. Because most LMMs produce noisy, verbose outputs that do not consistently follow the requested single-letter format, a Gemini-Pro parser extracts the answer letter from raw model text, enabling fully automatic evaluation at scale.

## Method

SciFIBench is curated from figure-caption pairs in the SciCap (CS papers, 2010–2020) and ArXivCap (32 arXiv domains, up to 2023) datasets through a multi-stage pipeline: joint CLIP embeddings are computed and indexed in a Faiss vector database, k nearest neighbours are retrieved per sample to form adversarial negatives, near-duplicate pairs are filtered by an L2 distance threshold, questions are categorised by arXiv subject and sorted by difficulty (mean embedding distance of negatives), and every included question undergoes human verification for answerability. The final 2000 questions are split into a CS subset (1000 questions, 1 category) and a General subset (1000 questions, 7 categories), with category-balanced downsampling of questions that GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Pro answered incorrectly. The paper evaluates 28 LMMs, three VLM baselines (CLIP, MetaCLIP, Google Multimodal Embedding), and a human baseline, and additionally probes alignment and reasoning faithfulness by augmenting questions with a <Correct> annotation on either the true or a random incorrect option and measuring how models respond to the instruction to ignore it.

## Results

On SciFIBench (adversarial negatives), GPT-4o achieves the highest overall accuracy at 73.8% (Figure→Caption) and 65.4% (Caption→Figure), while Gemini 1.5 Flash follows closely at 70.1% and 66.1%. The human baseline substantially outperforms all models, scoring 86.4% and 78.4% on the two tasks respectively. Among open-source LMMs, TransCore-M leads at 39.2% (Figure→Caption), still trailing the weakest closed-source model (Claude 3 Haiku at 52.6%) by over 13 points. The CLIP VLM baseline (36.2%/36.3%) remains competitive with the leading open-source LMMs on adversarial negatives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Closed-source LMMs dominate open-source models by a wide margin: GPT-4o (73.8%) outperforms the best open-source model TransCore-M (39.2%) by 34.6 points on Figure→Caption, and the gap is even larger on Caption→Figure. Among closed-source models, GPT-4o and Gemini 1.5 Flash/Pro form a clear first tier (69–75%), while Claude 3 variants and Gemini Pro Vision trail by 10–20 points. All LMMs are outperformed by the human baseline (86.4%/78.4%), confirming SciFIBench remains challenging even for frontier models. The CLIP VLM baseline (36.2%/36.3%) is competitive with mid-tier open-source LMMs on adversarial negatives but is far surpassed by closed-source LMMs; on random negatives, CLIP nearly matches GPT-4V, highlighting that adversarial filtering is what makes the benchmark difficult. The Caption→Figure task is consistently harder than Figure→Caption across all model families, with the human baseline showing a larger drop (78.4% vs. 86.4%) than most LMMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of scientific figure understanding (SciCap, SciMMIR, ChartQA, PlotQA) and LMM benchmarking (MMMU, MMBench, MathVista), but carves new ground by reframing scientific figure interpretation as a multiple-choice matching task evaluated specifically on LMMs. Methodologically, it borrows the adversarial filtering strategy from HellaSwag/MMMU for constructing hard negatives via joint-embedding nearest neighbours (using CLIP and Faiss), and sources figure-caption pairs from SciCap and ArXivCap. The key departure from prior scientific figure work is the dual-directional formulation (figure→caption and caption→figure) combined with human-verified answerability, targeting the LMM evaluation gap the authors identify as 'yet to be reported.'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SciFIBench provides the first systematic quantitative evaluation of LMMs on scientific figure interpretation, revealing that even frontier models (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5) achieve only ~70-75% accuracy versus ~86% for humans, highlighting a substantial gap for AI-assisted scientific research. The finding that CLIP remains competitive with or superior to leading open-source LMMs on adversarial negatives challenges the assumption that generative LMMs should dominate matching tasks, and the alignment experiments expose that models can be significantly 'cheated' by explicit answer annotations. Limitations include the small scale (2000 questions), restriction to arXiv papers, and the multiple-choice format which may not capture open-ended scientific reasoning; open questions include whether performance gaps close with better scientific-domain pretraining and how faithfulness issues affect real research workflows.
