## Problem

Plasticity loss—the degradation of a neural network's ability to fit new data as training progresses—has been well characterized in supervised learning and off-policy RL, but remains poorly understood in the on-policy deep RL setting. This paper asks whether previously proposed mitigation methods transfer to on-policy RL and, if not, what properties an intervention must have to be effective under the non-i.i.d. data conditions inherent to on-policy training.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes environment observations in two formats: an 11×11×4 one-hot encoded gridworld state (flattened to a vector for an MLP encoder) or 64×64×3 RGB frames (processed by a 4-layer convolutional encoder for CoinRun and Montezuma's Revenge). The policy outputs a discrete action—four cardinal directions in the gridworld or nine standard ProcGen actions. Experiments are structured into ten rounds of k=100 environments, with one of three distribution-shift modifications (permute, window, expand) applied between rounds to stress-test plasticity.

## Method

This is an empirical study that systematically evaluates nine intervention methods (intermittent: reset-final, shrink+perturb, plasticity injection, ReDo; continuous: L2 norm, LayerNorm, CReLU, regenerative regularization, soft shrink+perturb) against warm-start and reset-all baselines using PPO across gridworld, CoinRun, and Montezuma's Revenge. The key finding is that architectural modifications (CReLU, plasticity injection) and intermittent resets fail in the on-policy regime, while continuous regularization methods that pull weights toward their initialization distribution consistently mitigate plasticity loss. Correlation and GLM analyses identify weight magnitude and dead ReLU unit count as the primary predictors of both training and generalization degradation, suggesting that successful interventions must induce sub-linear growth in parameter norm. The best-performing combination is soft shrink+perturb (applying x_new = αx_current + βx_init at every gradient step) combined with LayerNorm, which addresses both training plasticity loss and generalization across all tested environments.

## Results

In the gridworld permute condition, warm-start shows significant plasticity loss relative to reset-all (p=0.04 train, p=0.02 test), confirming the phenomenon in on-policy RL. Among interventions, soft shrink+perturb+LN and regenerative regularization+LN are not significantly different from the reset-all baseline in both train (p=0.837, p=0.208) and test (p=0.82, p=0.445) distributions, effectively eliminating plasticity loss. In contrast, CReLU (p=0.0) and Plasticity Injection (p=0.007) perform significantly worse than reset-all on test performance in the permute condition. On Montezuma's Revenge, RND agents trained with soft shrink+perturb or regenerative regularization achieve higher cumulative reward than standard RND over 20 replicates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 9 intervention methods against warm-start and reset-all baselines across three distribution shift conditions (permute, window, expand) on Gridworld, CoinRun, Jumper, and FruitBot. The regenerative regularization class—soft shrink+perturb, regenerative regularization (L2), and LayerNorm—consistently resolves plasticity loss, with soft shrink+perturb+LN and regen reg+LN being statistically indistinguishable from the ideal reset-all baseline in most conditions. In contrast, architectural modifications (CReLU, Plasticity Injection) and intermittent interventions (Reset-final) fail: CReLU significantly underperforms reset-all in the permute condition (p=0.0), and Plasticity Injection underperforms even the warm-start baseline in all three shift conditions. LayerNorm alone resolves training plasticity loss but degrades generalization (p=0.0 on permute-test), though combining it with soft shrink+perturb or regenerative regularization overcomes this. On CoinRun, the same three methods (LayerNorm, regenerative regularization, soft shrink+perturb) are again the most effective, confirming consistency across environments. ReDo performs worst among the regularization methods, underperforming even warm-start in the expand condition because it discards positive transfer from prior rounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the plasticity loss literature, previously established in supervised continual learning and off-policy RL (Lyle et al., 2023; Nikishin et al., 2023; Abbas et al., 2023), to the on-policy RL regime using PPO. Rather than proposing a new method, it systematically re-evaluates a suite of existing interventions—CReLU, plasticity injection, shrink+perturb, ReDo, regenerative regularization, and LayerNorm—under three distinct distribution-shift conditions (permute, window, expand) that were not previously studied in on-policy settings. The key conceptual departure is showing that methods validated under the i.i.d. data assumptions of off-policy RL and supervised learning do not transfer to the non-i.i.d. experience stream of on-policy training, and that a 'regenerative' class of continuous regularizers (soft shrink+perturb, regenerative regularization, LayerNorm) consistently outperforms intermittent or architectural interventions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since PPO is the dominant on-policy algorithm in practice, identifying which plasticity-loss mitigations actually work (and which actively harm performance) provides directly actionable guidance for practitioners deploying continual or online RL agents. The finding that LayerNorm combined with soft shrink+perturb is a simple, architecture-agnostic fix that addresses both training plasticity and generalization degradation is practically significant. Open questions include whether the connection between weight magnitude, dead units, and optimization landscape curvature (noted but not resolved) can yield principled rather than heuristic interventions, whether findings generalize beyond PPO to other on-policy methods, and how plasticity loss interacts with the related but distinct phenomenon of policy collapse in long-horizon training.
