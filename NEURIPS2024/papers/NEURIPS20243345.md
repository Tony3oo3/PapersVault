## Problem

Standard LLM alignment via RLHF requires expensive human-written positive samples, yet in practice practitioners often only have negative samples (harmful outputs, copyrighted text, hallucinated answers) and need to quickly remove their impact without prohibitively costly retraining. Prior to this work, no LLM unlearning method, benchmark, or formal evaluation framework existed, and it was unclear whether unlearning could generalize beyond the exact unlearned samples to similar unseen prompts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes negative prompt-output pairs (e.g., a harmful prompt paired with its harmful response) that need not originate from the model's original training corpus, plus a normal dataset in the same format (e.g., Q&A pairs) for utility preservation. The output is an unlearned LLM that, when given the unlearned or similar prompts, generates non-undesirable text (nonsensical strings, whitespace, or templated refusals) while maintaining comparable quality on normal prompts. Unlike classification unlearning, the unlearned samples represent a general concept (harmfulness, copyright, hallucination) rather than exact training instances, requiring the method to generalize to paraphrased or unseen variants.

## Method

The core update rule performs gradient ascent on the forget samples with three weighted loss terms: (1) a GA loss computed only on the output tokens (not the prompt) to increase the model's loss on undesirable responses, (2) a random mismatch loss that forces the model to predict irrelevant random outputs on the unlearned prompts, and (3) a forward KL divergence loss on normal prompts to anchor the unlearned model's distribution to the original LLM. A key design choice is computing the loss exclusively on the output (y) conditioned on the prompt (x), which the authors find substantially more effective than computing it on the full (x,y) sequence. The random mismatch term stabilizes unlearning when the gradient on the forget samples becomes small, and continuing training 3–5× beyond the point where the forget loss spikes abnormally high is necessary for actual behavioral change. The approach is computationally comparable to standard fine-tuning, requiring only ~2% of the time of a full RLHF pipeline.

## Results

On OPT-1.3B with PKU-SafeRLHF data, GA achieves a 1% harmful rate on unlearned prompts and 3% on unseen harmful prompts, outperforming Full RLHF (4% and 7.5% respectively) while using only 2% of RLHF's computational time on a single A100 GPU. GA+Mismatch achieves 6% and 7% harmful rates on unlearned and unseen prompts respectively, with a utility reward of -2.982 on normal prompts (vs. -3.212 for Full RLHF). Across all three applications (harmfulness, copyright, hallucination), both GA and GA+Mismatch reduce the target misbehavior rate to near-zero (0–1% leak rate for copyright; ~10% hallucination rate) while preserving utility on normal prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its unlearning methods (GA and GA+Mismatch) against SFT and Full RLHF (Ouyang et al., NeurIPS 2022) on OPT-1.3B with PKU-SafeRLHF data. GA achieves a strictly lower harmful rate than Full RLHF on both unlearned (1% vs. 4%) and unseen (3% vs. 7.5%) harmful prompts, despite having access only to negative samples and no human-written positive examples. GA+Mismatch matches Full RLHF on unseen harmful prompts (7% vs. 7.5%) and achieves a better utility reward on normal prompts (-2.982 vs. -3.212), though its similarity to the original model is slightly lower (-0.943 vs. -0.834). The critical advantage is computational: unlearning requires only ~2% of the wall-clock time of the full RLHF pipeline (SFT + reward model + PPO) on a single A100-80GB GPU. SFT alone performs poorly, reducing the harmful rate only to 34% (unlearned) and 38% (unseen), confirming that standard fine-tuning on remaining data is insufficient for safety alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-ascent unlearning framework from Thudi et al. (EuroS&P 2022), originally designed for classification models, to the fundamentally different setting of large language models. It departs from prior unlearning work in three key ways: the output space is open-ended natural language rather than discrete labels, the training corpus is massive and inaccessible, and the goal shifts from matching a retrained model's behavior to simply deviating from undesirable outputs. The method also draws on RLHF practices (KL divergence for utility preservation) and label smoothing (random mismatch loss), but positions itself as a lightweight alternative to the full RLHF pipeline by requiring only negative samples rather than expensive human-written positive examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is among the first papers to formally define LLM unlearning—its settings, goals, and evaluation metrics—establishing a problem formulation that subsequent work builds upon. The practical implication is significant: when the immediate priority is stopping harmful or copyrighted outputs and only negative examples are available (e.g., from red teaming or user reports), unlearning offers a computationally cheap path (~2% of RLHF cost) without requiring human-written positive responses. Key limitations include that unlearned outputs are nonsensical rather than helpful, the method may induce refusals on normal prompts, and the comparison to RLHF is asymmetric (unlearning only needs to avoid harm, not produce quality). Open questions include robustness to paraphrasing and jailbreak attacks, scaling to frontier-scale models, and whether unlearning can be combined with positive-signal methods to produce genuinely helpful refusals.
