## Problem

Standard RLHF reward learning relies on the Bradley-Terry model, which assumes a fixed linear scaling between the preference logit and the reward difference for every pair, failing to capture the varying strengths of human preferences across different pairs. This rigidity limits the reward function's flexibility for downstream policy optimization and creates a misalignment where the reward model selected by highest preference accuracy often yields a poor policy, forcing costly joint hyperparameter tuning across both stages.

## Input / Output

The method consumes binary preference labels over pairs of trajectory segments (robotic control) or pairs of text responses (NLP), with no explicit preference-strength magnitude provided. It outputs a learned reward function parameterized by a neural network (for RL) or a LoRA-adapted policy (for DPO), where each training pair is additionally assigned an instance-specific scaling parameter τ ∈ [τ₀, τ_max] that modulates how strongly the reward difference maps to the preference probability. The scaling parameters are discarded after reward learning and do not appear in the final policy.

## Method

The paper derives an adaptive preference loss from a KL-constrained distributionally robust optimization formulation applied at the instance level, yielding the per-pair loss −τ log σ((r(z_w) − r(z_l))/τ) + ρτ, where τ is a learned scaling factor that makes the logit-to-reward-difference mapping non-linear. Theoretically, τ* is driven to the lower bound τ₀ for ambiguous preferences (small reward differences) and to the upper bound τ_max for clear preferences (large reward differences), as shown by Proposition 3.1. Computationally, the loss is strictly convex and univariate in τ, so each τ is optimized to near-optimality via a few iterations of projected Newton's method (K=3–5) before a standard SGD/Adam step on the reward network. The approach extends naturally to DPO (Ada-DPO) by substituting the implicit reward r_π(a|s) = log(π(a|s)/π_ref(a|s)) into the same adaptive loss, and a quadratic-regularization variant removes the need for the τ_max hyperparameter entirely.

## Results

On three PyBullet robotic control tasks, Ada-Pref consistently outperforms the standard cross-entropy baseline (Pref) in policy return: HalfCheetah 2875.45 vs. 2724.42, Ant 3177.11 vs. 2917.81, and Hopper 1692.10 vs. 1324.91, while maintaining comparable preference prediction accuracy (within ~1%). Under the second tuning criterion (selecting the reward model by preference accuracy rather than policy return), Ada-Pref shows substantially less performance degradation (e.g., Hopper: 1134.73 vs. 744.66 return), demonstrating better alignment between reward modeling and downstream policy optimization. On natural language generation with Llama-2 7B, Ada-DPO achieves the highest win rate against the instruction-tuned reference model on both summarization and single-turn dialogue, outperforming DPO, IPO, and SLiC-HF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard cross-entropy reward learning baseline (Pref, from Christiano et al. 2017), Ada-Pref wins on all three robotic control tasks in policy return (by 5.5% on HalfCheetah, 8.9% on Ant, and 27.7% on Hopper) while achieving comparable preference accuracy. The alignment benefit is most pronounced under the second tuning criterion: when the reward model is selected by preference accuracy alone, Ada-Pref degrades far less than Pref (e.g., Hopper return drops from 1692 to 1135 for Ada-Pref vs. 1325 to 745 for Pref), confirming that the adaptive loss better aligns reward quality with policy performance. In the NLP setting, Ada-DPO outperforms DPO (Rafailov et al. 2024), IPO (Azar et al. 2024), and SLiC-HF (Zhao et al. 2023) on win rate for both summarization and dialogue tasks. Notably, when the model must be selected by preference accuracy rather than by an expensive LLM judge (Claude 3), Ada-DPO's advantage over DPO becomes even more pronounced, as the adaptive scaling reduces the gap between preference accuracy and actual policy quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard Bradley-Terry reward learning framework used in RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) by replacing the fixed linear scaling between preference logits and reward differences with instance-specific adaptive scaling factors. The scaling is derived from a KL-constrained distributionally robust optimization formulation (Qi et al., 2023), where each preference pair gets its own Lagrange multiplier τ that modulates the effective 'temperature' of the sigmoid. The approach is explicitly distinguished from adaptive temperature scaling in calibration literature (where larger τ means more uncertainty) and from prior preference-loss modifications like IPO (Azar et al., 2024) or SLiC-HF (Zhao et al., 2023), which alter the loss shape globally rather than per-instance. The method is further extended to DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024) as Ada-DPO, showing the loss is a drop-in replacement across preference optimization frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and addresses a practical misalignment: the reward model with the highest preference prediction accuracy often does not yield the best downstream policy, forcing practitioners to jointly tune both stages. By making the reward function more flexible (larger reward gaps for clear preferences, smaller for ambiguous ones), the method partially decouples reward-model selection from policy performance, simplifying the tuning pipeline. Limitations include the need to tune τ_max and ρ (mitigated by the quadratic regularization variant), single-seed NLP experiments, and reliance on synthetic preference labels in robotics. Open questions include whether the DRO-derived scaling generalizes to multi-turn or sequential preference settings, and whether the misalignment phenomenon persists at larger model scales where reward overfitting is less of a concern.
