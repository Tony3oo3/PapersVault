## Problem

Continual learning for scene graph generation (CSEGG) remains unexplored despite extensive work on continual image classification and object detection. SGG introduces unique challenges absent in simpler tasks: combinatorial explosion of object-relationship pairs, long-tailed class distributions that shift across scenes, and the need to simultaneously expand, modify, retain, and reason over graph-structured knowledge. No prior benchmark or method systematically addresses these challenges.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244334/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The CSEGG benchmark is built from Visual Genome images with dense object and relationship annotations, organized into three incremental learning scenarios (relationship-incremental, scene-incremental, and relationship-generalization). The SGG model takes an RGB scene image and outputs a structured scene graph: object nodes with class labels and bounding boxes, plus relationship edges expressed as (subject, predicate, object) triplets. In the RAS method, the replay pipeline consumes only symbolic triplet labels (e.g., <man, on, horse>) rather than original images, feeding them as text prompts into Stable Diffusion to synthesize new scene images, which are then annotated by a frozen model snapshot to produce pseudo scene-graph labels for training.

## Method

RAS (Replays via Analysis by Synthesis) is a generative replay method that avoids storing original images by decomposing previous-task scene graphs into triplet labels and re-composing them into contextually coherent text prompts. A context checker uses BERT embeddings and agglomerative clustering (threshold 0.6) to group semantically related triplets, ensuring generated scenes respect real-world co-occurrence rules. A Long-Tailed Distribution (LTD) module applies frequency-proportional dropout at the triplet level to balance head and tail classes during prompt construction. Stable Diffusion generates γ=10 images per prompt, and a frozen model snapshot from the previous task predicts scene-graph annotations on these synthesized images, which serve as pseudo-replay exemplars. The approach is evaluated on two SGG backbones (SGTR transformer and TCNN) across three learning scenarios, outperforming EWC, PackNet, and standard image-replay baselines while using less storage and preserving data privacy.

## Results

On the CSEGG benchmark (Visual Genome), RAS achieves Avg.R of 10.78 and mR of 5.6 in Scenario 1 (relationship incremental, SGTR backbone), outperforming the best replay baseline Replay@20% (Avg.R 9.25, mR 4.78) and the upper-bound Joint training (Avg.R 20.15) by a smaller margin. In the harder Scenario 2 (scene incremental), RAS reaches Avg.R 3.45 and mR 2.75 (SGTR), still trailing Joint (Avg.R 12.64) but leading all other baselines. Forgetting (F) is reduced from −20.35 (Replay@20%) to −18.92 in S1 and from −17.17 to −10.23 in S2, while RAS requires only ~1.2 GB storage (equivalent to 15% exemplar images) versus ~1.2 GB for Replay@20%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RAS outperforms all three major categories of continual learning baselines across both SGG backbones and both scenarios. Against EWC [24] (weight regularization), RAS achieves substantially higher Avg.R (10.78 vs. 1.89 in S1-SGTR; 3.45 vs. 0 in S2-SGTR) and lower forgetting (−18.92 vs. −28.4 in S1), confirming that parameter-space regularization fails to capture the complex SGG architecture. Against PackNet [44] (parameter isolation), RAS leads by 3.59 Avg.R in S1-SGTR (10.78 vs. 7.19) and 1.78 in S2-SGTR (3.45 vs. 1.67), showing that iterative pruning is insufficient for the combinatorial relationship space. Against Replay@20% [57] (the strongest image-replay baseline), RAS gains 1.53 Avg.R in S1-SGTR (10.78 vs. 9.25) and a larger 0.88 in S2-SGTR (3.45 vs. 2.57), while requiring equivalent or less storage. RAS also surpasses RAS_GT (which uses ground-truth scene graphs for synthesis), indicating that decomposing and re-composing scene graphs into diverse in-context prompts is more effective than direct ground-truth replay. The gap to the Joint upper bound remains large (10.78 vs. 20.15 in S1-SGTR), highlighting that CSEGG is still an open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generative replay paradigm for continual learning (Shin et al., 2017) from GAN-based image synthesis to diffusion-model-based synthesis, but with a key departure: instead of generating arbitrary images, RAS uses scene graph triplets as symbolic prompts to produce contextually coherent replay images. It also builds on the SGG backbone literature (SGTR, TCNN) and standard CL methods (EWC, PackNet, Replay), but positions CSEGG as a fundamentally harder problem than continual object detection or image classification due to combinatorial relationship complexity and long-tailed distributions. The paper carves new ground by being the first to systematically benchmark continual learning specifically for scene graph generation, reorganizing Visual Genome into three learning protocols that have no direct precedent in prior CL or SGG literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive CSEGG benchmark, providing the field with standardized protocols (relationship-incremental, scene-incremental, relationship-generalization) and evaluation metrics (Gen R_bbox, Gen R) that were previously undefined. The finding that all standard CL methods (EWC, PackNet, even Replay@100%) suffer severe forgetting on SGG highlights a genuine gap that practitioners deploying SGG in dynamic environments (robotics, medical imaging, AR) must address. Key open questions remain: the large performance gap between RAS and Joint training in S2, error propagation from pseudo-labels in the replay pipeline, and whether the approach extends to video SGG or benefits from fine-grained control signals in the diffusion model. The memory efficiency and privacy preservation of storing only triplets rather than images is a practical advantage for deployment.
