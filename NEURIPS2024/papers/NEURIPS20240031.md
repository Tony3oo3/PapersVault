## Problem

Tree-based search methods like MCTS (used in AlphaZero/MuZero) are inherently sequential, perform poorly at low compute budgets, and require algorithmic modifications to handle continuous or large action spaces. This paper addresses the need for a scalable, parallelizable policy improvement operator that works uniformly across discrete and continuous domains without environment-specific adaptations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240031/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

SPO consumes environment state observations (state vectors of dimension 18–376 for Brax continuous tasks; 10×10 integer grids for Boxoban; 6×3×3 face arrays for Rubik's Cube), a world model providing transition dynamics and reward predictions, and a trained policy network. It produces an action distribution estimated from N=16 SMC particles over a horizon of h=4 steps, updated policy parameters via a KL-constrained M-step, and a value function trained with GAE on true environment rollouts. The particle-based search is fully parallel across particles, enabling direct use of GPU/TPU accelerators without the sequential bottleneck of tree search.

## Method

SPO formalises policy improvement as an EM procedure: the E-step estimates a KL-constrained target distribution q_i(a|s) ∝ π(a|s)·exp(A(s,a)/η*) using Sequential Monte Carlo with N particles that are sampled from the current policy, weighted by advantage-based importance ratios, and periodically resampled to combat weight degeneracy. The M-step projects this non-parametric target back onto the parametric policy space via a KL-constrained maximum-likelihood update. A key distinction from prior EM methods is that SMC simultaneously leverages breadth (multiple particles per state) and depth (multi-step rollouts through the world model), whereas MPO uses breadth without depth and V-MPO uses depth for a single action. The temperature η* is solved adaptively at each iteration by minimising a convex dual objective, automatically enforcing the KL constraint without per-environment hyperparameter tuning. The monotonic improvement guarantee of EM holds under perfect target estimation, and the parallel particle structure yields favourable wall-clock scaling compared to sequential MCTS.

## Results

SPO (16 particles × 4 horizon = 64 total search budget) outperforms all model-free and model-based baselines across both continuous (Ant, HalfCheetah, Humanoid) and discrete (Boxoban Hard, Rubik's Cube 7-scramble) environments, measured by interquartile mean (IQM) of min-max normalised episode returns over 1280 evaluation episodes. The improvement is statistically significant: all probability-of-improvement values exceed 0.5 with 95% confidence intervals entirely above 0.5, and SPO is stochastically dominant over every baseline in performance profiles. At test time, SPO achieves 4× more search budget than AlphaZero within a 0.1 s/step wall-clock constraint on a TPUv3-8, and competitive performance is maintained even when the training search budget is reduced by a factor of four.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPO outperforms all six baselines across every evaluated environment. Against model-free methods (PPO, MPO, V-MPO), SPO achieves consistently higher IQM scores on both continuous and discrete tasks, with the gap widening over training as the SMC-based policy improvement compounds. Against AlphaZero (64 simulations, matched search budget), SPO wins on all environments with statistically significant margins; AlphaZero exhibits high variance across tasks (strong on Boxoban and HalfCheetah but degraded on Rubik's Cube, Ant, and Humanoid), whereas SPO performs uniformly well. Against SMC-ENT (Piché et al.), SPO's advantage demonstrates the critical benefit of using SMC posterior estimates directly for policy improvement in the M-step rather than training a separate proposal. Against Sampled MuZero on continuous control, SPO also leads, confirming that the SMC search operator is effective even in high-dimensional continuous action spaces without environment-specific modifications. The wall-clock comparison on Rubik's Cube further shows SPO's parallel search scaling favourably: at equal compute, SPO with horizon 4 or 8 outperforms AlphaZero, and the gap widens as more time is available per step.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPO sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) EM-based policy optimisation (MPO, V-MPO, REPS), which provides the KL-constrained E-step/M-step structure; (2) Sequential Monte Carlo for trajectory distribution estimation (Piché et al., Lazaric et al.), which supplies the parallel, sample-based search mechanism; and (3) expert-iteration / dual-policy-iteration methods (AlphaZero, MuZero), which amortise search-derived policies into neural networks. The key departure is that prior SMC-based RL methods (Piché et al., CriticSMC) either did not use SMC posterior estimates for iterative policy improvement or lacked KL regularisation, while prior EM methods (MPO, V-MPO) exploited either breadth or depth of search but not both. SPO unifies these by using SMC's multi-particle, multi-step rollouts to estimate the EM target distribution, then projecting it back onto the parametric policy space with a KL constraint, yielding a method that is inherently parallelisable unlike MCTS-based alternatives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is the parallelism: SMC search scales across hardware accelerators without the virtual-loss degradation that plagues parallelised MCTS, enabling 4× more search budget at equal wall-clock time on Rubik's Cube. This makes model-based policy improvement more accessible for large-scale training where MCTS's sequential bottleneck is prohibitive. Key limitations constrain broader impact: the method requires an exact (or highly accurate) world model and is validated only in deterministic settings, where SMC weight updates in stochastic environments could induce risk-seeking behaviour. Extending SPO to learned world models and stochastic dynamics is the most important open question, as it would determine whether the approach generalises beyond the simulator-rich benchmarks used here.
