## Problem

CLIP's dense, high-dimensional embeddings encode rich semantic information but are not human-interpretable, limiting transparency in downstream applications. Existing interpretability methods for vision-language models require predefined concept sets, labeled concept data, task-specific training, or unreliable qualitative visualizations. The paper asks whether CLIP representations can be decomposed into sparse, human-interpretable concept combinations in a task-agnostic, training-free manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242677/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a CLIP image embedding (512-dim, from OpenCLIP ViT-B/32) and a fixed overcomplete concept dictionary of 15,000 one- and two-word phrases (top 10k unigrams + 5k bigrams from LAION-400m captions, encoded via CLIP's text encoder). It produces a sparse nonnegative weight vector w with l0 norm of 5–20 (typically 0.2–0.3 relative sparsity) and a reconstructed dense embedding. Both the image embedding and concept dictionary are mean-centered and re-normalized to address CLIP's modality gap before decomposition, and the reconstruction is uncentered back to the original image cone.

## Method

SpLiCE formulates the decomposition as a sparse recovery problem: find the sparsest nonnegative combination of concept dictionary vectors that achieves a target cosine similarity with the centered CLIP embedding. This is relaxed to a convex LASSO objective (MSE + l1 penalty with nonnegativity constraint), solved per-image via scikit-learn's Lasso or a custom ADMM implementation for batched GPU inference. Three key design choices distinguish it from prior work: (1) nonnegative weights ensure all concepts carry positive semantic meaning, avoiding uninterpretable 'negative concepts'; (2) an overcomplete, task-agnostic dictionary derived from LAION-400m token frequencies avoids dependence on LLM-generated or task-specific concept sets; and (3) explicit modality alignment via mean-centering rectifies the cone structure of CLIP's image and text spaces, enabling valid nonnegative decomposition. The method is entirely training-free and can serve as a drop-in replacement for dense CLIP embeddings while preserving downstream task performance.

## Results

At human-interpretable sparsity levels (20–35 concepts), SpLiCE achieves zero-shot accuracy within 1–9% of dense CLIP across datasets (e.g., 0.86 vs. 0.90 on Caltech-101, 0.66 vs. 0.67 on SUN397, 0.96 vs. 0.96 on STL-10). At sparsity 512, SpLiCE completely recovers CLIP zero-shot accuracy (0.768 vs. 0.750 on CIFAR-100, 0.474 vs. 0.469 on MIT States, 0.552 vs. 0.552 on ImageNet). On probing tasks, SpLiCE representations match CLIP almost exactly (0.95 vs. 0.97 on CIFAR-100 at l0=3; 0.883 vs. 0.883 on MIT States). Notably, cosine similarity between SpLiCE and CLIP embeddings remains low (~0.75), indicating SpLiCE captures semantic content but discards non-semantic noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against dense CLIP embeddings, SpLiCE at practical sparsity (20–35 concepts) incurs a small zero-shot accuracy drop (1–9% depending on dataset) but fully recovers CLIP performance at sparsity 512; on probing tasks the gap is negligible (≤0.02). Against the random and FISTA-learned dictionary baselines (Figure 3), the semantic LAION dictionary significantly outperforms both on zero-shot classification, probing, and retrieval, while all three dictionaries perform similarly on cosine reconstruction—confirming that the semantic vocabulary captures task-relevant information that random or purely data-driven dictionaries miss. In the user study (Figure 8), SpLiCE explanations are significantly preferred over both LF-CBM [Oikarinen et al.] and IP-OMP [Chattopadhyay et al.] on relevance to the image and informativeness (p < 0.01), without requiring task-specific concept mining or trained classification probes. The ablation study (Figure 5) further shows that removing non-negativity, modality alignment, or replacing the LAION dictionary with an LLM-generated one each degrades performance, validating all three design choices relative to prior concept-bottleneck approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpLiCE builds on the linear representation hypothesis and the observation that multimodal models encode concepts additively, but departs from prior concept-bottleneck and mechanistic-interpretability work by requiring no training, no task-specific concept labels, and no manual annotation of visualizations. Where prior CLIP-interpretability methods (Chattopadhyay et al., Gandelsman et al.) either use task-specific dictionaries, produce non-sparse or negative-weight decompositions, or are limited to specific architectures, SpLiCE uses a fixed, overcomplete, task-agnostic dictionary of 15K one- and two-word phrases drawn from LAION-400m and enforces nonnegativity and sparsity. The modality-alignment step (mean-centering to collapse the image and text cones) is a practical contribution not present in earlier sparse-decomposition approaches for CLIP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SpLiCE offers a training-free, drop-in replacement for dense CLIP embeddings that preserves near-CLIP zero-shot accuracy while making the semantic content of each representation explicit, enabling downstream applications like spurious-correlation auditing (e.g., the woman/swimwear bias in CIFAR100) and surgical model editing without retraining. Key limitations include the restriction to one- and two-word concepts (missing fine-grained or multi-word semantics), reliance on LAION's token-frequency biases for dictionary construction, and the use of an ℓ₁ relaxation that may not recover the true sparsest solution. Open questions include whether learned or binary dictionaries, alternative relaxations, or token-level (rather than word-level) vocabularies can further close the gap to full CLIP performance while maintaining interpretability.
