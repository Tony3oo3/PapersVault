## Problem

Relightable 3D reconstruction requires rendering a captured object under novel illumination from arbitrary viewpoints. Existing approaches frame this as inverse rendering—jointly optimizing geometry, materials, and lighting through differentiable Monte Carlo rendering—which is computationally expensive, brittle to optimize, and inherently ambiguous: the single recovered material/lighting decomposition may be wrong, and errors propagate catastrophically into relit renderings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241349/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of posed RGB images of an object under unknown lighting plus a target HDR environment map. Geometry is first extracted from the input views via UniSDF, and radiance cues (four renderings of that geometry under the target light with predefined GGX materials) are generated at 512×512 resolution to condition the diffusion model. The output is a 3D latent NeRF (built on UniSDF with GLO embeddings) that renders relit novel views at 512×512; different latent codes correspond to different plausible material explanations, and at inference the zero latent is used for a single consistent rendering.

## Method

IllumiNeRF replaces 3D inverse rendering with a two-stage pipeline: (1) a single-image Relighting Diffusion Model (RDM), a ControlNet-fine-tuned latent diffusion model (StableDiffusion-like) conditioned on radiance cues, generates S=16 plausible relit samples per input viewpoint under the target lighting; (2) a latent NeRF (UniSDF with GLO-based latent conditioning on the appearance branch) is optimized to reconcile all S×N relit samples into a single consistent 3D representation. The RDM is fine-tuned on ~152K Objaverse objects rendered under 509 Polyhaven environment maps, learning to map an input image plus radiance cues to a relit image. The key insight is that by treating the unknown material/lighting decomposition as a latent variable and sampling multiple plausible explanations from the diffusion model, the latent NeRF can distill a consistent 3D appearance without ever explicitly solving the ambiguous inverse rendering problem. Geometry is fixed from the UniSDF stage; only appearance-related weights and the latent codes are optimized during the NeRF fitting stage.

## Results

On the TensoIR synthetic benchmark, IllumiNeRF achieves 29.71 PSNR, 0.947 SSIM, and 0.072 LPIPS, outperforming the previous best (TensoIR) by 1.13 dB in PSNR and 0.009 in LPIPS, while running in ~2.5h total versus 5h for TensoIR. On the real-world Stanford-ORB benchmark, IllumiNeRF achieves 25.42 PSNR-H, 32.62 PSNR-L, 0.976 SSIM, and 0.027 LPIPS, surpassing all baselines except Neural-PBIR (26.01 PSNR-H, 33.26 PSNR-L). The single-GPU variant retains competitive performance (29.25 PSNR on TensoIR, 25.56 PSNR-H on Stanford-ORB) with roughly 3× longer runtime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On TensoIR, IllumiNeRF decisively outperforms all three baselines on every metric: it beats TensoIR (the prior SOTA) by 1.13 dB PSNR and 0.009 LPIPS, and surpasses NeRFactor and InvRender by over 5 dB PSNR. On Stanford-ORB, IllumiNeRF outperforms NeRFactor (23.54 PSNR-H), InvRender (23.76), NVDiffRec (22.91), NVDiffRecMC (24.43), NeRD (23.29), and PhySG (21.81) on all metrics. The sole exception is Neural-PBIR, which achieves slightly higher metrics (26.01 PSNR-H vs. 25.42), but the authors argue this is due to Neural-PBIR's predominantly diffuse output lacking specular highlights, which the Stanford-ORB per-image illumination mismatch penalizes less. Qualitatively, IllumiNeRF recovers realistic specular reflections that all inverse-rendering baselines fail to produce, and the paper notes that the Stanford-ORB benchmark's inconsistent per-image illumination (from a moving light probe) inherently disadvantages methods that faithfully model specularities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant inverse rendering paradigm for relightable 3D reconstruction (NeRFactor, InvRender, TensoIR, NVDiffRec, Neural-PBIR), which decomposes appearance into geometry, materials, and lighting via differentiable Monte Carlo rendering. Instead, it repurposes single-image relighting diffusion models (in the lineage of DiLightNet, LightIt, and DiffusionLight) as a generative prior, conditioning on radiance cues rendered from UniSDF-estimated geometry, and then distills multiple plausible relit samples into a latent NeRF (building on NeRF in the Wild's latent-code conditioning). The key conceptual shift is replacing the ill-posed 3D inverse rendering optimization with a two-stage pipeline: 2D generative sampling followed by 3D consistency enforcement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a diffusion-based 'generate-then-distill' pipeline outperforms purpose-built inverse rendering systems on both synthetic (TensoIR) and real-world (Stanford-ORB) benchmarks challenges the field's assumption that physically-based decomposition is necessary for relighting. Limitations include the non-real-time nature (new NeRF optimization per target light), dependence on geometry quality for specular accuracy, and the Stanford-ORB illumination inconsistency that complicates fair comparison. Open questions include whether this paradigm extends to full scenes with inter-object interactions, whether the latent NeRF can be made real-time, and whether the diffusion model's training on synthetic geometry limits generalization to real-world material diversity.
