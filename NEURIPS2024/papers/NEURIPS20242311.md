## Problem

Estimating the governing equations of motion from observed trajectories in collective motion is essential for decoding emergent behaviors, but existing system identification methods either scale poorly with system size (e.g., SINDy) or have not been applied to mixed-species systems where different cell types interact in unknown, potentially non-reciprocal ways. This paper tackles the gap of learning species-dependent interaction rules in multi-species active matter, where the force between two entities depends on the identity of both interacting partners.

## Input / Output

The method consumes time-series trajectory data (positions and orientations) of N collective entities along with categorical species labels, structured as a dynamic directed graph whose edges are redefined at each time step by a distance threshold. The output consists of two neural-network-parameterized force functions—self-propulsion F^(1) and pairwise interaction F^(2)—that are rotationally invariant (inputs converted to relative angles via rotation matrices) and species-dependent (categorical types passed through embedding layers), enabling prediction of future trajectories from arbitrary initial conditions.

## Method

The core contribution is a custom wrapper that tightly couples a GNN with a Neural ODE (or Neural SDE for stochastic cases), where the GNN dynamically defines edges at each integration step using a stored distance function and threshold, computes interaction forces on edges, and adds self-propulsion on nodes before returning the total force to the ODE solver. Unlike prior GNN+ODE approaches that require fixed or pre-specified graph structures, this design makes edge topology a function of the current state, reducing memory from 200 GB to 30 GB for 400-body simulations while preserving extrapolatability. Forces are parameterized as three-layer fully connected networks (128 nodes, ELU activation) followed by a learnable scaling layer, and training minimizes a trajectory-level loss (position and orientation MSE over a prediction horizon) using the LAMB optimizer. The framework is validated on a harmonic oscillator toy model for hyperparameter tuning, then applied to a mixed-species model with contact following, chemotaxis, and excluded volume interactions that mimics cellular slime mold dynamics.

## Results

In the mixed-species model (400 individuals, two species), the estimated self-propulsion force F_r^(1) achieves normalized MSE of 5.8×10⁻⁴ to 2.3×10⁻³ across all parameter sets, while the two-body interaction forces F_r^(2) and F_φ^(2) show higher errors of order 10⁻¹ (MSE 1.9×10⁻¹ to 9.9×10⁻¹ for F_r^(2); 5.4×10⁻² to 2.4×10⁻¹ for F_φ^(2)). In the simpler harmonic interaction model, with sufficient training data (M_tra=270, γ=10⁻²), both F_v^(1) and F_v^(2) are estimated with normalized MSE below 6×10⁻³. The estimated model qualitatively reproduces the correct collective dynamics: rotational mixed-species clusters for parameter set (i) and centipede-like translational clusters for parameter set (ii), including in single-species extrapolation tests.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other system identification methods (e.g., SINDy, Bayesian optimization, or attention-based approaches) under a common protocol. Instead, validation is performed by directly comparing the estimated force functions F_NN^(1) and F_NN^(2) against the known ground-truth F_sim^(1) and F_sim^(2), and by verifying that the estimated model S_NN(θ*) reproduces the correct collective dynamics from random initial conditions. A key negative control confirms that when both species share identical parameters (sets iii–v), the estimated forces show minimal species dependence, ruling out methodological artifacts. The authors note that interaction force estimation errors (MSE ~10⁻¹) are substantially higher than self-propulsion errors (MSE ~10⁻³), likely due to the high dimensionality of the interaction network inputs and the neglect of noise during training. Convergence is notably slow, requiring 20,000 epochs (~25 days) for the mixed-species model, and the authors acknowledge they could not identify hyperparameters that simultaneously achieve faster convergence and accurate functional form recovery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper integrates graph neural networks with neural ODEs/SDEs to perform system identification of pairwise interaction forces in collective motion, building on the neural ODE framework (Chen et al., 2018), neural SDE solvers (Li et al., 2020; Kidger et al., 2021), and prior GNN+ODE approaches that operate on fixed or pre-specified graph structures (Xhonneux et al., 2020; Poli et al., 2021). It departs from these by dynamically defining edges at each time step via a distance threshold, avoiding the memory cost of fully connected graphs. In the system-identification lineage, it extends beyond SINDy (Champion et al., 2019) and Bayesian basis-expansion methods (Brückner et al., 2019–2021), which struggle with many-body scaling, and goes beyond prior deep-learning approaches to collective motion (Heras et al., 2019; Koyama et al., 2023) by explicitly targeting non-reciprocal, species-dependent two-body forces in mixed-species systems—a setting the authors note has not been addressed by any prior method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled route to decoding the interaction rules governing mixed-species collective motion (e.g., cellular slime molds, immune cell swarms), a step toward understanding emergent tissue-level behaviors from individual-level data. Key limitations temper the impact: training requires days to weeks of GPU time, the method is restricted to deterministic pairwise interactions (noise is not estimated), and all validation is on synthetic data with no application to real experimental trajectories. Open questions include extending to higher-order (three-body) interactions, incorporating stochastic dynamics into the learned equations, and scaling to the noisy, partially observed data produced by real microscopy experiments.
