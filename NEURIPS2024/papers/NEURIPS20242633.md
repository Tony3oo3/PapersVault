## Problem

While differential privacy provides worst-case guarantees against label inference attacks, heuristic PETs like random label aggregation (used in production APIs such as Chrome's Privacy Sandbox and Apple's SKAN) lack provable guarantees, and no unified empirical metric existed to place both DP and non-DP mechanisms on the same footing. The paper asks: how much does a PET's output increase an attacker's ability to infer a target's label beyond what public features already reveal, and can this be quantified in a way that enables direct comparison of privacy-utility tradeoffs across fundamentally different mechanisms?

## Input / Output

The auditing framework consumes public feature vectors, the PET's privatized output (flipped labels for RR, per-bag label proportions for LLP, or noisy proportions for LLP+Lap/Geom), and an estimate of the class-conditional prior η(x) = P(y=1|x) — computed analytically for synthetic data or approximated by a trained classifier on real data. It produces two scalar advantage values per data point (additive: difference in Bayes-optimal success rates; multiplicative: difference in log odds ratios) and, aggregated over the dataset, a privacy-utility tradeoff curve mapping each PET parameter setting to a (advantage, test AUC) pair. The multiplicative measure is unbounded (infinite when a bag is homogeneous), so experiments report its 98th percentile to handle the tail.

## Method

The paper introduces two reconstruction advantage measures grounded in the prior-posterior shift of an optimal attacker: an additive measure (difference in Bayes-optimal label prediction accuracy with and without the PET output) and a multiplicative measure (difference in log odds ratios, which captures relative confidence changes and tail events invisible to the additive metric). Theoretically, they derive tight distribution-dependent bounds: for RR, the individual advantage equals (min{η(x),1−η(x)} − π)·𝕀{η(x)∈[π,1−π]}; for LLP, they exploit the Poisson-Binomial structure of bag sums and a discrete Fourier transform argument to show the advantage decays as Õ(μ^{1/4}(p(1−p))^{1/4}/√k + μ^{1/4}/k). Empirically, they compute posterior probabilities analytically via Bayes' theorem for each PET, train models on privatized labels using debiased losses (for RR) or proportion matching (for LLP variants), and sweep privacy parameters on Higgs and KDD12 to trace AUC-vs-advantage curves. The key empirical finding is that differentially private mechanisms (RR, LLP+Geom) dominate or match the privacy-utility tradeoff of deterministic label aggregation across all or most privacy levels, even when measured by the additive metric where the gap is smaller.

## Results

At AUC-comparable parameter settings (ε=1 for RR, k=8 for LLP and LLP+Geom) on the Higgs dataset, all three PETs achieve nearly identical test AUC, yet the multiplicative advantage CDFs differ dramatically: RR's CDF is a vertical line (bounded advantage), while LLP exhibits a significant mass of points with very large (even infinite) multiplicative advantage. On the imbalanced KDD12 dataset, this gap widens further. The paper also demonstrates that the distribution-dependent bound for RR at ε=1 yields an attack advantage of only 0.00095 on one dataset, versus the distribution-independent worst-case bound of ~0.46 from prior work [31]. Across all settings, differentially private schemes (RR, LLP+Geom) dominate or match the privacy-utility tradeoff of deterministic label aggregation (LLP) for both additive and multiplicative advantage measures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four PETs: Randomized Response (RR, ε-label DP), Label Proportions (LLP, deterministic aggregation), LLP+Geom (LLP + clipped geometric noise, ε-label DP), and LLP+Lap (LLP + Laplace noise, ε-label DP). When privacy is measured by multiplicative advantage, RR achieves the best privacy-utility tradeoff: at the same AUC on Higgs, LLP has a substantially higher 98th-percentile multiplicative advantage, and this gap is more pronounced on the imbalanced KDD12 dataset. LLP+Geom sits between RR and LLP, with its CDF first following LLP's curve and then transitioning to RR's bounded behavior. For additive advantage, all three mechanisms show roughly similar CDFs at comparable AUC, meaning the distinction is invisible under the coarser additive metric. The key takeaway is that even 'on its own turf'—where deterministic aggregation lacks provable guarantees—LLP does not provide a significant privacy advantage over DP mechanisms, and in fact is strictly worse on the multiplicative (tail-sensitive) measure due to the non-zero probability of homogeneous bags producing infinite log-odds ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the additive reconstruction advantage measure from Wu et al. [31] (which was defined only for label-DP mechanisms) to the Learning from Label Proportions (LLP) setting, and introduces a complementary multiplicative advantage measure inspired by the log-odds-ratio formulation underlying differential privacy [17, 10]. It draws on the PROPMATCH algorithm [12] and Empirical Proportion Risk Minimization [33] for utility evaluation, and positions its distributional auditing framework alongside membership inference attacks [21, 29] and distributional DP variants [9, 7, 16]. The key conceptual departure from prior work is placing DP-based (randomized response) and non-DP (random aggregation) PETs on a single, comparable footing via shared advantage metrics rather than treating them as incommensurable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified empirical and theoretical framework for comparing the privacy-utility tradeoffs of DP and non-DP label privatization mechanisms, yielding the practically important finding that DP schemes dominate or match aggregation-based schemes across all tested settings. Limitations include the i.i.d. assumption, restriction to binary labels, and the requirement that the adversary's prior η(x) be known or estimable; the authors note that data reuse, side information, and multiclass or semantically asymmetric labels remain open. The multiplicative measure's revelation that LLP has non-negligible probability of infinite per-individual leakage (homogeneous bags) is a concrete risk that additive metrics alone would miss, with direct implications for system designers choosing between PETs at scale.
