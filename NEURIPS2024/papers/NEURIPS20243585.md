## Problem

While LLMs trained on next-token prediction exhibit remarkable capabilities, the structural properties of language that make this objective effective remain poorly understood. Prior linguistic studies suspected self-similarity in language but relied on crude proxies (word length, single-word recurrence) that could not capture the full joint distribution. This paper provides the first formal, information-theoretic quantification of language's fractal structure—self-similarity and long-range dependence—and connects these properties to why next-token prediction succeeds.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw text documents (restricted to >4K tokens, using the first 2K) from The Pile validation split across 8 domains. An LLM (primarily PaLM2-L) scores each token's conditional probability, converting text into a sequence of negative log-probabilities (bits) that is then normalized to zero-mean, unit variance to form a stationary increment process. The outputs are four fractal parameters (S ≈ 0.59, H ≈ 0.70, D ≈ 1.41, J ≈ 0.49) plus a combined metric H_B = 1/BPB + H that predicts downstream benchmark performance with adjusted R² > 0.86, outperforming BPB alone (R² ≈ 0.65).

## Method

The paper converts language into a standardized time series by computing per-token bits z_t = -log p(w_t | w_[t-1]) using an LLM, then normalizing to a zero-mean, unit-variance increment process and its integral. Self-similarity is quantified by fitting a power law p_ε(τ) ~ τ^{-S} to the probability mass of small τ-increments; long-range dependence is quantified via rescaled-range (R/S) analysis fitting R(n)/S(n) ~ n^H; fractal dimension follows as D = 2 − S; and burstiness is captured by the Joseph exponent J from σ_τ ~ τ^J. The key empirical finding is that these parameters are robust across 12 model architectures (T5-Decoder 110M–5B, PaLM 8B–540B, PaLM2 XXS–L) and 8 domains, with DM-Mathematics as a notable exception (H ≈ 0.5, no LRD due to independent questions). The paper further shows that the median Hurst exponent, computed after full normalization, correlates with BPB (r = 0.83) and that combining them into H_B yields a strictly better predictor of downstream performance than either alone, suggesting second-order statistics of token losses encode information beyond average perplexity.

## Results

The paper establishes that language is self-similar (Hölder exponent S = 0.59 ± 0.08) and long-range dependent (Hurst parameter H = 0.70 ± 0.09), with a fractal dimension D = 1.41 ± 0.08 and Joseph exponent J = 0.49 ± 0.08. These parameters are robust across 12 LLMs (110M to 540B parameters) and 8 domains. Most practically, the combined metric H_B = 1/BPB + H improves the adjusted R² for predicting downstream performance from ~0.65–0.78 (BPB alone) to 0.83–0.98 across BIG-Bench Hard, MMLU, and GSM8K benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new model architecture but instead compares different metrics for predicting LLM downstream performance. BPB alone achieves adjusted R² of 0.65–0.88 across benchmarks, while the median Hurst exponent H alone reaches 0.83–0.90, and the combined metric H_B = 1/BPB + H consistently outperforms both, reaching 0.83–0.98 (e.g., 0.979 for 3S BBH CoT vs. 0.881 for BPB). The self-similarity exponent S and Joseph exponent J do not improve upon BPB when combined (Table 9), confirming that H is the uniquely informative fractal parameter. Across domains, most share similar fractal parameters, but DM-Mathematics is a notable exception with H ≈ 0.50 (no LRD, consistent with independent questions) and GitHub shows the highest H = 0.79 (more structure in code). Across architectures (T5-Decoder 110M–5B, PaLM 8B–540B, PaLM2 XXS–L), fractal parameters are remarkably stable, with only tiny monotonic increases in H tracking model quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the information-theoretic characterization of language complexity (Futrell & Hahn 2022, Hale 2001, Levy 2008) by representing text as a sequence of conditional-entropy bits computed by an LLM, rather than the crude proxies (word length, single-word recurrence) used in prior linguistic fractal studies (Ausloos 2012, Najafi & Darooneh 2015, Altmann et al. 2012). The fractal analysis toolkit—Hölder exponent, rescaled-range Hurst parameter, fractal dimension, Joseph exponent—is drawn from the stochastic-processes literature (Gneiting & Schlather 2004, Watkins 2019, Hurst 1951) and applied here for the first time to full LLM-conditional probability sequences. The key departure is the explicit connection between these quantified fractal properties and the success of next-token prediction in LLMs, a link that prior work only hypothesized without formal measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a formal, reproducible framework for quantifying long-range dependence and self-similarity in language, and demonstrates that the Hurst parameter (H ≈ 0.70) sits in a 'sweet spot' between predictability and noise that mirrors values in natural phenomena—a conceptual bridge that may inform future architecture design. Practically, the combined metric H_B (reciprocal BPB + median Hurst) improves downstream-performance prediction (adjusted R² from ~0.65 to ~0.86), offering a cheap diagnostic for model quality. Key limitations include English-only analysis, no modeling of semantic structure, and a negative result showing that longer training context (2K→8K) did not improve downstream performance in their T5 setup, leaving open whether the fractal argument about multi-scale context actually translates into architectural or training-time gains.
