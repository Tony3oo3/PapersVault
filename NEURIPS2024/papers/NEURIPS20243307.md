## Problem

Human-AI collaborative Bayesian optimization lacks principled theoretical guarantees: existing methods either assume near-oracle expert knowledge or rely on hand-tuned trust levels, with no assurance that expert advice won't degrade convergence. This paper is the first to simultaneously provide a no-harm guarantee (convergence rate no worse than vanilla GP-UCB even under adversarial expert labels) and a handover guarantee (sublinear cumulative expert queries), closing a gap between optimistic and pessimistic collaborative BO frameworks.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes noisy scalar evaluations of a black-box objective f(x) and binary accept/reject labels from a human expert on candidate points, where the expert's belief is modeled as an unknown function g in an RKHS with a sigmoid link to the rejection probability. At each iteration, the algorithm outputs a next query point x_t and a binary decision on whether to solicit an expert label, with the expert query frequency asymptotically converging to zero. The expert labels are deliberately kept cognitively simple (binary, qualitative) rather than requiring quantitative distributions or pinpoint locations, and the algorithm does not query the expert for the vanilla LCB candidate to minimize human effort.

## Method

COBOL maintains two surrogate models: a zero-mean GP for the objective f (yielding LCB/UCB) and a likelihood-ratio confidence set for the expert belief function g, where the confidence set is defined by functions whose log-likelihood is within a data-dependent radius of the MLE. The acquisition policy is a primal-dual problem that minimizes the LCB of f plus a dual weight λ_t times the lower confidence bound of g, with λ_t updated by dual ascent (increasing when the candidate is likely to be rejected, decreasing otherwise). A no-harm condition gates the use of the expert-augmented candidate: it is only selected if its LCB value is at least as good as the worst-case UCB of the optimum and its GP uncertainty is not excessively larger than the vanilla candidate's, ensuring the regret bound is independent of g. A handover threshold on the width of the g confidence interval [g̲_t(x), g̅_t(x)] triggers expert queries only when the model of expert belief is still uncertain, yielding sublinear cumulative expert queries. The norm bound B_g is estimated online by doubling when the likelihood at the doubled bound significantly exceeds the current bound, avoiding miscalibrated uncertainty.

## Results

On five synthetic benchmark functions (from the Virtual Library of Simulation Experiments), COBOL outperformed random sampling, vanilla LCB, and expert sampling in all cases. On the 4D Ackley function with varying expert accuracy (a = 1, 2, 0, −1, −2), the no-harm guarantee is empirically confirmed: even under fully adversarial feedback (a = −1, −2), convergence matches vanilla LCB, while informative feedback (a = 1, 2) accelerates convergence. In real-world lithium-ion battery experiments with four post-doctoral experts, COBOL outperformed all baselines on standard electrolyte composition tasks, and on emerging new-materials tasks (polymer-nanocomposite, ionic liquid) where expert knowledge was unreliable, COBOL matched the distrustful baseline [50] while trustful baselines [11, 7] failed to converge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares COBOL against three state-of-the-art human-AI collaborative BO methods with predefined trust levels, ranked from strong to weak: [11] AV et al. (NeurIPS 2022), [7] Adachi et al. (AISTATS 2024), and [50] Khoshvishkaie et al. (ECML 2023). On the two standard lithium-ion battery electrolyte tasks where experts had informative knowledge, COBOL outperformed all three baselines, with the advantage most pronounced on the modified electrolyte task where human sampling was already better than vanilla LCB. On the two new-materials tasks (polymer-nanocomposite and ionic liquid) where expert labels were effectively uninformative or worse than random, the strong-trust method [11] and medium-trust method [7] both struggled to converge, while the weak-trust method [50] converged on par with vanilla LCB. COBOL's data-driven trust adjustment allowed it to match [50]'s performance on these difficult tasks while still accelerating convergence when expert knowledge was useful, demonstrating the no-harm guarantee in practice. Additionally, on synthetic benchmarks, COBOL outperformed simpler baselines (random sampling, vanilla LCB, and direct expert sampling) across all five test functions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on GP-UCB (Srinivas et al., 2010) for the objective surrogate and likelihood ratio confidence sets (Emmenegger et al., 2023) for the expert belief surrogate, combining them via a primal-dual acquisition function. It departs from prior human-AI collaborative BO methods (AV et al. 2022, Khoshvishkaie et al. 2023, Adachi et al. 2024) that assume quantitative expert input or hand-tuned trust levels, instead modeling the expert as a cognitively simple binary classifier and deriving data-driven trust adjustment. The paper is the first to provide both a no-harm guarantee (convergence no worse than vanilla BO under adversarial advice) and a handover guarantee (sublinear expert queries) in a continuous-domain collaborative BO setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The no-harm and handover guarantees address a critical practical gap: in scientific discovery (e.g., novel battery materials), expert knowledge may be unreliable or even harmful, yet current methods lack principled safeguards. The real-world validation showing expert sampling performing worse than random for emerging materials underscores the necessity of adaptive trust. Limitations include reliance on GP-UCB (high-dimensionality constraints), single-expert setting, and no batch queries; future directions include LLM-as-expert integration, multi-expert aggregation, and high-dimensional extensions. The theoretical tension between acceleration and robustness (no-free-lunch) clarifies why prior methods with fixed trust levels struggle across diverse expert reliability.
