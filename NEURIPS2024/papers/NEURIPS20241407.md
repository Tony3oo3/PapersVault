## Problem

Current state-of-the-art adversarial attacks like AutoAttack select target classes naively by model confidence (top-9 logits), which misses viable targets that have lower confidence but smaller adversarial perturbations. No principled non-naive targeting method has been proposed, and attacking all classes is computationally infeasible for large-scale datasets like ImageNet with 1000 classes. The paper asks whether the mesoscopic almost linearity of neural networks can be exploited to identify the truly closest adversarial targets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained classification model (e.g., Swin-L, ConvNeXt-L, WRN-28-10) and a single test image (32×32×3 for CIFAR-100, 224×224×3 for ImageNet), along with hyperparameters c=100 (number of candidate classes scored) and a=9 (number of targets attacked). It computes the Jacobian row for each of the top-c candidate classes at the input point, scores them, and outputs an L∞-bounded adversarial perturbation (ε=8/255 for CIFAR-100, ε=4/255 for ImageNet) that flips the model's prediction to one of the top-a MALT-scored classes. The attack is a single forward pass plus c backward passes for scoring, followed by a·100 APGD iterations on the selected targets.

## Method

MALT (Mesoscopic Almost Linearity Targeting) replaces the naive logit-based target ordering with a Jacobian-normalized score: for each candidate class j, it computes (N(x₀)_j − N(x₀)_ℓ) / ‖(∇N(x₀))_j − (∇N(x₀))_ℓ‖, where ℓ is the true class. This ratio is derived from Lemma 3.1, which shows that for a linear predictor the optimal adversarial target minimizes exactly this quantity — the logit gap divided by the gradient-difference norm, interpreted as 'distance over speed.' The top-a classes by this score are then attacked using standard APGD with DLR loss. The theoretical justification (Theorems 4.1–4.2, Corollary 4.1) proves that 2-layer ReLU networks trained on data lying in a low-dimensional subspace P are mesoscopic almost linear in directions orthogonal to P, meaning the gradient norm changes negligibly along adversarial trajectories and the linear-inspired ordering is preserved. Empirically, MALT matches all of AutoAttack's successes while attacking additional samples, and runs ~5× faster on ImageNet by eliminating the need for FAB and Square attacks.

## Results

On ImageNet (ε=4/255 L∞), MALT achieves robust accuracy of 59.84% on Swin-L, 58.82% on ConvNeXt-L, 57.94% on ConvNeXt-L+, 56.54% on Swin-B, and 56.48% on ConvNeXt-B+, consistently outperforming AutoAttack by 0.02–0.06% while running 4.86×–5.22× faster. On CIFAR-100 (ε=8/255 L∞), MALT achieves 38.79%–42.66% robust accuracy across four WRN models, matching or slightly beating AutoAttack (by 0.01–0.07%) with a 3.36×–3.87× speedup. MALT uses only APGD with DLR loss (no FAB, no Square attack), requiring ~1900 forward/backward passes versus ~9727 for AutoAttack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is AutoAttack (Croce & Hein, ICML 2020), the current SOTA ensemble attack combining untargeted APGD, targeted APGD, targeted FAB, and Square black-box attack. MALT wins on every model and both datasets: on ImageNet it reduces robust accuracy by 0.02–0.06% (e.g., 59.84% vs. 59.90% on Swin-L) while running ~5× faster, and on CIFAR-100 it reduces robust accuracy by 0.01–0.07% with a ~3.5× speedup. Critically, MALT exhibits a strict inclusion property: every image successfully attacked by AutoAttack is also attacked by MALT, so the improvement comes entirely from additional images that AutoAttack fails on. The speedup is achieved because MALT replaces AutoAttack's four-attack ensemble (requiring ~9727 passes) with a single APGD attack guided by Jacobian-normalized targeting (~1900 passes). The paper also shows that integrating MALT with FAB or APGD-CE yields worse results (60.64% and 60.52% on Swin-L) than the chosen APGD-DLR combination (59.84%), confirming the targeting method's synergy with the DLR loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

MALT builds on the local linearity hypothesis (Goodfellow et al. 2014; Bubeck et al. 2021) and the low-dimensional manifold analysis of Melamed et al. (2023) and Haldar et al. (2024), but repurposes these ideas for a practical targeting problem rather than existence proofs. It wraps the standard APGD attack from AutoAttack (Croce and Hein 2020b) with a new Jacobian-normalized class-selection criterion, departing from AutoAttack's strategy of ensembling multiple diverse attacks (APGD, FAB, Square) and from the step-wise microscopic targeting used in FAB and DeepFool. The key conceptual departure is computing the targeting score once at the starting point using mesoscopic-scale linearity, rather than iteratively re-targeting at each step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5× speedup over AutoAttack while maintaining an inclusion property (every image AutoAttack attacks, MALT also attacks) makes adversarial robustness evaluation substantially cheaper, which is practically important for the RobustBench community. The theoretical and empirical evidence for mesoscopic almost linearity in adversarial directions is a useful characterization, though the formal results are limited to 2-layer networks with smooth, lower-bounded activations. Open questions include extending the theory to deeper architectures, understanding whether mesoscopic linearity interacts with robustness or transferability, and whether second-order targeting could further improve the method. The finding that c=100 candidate classes suffice (matching c=1000) is a reassuring practical result but leaves room for further optimization.
