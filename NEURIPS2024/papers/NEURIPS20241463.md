## Problem

Generating crystalline materials requires simultaneously modeling discrete variables (atom types) and continuous variables (atomic positions, lattice geometry), but existing approaches handle only one well: LLMs excel at discrete tokens but produce imprecise continuous values, while denoising models (diffusion, flow matching) handle continuous values and equivariances but struggle with discrete elements. No prior method effectively combined both paradigms to improve the rate of generating stable, unique, and novel (S.U.N.) materials.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural-language prompt (unconditional or conditioned on a chemical formula) as input to the LLM, which produces a text-encoded crystal with atom types as discrete tokens, fractional coordinates at 2-decimal precision, and lattice parameters (side lengths at 1 decimal, angles as integers). The RFM stage then refines the continuous values (fractional coordinates on a flat torus and lattice parameters in a bounded Euclidean space) while keeping atom types fixed, yielding a final crystal structure in the product space C = (a, f, l) with Niggli-reduced unit cells.

## Method

FlowLLM is a two-stage hybrid: first, a LLaMA-2 70B model is fine-tuned with LoRA on MP-20 crystal strings to serve as a learned base distribution p_LLM; second, a GNN-based Riemannian Flow Matching model is trained on pairs of (LLM sample, ground-truth crystal) to transport from p_LLM to the data distribution via geodesic paths on the crystal manifold. The key insight is that the LLM's output distribution, being trained on material data, is much closer to the target than a uniform base, so RFM needs far fewer integration steps (~250 vs. ~1000) and produces structures significantly closer to their relaxed ground state. The RFM velocity field is a message-passing GNN that enforces permutation equivariance and translation invariance, with separate heads for fractional-coordinate and lattice-parameter tangent vectors, and the training objective regresses onto conditional vector fields along closed-form geodesics on the product of flat tori and bounded Euclidean space.

## Results

On the MP-20 dataset, FlowLLM (τ=0.7, P=0.9) achieves a Stability Rate of 17.82% and a S.U.N. Rate of 4.92%, representing a ~300% improvement in stability rate and ~50% improvement in S.U.N. rate over the best prior methods. The best prior stability rates are CrystalLLM at 5.28%, DiffCSP at 5.06%, and FlowMM at 4.65%. FlowLLM also generates structures significantly closer to their relaxed ground state, with a 94.9% match rate and 0.023 Å RMSD to CHGNet-relaxed structures, compared to 74.3% and 0.096 Å for FlowMM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FlowLLM decisively outperforms all four baselines on the primary metrics. Against CrystalLLM (70B) [11], which is the closest LLM-based competitor, FlowLLM achieves 17.82% stability rate versus 5.28% (a 3.4× improvement), while CrystalLLM retains a slight edge on Composition Validity (95.4% vs. 90.84%). Against DiffCSP [16], FlowLLM more than triples the stability rate (17.82% vs. 5.06%) and improves S.U.N. rate from 3.34% to 4.92%, though DiffCSP achieves higher Coverage Recall (99.71% vs. 96.95%). Against FlowMM [26], the closest flow-matching competitor, FlowLLM nearly quadruples the stability rate (17.82% vs. 4.65%) and doubles the S.U.N. rate (4.92% vs. 2.34%), while also producing structures much closer to their relaxed ground state (0.023 Å RMSD vs. 0.096 Å). Against CDVAE [47], the improvement is most dramatic: a 11× increase in stability rate (17.82% vs. 1.57%). The ablation FlowLLM-Types (LLM only for atom types, uniform base for coordinates) still reaches 8.79% stability, confirming that the learned LLM base distribution for continuous variables is the key driver of the full model's advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

FlowLLM sits at the intersection of two recent lines of work on crystalline material generation: LLM-based approaches (CrystalLLM, Gruver et al. 2024) that represent materials as text sequences, and Riemannian Flow Matching (FlowMM, Miller et al. 2024) that models continuous crystal geometry on a manifold. The key conceptual departure is using the fine-tuned LLM's output distribution as the learned base distribution for RFM, replacing the standard uniform base. This bridges the complementary weaknesses of the two paradigms—LLMs handle discrete atom types well but struggle with finite-precision continuous values, while flow matching handles continuous geometry and equivariances but lacks a natural mechanism for discrete elements and natural-language conditioning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3× improvement in stability rate and ~50% gain in S.U.N. rate are practically significant given that DFT validation of each candidate is extremely expensive, making generation rate a direct bottleneck for materials discovery. A secondary but important finding is that FlowLLM-generated structures are far closer to their relaxed ground state (94.9% match rate, ~38 CHGNet steps vs. 74.3% and ~192 for FlowMM), which could substantially reduce post-hoc computational cost. The main open limitation is the lack of end-to-end differentiability, which blocks direct application to inverse design (optimizing for target properties), a capability that purely denoising-based models already support. Whether the learned-base-distribution principle generalizes beyond materials to other hybrid discrete-continuous generation tasks remains an open question.
