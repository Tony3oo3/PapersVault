## Problem

Multi-source light detection in driving scenarios lacks a public benchmark dataset, with existing LD datasets limited to binary light-vs-background classification or single light sources. Without a shared benchmark, it is difficult to compare detectors or establish baselines for this task, which is critical for autonomous driving and night vision but has received far less attention than general object detection.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242224/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes night/evening driving images (HD 1280×720 or UHD 3840×2160) captured across 19 countries and produces bounding boxes with one of three class labels: Carlight, TrafficSignallight, or Streetlight. The average bounding box is only ~19×15 pixels, with 68% of all annotations falling in the very-tiny or tiny size category, making this effectively a tiny-object detection problem. Evaluation follows AI-TOD metrics computing AP at IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, with separate scores for very-tiny, tiny, small, and medium objects.

## Method

The core contribution is a Lightness Focal (LF) loss that extends standard focal loss with two additional terms for negative samples: a false-positive focal term (1/(q_c+ε)) that polynomially increases the penalty as the predicted confidence for a non-light region rises, and a lightness spatial attention prior (η−φ(d_c)) that modulates the loss based on a pre-learned density map encoding where each light class geometrically tends to appear relative to vanishing points (e.g., streetlights above the horizon, carlights on the ground plane). This LF loss is embedded into a semi-supervised focal light detection (SS-FLD) framework following the Mean Teacher paradigm, where a teacher network generates pseudo-labels on weakly augmented unlabeled images and a student network is trained on both labeled and pseudo-labeled data with EMA parameter updates. The approach is architecture-agnostic and is demonstrated to consistently boost mAP across 20 different detector types (single-stage, two-stage, transformer, and tiny-object detectors) on the newly constructed YDLD benchmark of 3,516 images and 116k annotations.

## Results

On the YDLD benchmark, SS-FLD w/t RFLA achieves the best mAP of 26.0 (APvt 12.8, APt 24.8, APs 39.2), while SS-FLD w/t DINO achieves 25.6 mAP (APvt 12.7, APt 23.1, APs 38.6). The best non-SS-FLD detector is DINO at 22.6 mAP, followed by PAA and RFLA w/t PAA at 21.6 mAP. Two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN, Cascade R-CNN) score only 8.6–10.2 mAP, highlighting the extreme difficulty of multi-source light detection due to tiny object sizes (~19×15 px average) and appearance similarity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SS-FLD w/t RFLA (26.0 mAP) outperforms the strongest supervised baseline DINO (22.6) by 3.4 points and PAA (21.6) by 4.4 points, while SS-FLD w/t DINO (25.6) improves over DINO itself by 3.0 points. Against semi-supervised baselines, SS-FLD w/t RFLA beats ConsistentTeacher (19.1) by 6.9 and MeanTeacher (14.4) by 11.6 mAP. Among tiny-object detectors, SS-FLD w/t RFLA surpasses NWD-RKA (16.6) by 9.4 and RFLA w/t RetinaNet (10.8) by 15.2 mAP. Two-stage detectors (Faster R-CNN 8.9, Cascade R-CNN 10.2) and single-stage baselines without improved label assignment (YOLOF 5.7, RetinaNet 8.1) perform poorly, confirming that the tiny size (~68% of boxes are tiny/very-tiny) and appearance similarity of light sources make standard detection pipelines inadequate. The paper also demonstrates transfer-learning generality: DINO pre-trained on YDLD achieves 38.3 mAP on BDD100K, matching COCO pre-training (38.3) despite having only 1 overlapping class versus 9 for COCO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the light detection literature, which has been limited to binary (light vs. background) or single-source (sun glare) formulations, into a multi-source classification problem with three distinct light types (vehicle, traffic signal, streetlight). Methodologically, it builds on the focal loss framework (Lin et al., ICCV 2017) by adding a false-positive-specific penalty term and a pre-computed spatial attention prior derived from vanishing-point geometry, then embeds this modified loss into the Mean Teacher / Consistent Teacher semi-supervised object detection pipeline (Tarvainen & Valpola, NeurIPS 2017; Wang et al., CVPR 2023). The lightness spatial attention prior is a novel contribution that encodes domain-specific geometric priors (e.g., streetlights appear above the horizon, carlights below) into the loss function rather than the network architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The YDLD dataset fills a critical gap as the first public multi-source light detection benchmark, enabling reproducible research in a domain previously limited to proprietary or single-class datasets. The transfer learning experiment showing that YDLD pretraining (3.5k images, 1 overlapping class) matches COCO (123k images, 9 overlapping classes) on BDD100k suggests scene diversity and domain specificity can outweigh class overlap and scale for driving-related detection. However, the best mAP remains only ~26, indicating the task is far from solved; open questions include whether the lightness prior generalizes beyond flat-road driving, whether the approach extends to sunlight (excluded due to irregular shapes and glare), and whether pixel-level annotations for instance segmentation would unlock downstream tasks like vehicle behavior prediction.
