## Problem

While Zeng et al. showed that simple linear models can rival or outperform Transformer-based forecasters, the specific role of self-attention versus cross-attention within the Transformer architecture for time series forecasting remained unexamined. This paper isolates that question by asking whether self-attention is actually necessary, or whether a cross-attention-only design can both improve accuracy and reduce computational cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243626/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a past multivariate time series of length L (e.g., 96 or 512 steps), which is split into non-overlapping patches of size P under a channel-independence assumption, and produces T future values (96, 192, 336, or 720 steps). A distinctive design choice is that each future horizon is represented by its own learnable query vector, so predictions for different horizons are computed independently, yielding per-horizon attention maps that reveal which past patches inform each specific forecast.

## Method

CATS (Cross-Attention-only Time Series transformer) removes all self-attention layers from the standard Transformer and retains only cross-attention, where horizon-dependent learnable parameters serve as queries and the embedded input patches serve as keys and values. Three mechanisms drive the design: (1) future-as-query, which treats each forecasting step as an independent question about the past; (2) parameter sharing across horizons, where the embedding, multi-head attention, and projection layers are shared among all horizon queries, keeping the parameter count nearly constant (≈0.4M) regardless of T; and (3) query-adaptive masking, a stochastic-depth-style technique that randomly severs the direct MHA-to-LayerNorm path with probability p to prevent the model from overfitting to past data rather than attending to the horizon-specific query. The resulting time complexity is O(LT/P²), substantially lower than the O(L²) of most Transformer-based forecasters, and the architecture achieves state-of-the-art MSE on ETT, Weather, Electricity, and Traffic benchmarks with fewer parameters and lower GPU memory than PatchTST, TimeMixer, and DLinear.

## Results

On multivariate long-term forecasting (input length 96), CATS achieves the lowest MSE on Traffic across all horizons (0.421–0.484) and is competitive or best on Weather, Electricity, and ETT datasets. For example, on Electricity at horizon 96, CATS reaches MSE 0.149 vs. TimeMixer 0.153 and PatchTST 0.190; on Weather at horizon 96, CATS achieves 0.161 vs. TimeMixer 0.163. On the M4 short-term benchmark, CATS attains the best averaged SMAPE (11.701), MASE (1.557), and OWA (0.838), reducing MASE by 19.94% over PatchTST. CATS also maintains near-constant parameter count (~0.4M) regardless of input length, versus 7.9× growth for PatchTST at L=2880.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CATS clearly dominates on the high-dimensional Traffic dataset, achieving the lowest MSE at every horizon (e.g., 0.421 vs. TimeMixer 0.462 and PatchTST 0.526 at horizon 96). On Electricity, CATS wins at horizons 96–336 but is marginally outperformed by Crossformer at horizon 720 (0.219 vs. 0.217). On Weather, CATS ties TimeMixer at horizon 192 (both 0.208) and is slightly behind at 336 (0.264 vs. 0.251) and 720 (0.342 vs. 0.339), but leads at horizon 96. On the ETT average, CATS achieves the best MSE at all four horizons, with the gap widening for longer horizons (0.434 vs. 0.439 for TimeMixer at 720). On the M4 short-term benchmark, CATS beats TimeMixer by a narrow margin (MASE 1.557 vs. 1.559) but substantially outperforms PatchTST (MASE 1.557 vs. 1.945, a 19.94% reduction) and DLinear (1.557 vs. 2.095). Overall, CATS wins or ties on 14 of 16 long-term dataset-horizon combinations and achieves the best short-term results across all M4 metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

CATS sits at the intersection of two competing narratives in time series forecasting: the Transformer-based line (Informer, Autoformer, PatchTST, Crossformer) that relies heavily on self-attention, and the linear-model critique (DLinear, TimeMixer) that argues self-attention is unnecessary or harmful for temporal data. Rather than discarding attention entirely as DLinear does, CATS retains the cross-attention mechanism from the original Transformer while removing all self-attention layers, repurposing future horizon parameters as learnable queries against past patches as keys/values. The patching and channel-independence design is inherited from PatchTST, and the query-adaptive masking technique draws an explicit analogy to stochastic depth in residual networks, making this the first application of that regularization strategy in time series Transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, well-ablated argument that self-attention is not a necessary component for time series forecasting and can even degrade long-horizon performance, which reframes how practitioners think about Transformer design for sequential data. The parameter-sharing design (constant parameter count regardless of forecasting horizon) offers a practical efficiency advantage for deployment scenarios requiring variable-length predictions. A key limitation is the channel-independence assumption, which the authors acknowledge may not hold for highly correlated multivariate data; extending CATS to capture cross-variate dependencies without reintroducing quadratic complexity remains an open question. The modest but consistent performance gains over strong baselines like TimeMixer and PatchTST, combined with the efficiency story, make this a useful architectural baseline for future time series Transformer research.
