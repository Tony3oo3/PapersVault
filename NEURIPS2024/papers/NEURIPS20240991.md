## Problem

Popular multilingual benchmarks such as FLEURS and FLoRes-101 are fundamentally misaligned with the needs of low-resource, Indigenous language communities: they contain grammatical and orthographic errors, poor pronunciation, limited vocabulary, and culturally irrelevant content that fails to reflect how the language is actually spoken. For Māori specifically, the FLEURS dataset perpetuates incorrect macron usage, beginner-level grammar, and a Wikipedia-derived register that bears no resemblance to everyday or ceremonial Māori speech. The paper asks how a language community can build a benchmark that is genuinely fit for its own revitalisation goals rather than chasing metrics on generalised, machine-translated datasets.

## Input / Output

The ASR system consumes raw speech audio in three registers—Māori monolingual, NZ English, and natural bilingual code-switching—and produces aligned text transcriptions scored by word error rate. The benchmark utterances are deliberately shaped to mirror natural conversational speech: they include single- and two-word exchanges (e.g., 'ae' for agreement), overlapping speaker turns, slow native-speaker prosody with long breath pauses, and fast contemporary speech with minimal inter-sentence breaks. The benchmark comprises 4,835 sentences (89,277 words, 6,402 unique words) from 37 speakers, substantially larger and more lexically diverse than FLEURS's 1,211 sentences from 4 speakers.

## Method

The core contribution is Te Taumata, a community-curated benchmark structured into five sub-benchmarks—Native Sound (four speakers born before 1930), Second Language learners, First Language Contemporary (six speakers aged 30–65), NZ English (eight speakers with 'true Kiwi accents'), and Bilingual code-switching (thirteen speakers drawn from marae gatherings)—each targeting a distinct speaker population and prosodic profile. The ASR model itself is a fastformer conformer-rnnt-large from NVIDIA NeMo with a bilingual BPE tokenizer (1,024 vocabulary) and adaptive length synchronous decoding beam search, trained from scratch on approximately 400 hours of community-labelled radio archive data. Data curation followed a rigorous pipeline: one transcriber per audio segment, peer review by a senior language expert, and a written orthography guideline covering macrons, numbers, verbatim transcription, and utterance segmentation rules. The benchmark is evaluated both qualitatively (target-sentence comparison reports) and quantitatively (WER against sub-benchmarks and against OpenAI Whisper models), with the Native Sound sub-benchmark serving as the primary success metric aligned with the community's aspiration to restore native-speaker sound in technology.

## Results

The Māori ASR model (Conformer-RNNT-Large, 120M parameters) achieved a substantial WER reduction from 27% to 10% across successive iterations, evaluated on the Te Taumata benchmark. The Te Taumata benchmark comprises 4,835 sentences (89,277 words, 6,402 unique words) from 37 speakers, compared to FLEURS's 1,211 sentences (30,794 words, 2,033 unique words) from only 4 speakers. Model performance on the Native Sound sub-benchmark has been steadily improving, while performance on the FLEURS benchmark has progressively degraded as the model was optimised for culturally relevant speech patterns, demonstrating the misalignment of generalised benchmarks with community-specific goals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's ASR model is compared against OpenAI's Whisper models (Whisper-large-v2 and v3, 1,550M parameters; MMS, 1,000M parameters) using WER on both Te Taumata and FLEURS. The key finding is that as the Māori ASR model improved on the Native Sound sub-benchmark of Te Taumata, its WER on FLEURS worsened (Figure 6), indicating that FLEURS's machine-translated, Wikipedia-derived content does not reflect the speech patterns the community considers important. The Te Taumata benchmark outperforms FLEURS in vocabulary diversity (6,402 vs 2,033 unique words), speaker diversity (37 vs 4 speakers), and cultural relevance. The paper argues that the 120M-parameter Conformer model, trained on high-quality community data, is more appropriate for Māori ASR than the much larger multilingual Whisper models, which apply an 'Anglocentric lens' and lack native Māori sound representation. No single head-to-head WER table is provided; comparisons are presented through figures showing per-sub-benchmark WER trajectories across model iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of applying generalised, high-resource benchmarks (FLEURS, FLoRes-101, Whisper) to low-resource and Indigenous languages, arguing that such benchmarks are linguistically and culturally inappropriate for communities like Māori. Conceptually, it aligns with a growing body of critical work on data sovereignty, the 'saviour complex' in speech technology, and the inadequacy of Anglocentric evaluation (Nicholas & Bhatia, 2023; Markl et al., 2024; Raji et al., 2021), but translates those critiques into a concrete, community-curated benchmark (Te Taumata) with explicit sub-benchmarks for native sound, contemporary speakers, and bilingual code-switching. The underlying ASR model builds on standard Conformer/Transformer architectures (NeMo fastformer) and earlier Deepspeech work, so the technical contribution is in the evaluation methodology and data curation rather than model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a replicable template for other Indigenous and low-resource language communities to design culturally grounded benchmarks that reflect their speakers' diversity, linguistic characteristics, and revitalisation aspirations, rather than chasing metrics on externally imposed datasets. A notable finding is that improving the Māori ASR model on the community's Native Sound sub-benchmark correlated with worsening performance on FLEURS, concretely demonstrating the misalignment between universal benchmarks and community-relevant quality. Key limitations include regional dialectal bias (far north of NZ), male skew in the bilingual sub-benchmark, and the non-public release of the benchmark due to data sovereignty, which limits external reproducibility. Open questions include how to expand dialectal coverage, how to balance sovereignty with broader research collaboration, and whether this community-centric benchmarking approach can be generalised to other language contexts with even fewer resources.
