## Problem

Neural networks exhibit several apparently unpredictable generalization behaviors—double descent, grokking, linear mode connectivity, and underperformance on tabular data—that resist explanation from standard capacity-based complexity measures. Existing linearized models (e.g., the neural tangent kernel) either assume gradients remain constant throughout training (lazy regime) or only approximate the final model in one step, missing the dynamics of how learning actually unfolds. The paper asks whether a more faithful, incrementally linearized model of the training trajectory can serve as a practical analytical lens for these phenomena.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fully trained neural network together with its complete training trajectory (model gradients ∇_θ f_θ_{t-1}(x) computed at every step t for every training and test example), plus the training labels and test inputs. It produces (i) an approximate prediction ũ_θ_T(x) via the telescoping sum, (ii) effective parameter counts p_s^0 computed separately on train and test data (the latter without needing test labels), and (iii) a time-varying tangent kernel K_t^T(x, x_i) that encodes the optimizer's effect on functional updates. The computational cost is O(T·n·p) gradient evaluations, which is feasible for moderate-width networks but prohibitive for very large models without further approximations.

## Method

The core contribution is a telescoping model (Eq. 5) that expresses a trained network as f_θ0(x) + Σ_t ∇_θ f_θ_{t-1}(x)^T Δθ_t, approximating each per-batch functional update with a first-order Taylor expansion rather than linearizing the entire trajectory around θ_0. This incremental approach is orders of magnitude more accurate than a single linearization and does not require the lazy-training assumption of constant gradients. From this representation, the paper derives three key analytical tools: (1) a smoother expression ũ_θ_t(x) = s_θ_t(x)·y + c_θ_t^0(x) that enables computation of the effective parameter measure p_s^0 on train vs. test data, quantifying benign overfitting in double descent and grokking; (2) a kernel representation structurally identical to gradient boosting (differing only in the kernel), revealing that the unboundedness of tangent kernels relative to bounded tree kernels explains GBTs' advantage on irregular tabular data; and (3) a sufficient condition for linear mode connectivity showing that gradient stabilization after a checkpoint t* makes weight-averaged and ensemble predictions near-identical. The framework also yields a pedagogical formalism for decomposing the functional-update effects of momentum, weight decay, adaptive learning rates, and output activations.

## Results

The telescoping model approximates trained neural networks with negligible error for small learning rates, improving over the standard linearization (first-order expansion around θ₀) by orders of magnitude. In the double descent experiment on CIFAR-10, the effective parameter count p_s^train increases monotonically with model width, while p_s^test decreases past the interpolation threshold, quantitatively resolving the apparent contradiction with the classical U-curve. In grokking experiments (polynomial regression and MNIST with large initialization), the delayed test-error improvement coincides precisely with the divergence of p_s^train and p_s^test, providing a measurable signature of benign overfitting. In the NN-vs-GBT comparison on the Houses dataset, GBTs outperform neural networks even without irregular examples, and the relative MSE gap widens monotonically as the proportion of irregular (far-from-centroid) test inputs increases, tracked by the unbounded growth of the tangent kernel norm ||k_t^θ(x)||₂ while the tree kernel norm remains bounded in [1/√n, 1].

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's most direct method comparison is between neural networks and gradient boosted trees (GBTs) on standard tabular benchmarks (Houses, plus additional datasets in the appendix). GBTs outperform neural networks in the absence of irregular examples, indicating that the tree kernel's inductive bias is better suited to the outcome-generating process of tabular data. As the proportion of irregular test inputs (those farthest from the PCA centroid) increases, the relative MSE gap (MSE_NN^p − MSE_GBT^p)/(MSE_NN^0 − MSE_GBT^0) grows monotonically, confirming that dataset irregularity disproportionately harms neural networks. The paper attributes this to a structural difference: tree kernels satisfy 0 ≤ K(x,x_i) ≤ 1 and Σ_i K(x,x_i) = 1 for all x (bounded norm in [1/√n, 1]), while neural tangent kernels are unbounded and can behave very differently on inputs not seen during training. This kernel-boundedness difference, made visible by the telescoping model's shared kernel-representation structure (Eq. 5 vs. Eq. 9), provides a new mechanistic explanation for the well-known GBT advantage on tabular data. The paper does not propose a new method that beats GBTs; rather, it provides an analytical lens that explains when and why the gap emerges.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural tangent kernel (NTK) and linearized-model literature (Jacot et al. 2018; Chizat et al. 2019) by departing from the standard lazy-training assumption: rather than applying a single first-order expansion around initialization, it telescopes out first-order approximations incrementally at each SGD step, yielding a much tighter approximation of the true trained network. It also directly builds on Curth et al. (2023), who introduced an effective-parameter complexity measure p_s^0 for non-deep models (linear regression, trees, boosting), by showing how the telescoping representation makes this measure computable for neural networks. The paper positions itself as a hybrid empirical-theoretical tool, using the kernel representation to draw a structural parallel between neural network learning and gradient boosting that had not been previously articulated in this form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The telescoping model provides a unified, computable lens through which several prominent deep learning puzzles (double descent, grokking, linear mode connectivity, GBT-vs-NN gaps) can be re-examined with quantitative metrics rather than qualitative narratives. The structural identity between the NN telescoping model and gradient boosting (differing only in kernel) offers a clean mechanistic explanation for why GBTs handle irregular tabular features better: tree kernels are bounded and sum-to-one, while tangent kernels are unbounded. Key limitations include the computational cost of computing model gradients at every training step (prohibitive for very large networks), the dependence of approximation quality on learning rate, and the explicit acknowledgment that gradient stabilization does not fully explain LMC (BatchNorm parameters remain unstable). Open questions include whether the framework scales to modern architectures, whether the train/test effective-parameter divergence is a necessary or merely correlated signal for grokking, and whether the kernel-boundedness argument generalizes beyond the specific tabular benchmarks tested.
