## Problem

Rejection-based data synthesis for mathematical instruction tuning samples an equal number of raw responses per query, but difficult queries are far less likely to yield correct answers, causing the final training distribution to be heavily biased toward easy problems. The authors show that 51.1% of the most difficult MATH queries receive zero synthetic responses in MetaMathQA, and responses to Level-5 queries account for only 10.5% of all samples. This under-representation of hard queries likely hinders learning of complex reasoning, yet no prior method explicitly controls the difficulty distribution of the synthesized data.

## Input / Output

The synthesis pipeline takes fixed mathematical problem queries from the GSM8K and MATH training splits as input and produces chain-of-thought responses generated by DeepSeekMath-7B-RL (a 7B open-weight model), filtered for answer correctness via a regex + SymPy grading pipeline. The output is two synthetic instruction-tuning datasets of ~590k (query, response) pairs each, augmented with the original GSM8K/MATH training data. These datasets are then used to fine-tune base models (Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B, Llama3-70B, DeepSeekMath-7B) via standard supervised instruction tuning, yielding DART-Math models that produce natural-language mathematical solutions at inference time.

## Method

DART introduces Difficulty-Aware Rejection Sampling (DARS) with two strategies that explicitly control the final response distribution across query difficulty. Uniform samples responses until every query accumulates k_u=40 correct responses, equalizing coverage; Prop2Diff samples until the number of correct responses per query is linearly proportional to its difficulty score (k_p=192 for the hardest queries), deliberately over-representing difficult problems. Difficulty is quantified by a 'fail rate' metric—the fraction of incorrect responses over n_d sampled trials—which also allows reusing those samples as synthetic data. A maximum raw-sample cap (n_max) prevents infinite sampling on pathological queries, and Prop2Diff guarantees at least one synthetic response per query to preserve easy-problem coverage. All synthesis is performed solely with the 7B DeepSeekMath-7B-RL model using temperature-1.6 top-p sampling via vLLM, eliminating dependence on proprietary models like GPT-4, and the resulting ~590k-sample datasets are substantially smaller than prior SOTA datasets while outperforming them across six benchmarks.

## Results

Across 6 mathematical benchmarks (MATH, GSM8K, CollegeMath, DeepMind-Mathematics, OlympiadBench-Math, TheoremQA), DART-Math-Llama3-8B (Prop2Diff) achieves 46.6% on MATH and 39.7% average accuracy using only 0.59M training samples, improving over the same-size vanilla rejection tuning (VRT) baseline by 4.5 points on average. On the math-specialized DeepSeekMath-7B base, DART-Math (Prop2Diff) reaches 49.4% average, matching the RL-trained DeepSeekMath-7B-RL (49.3%) using only supervised fine-tuning. The 70B variant (DART-Math-Llama3-70B Prop2Diff) achieves 56.1% on MATH and 49.3% average, outperforming the 2.3M-sample MMIQC baseline (45.9%) with less than a quarter of the training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the same-size vanilla rejection tuning (VRT) baseline (0.59M samples), DART-Math (Prop2Diff) wins on 5 of 6 benchmarks for Llama3-8B, improving MATH by +6.9 points (39.7→46.6) and the 6-benchmark average by +4.5 points (35.2→39.7); gains are largest on harder OOD benchmarks (CollegeMath +4.9, DeepMind +6.3, Olympiad +5.2). Compared to MetaMath (0.40M, GPT-3.5-synthesized), DART-Math wins decisively in all cases — e.g., Llama3-8B average jumps from 30.8 to 39.7. Against MMIQC (2.3M, GPT-4+GPT-3.5+human), DART-Math (Prop2Diff) surpasses it by 4.1 points on Llama3-8B average (39.7 vs 35.6) while using only ~26% of the training data. Versus Xwin-Math (1.4M, GPT-4), DART-Math (Prop2Diff) leads on all challenging benchmarks (MATH, College, DM, Olympiad, Theorem) though Xwin-Math is based on the older Llama2 architecture. Against KPMath-Plus (1.6M, GPT-4, not open-sourced), DART-Math-Mistral-7B slightly underperforms on GSM8K (81.1 vs 82.1) and MATH (45.5 vs 46.8), but DART-Math-DSMath-7B substantially outperforms it on MATH (53.6 vs 48.8) using roughly one-third the data. All DART-Math results remain well below GPT-4-Turbo (73.4% MATH), confirming the gap between open-weight instruction-tuned models and proprietary systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rejection sampling/tuning paradigm for mathematical instruction tuning (established by MetaMath, Yuan et al., and others) by identifying and correcting a systematic bias: vanilla rejection sampling allocates equal raw samples per query, which implicitly starves difficult queries of correct responses. Unlike ToRA and MARIO, which improve coverage without explicitly controlling the final response distribution, DART prescribes a target number of correct responses per query (uniform or proportional to difficulty), directly shaping the training data distribution. A key departure from prior work is the exclusive use of a 7B open-weight model (DeepSeekMath-7B-RL) for both difficulty assessment and response synthesis, eliminating the reliance on proprietary GPT-4 that characterizes most competing datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DART-Math datasets (~590k samples) are positioned as the most cost-effective public resources for math instruction tuning, outperforming larger GPT-4-based datasets (MetaMathQA at 395k, MMIQC at 2.3M) with a fraction of the synthesis cost. The finding that the difficulty bias narrows for stronger base models (DeepSeekMath-7B, Llama3-70B) suggests that for well-pretrained models, query coverage rather than response quality becomes the bottleneck, pointing toward query augmentation as a complementary direction. Limitations include restriction to natural-language reasoning without code execution, the use of fail rate as a potentially suboptimal difficulty proxy, and the one-time synthesis cost (~160 A100 GPU-days) that, while amortizable, is non-trivial. Whether DART's distributional control generalizes to code-based math solving or other reasoning domains remains open.
