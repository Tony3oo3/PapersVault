## Problem

Existing genetic variant (GV) datasets for deep learning are limited in scale, diversity, and biological complexity, often reducing variant interpretation to binary pathogenicity/benignity classification that ignores tissue-specific effects, penetrance, and multi-variant interactions. There is no standardized, large-scale benchmark that captures the full spectrum of GV effects across cell types, tissues, and experimental contexts, making it unclear how well genomic foundation models (GFMs) can represent and classify unknown variants.

## Input / Output

Each GV record is a (reference sequence, alternate sequence) pair centered on the variant with user-defined context length (tested from short to 1024+ bp on both flanks), accompanied by structured annotations (chromosome, position, variant type, cell/tissue context). The dataset yields 28.3M (x, y) pairs where y ranges over discrete labels (four-class pathogenicity, disease/trait classification, sQTL significance, gene-KO cancer driver status) and continuous targets (gene-KO fitness scores, MAVE effect scores). Downstream, finetuned GFMs produce fixed-dimensional vectors usable for FAISS-based nearest-neighbor indexing against the 155 clinician-verified GV anchors.

## Method

GV-Rep is constructed by extracting GV records from seven public databases (ClinVar, Cell Passport, Project Score, GTEx eQTLs/sQTLs, GWAS Catalog, MAVEDB, OLIDA) plus a clinician-verified set of 155 variants from 84 patients, then unifying them into a standardized (ref, alt, annotation) → label triplet format with a sequence extractor that maps each variant to its hg38 genomic context. For experimentation, four pretrained GFMs (HyenaDNA, DNABERT2, Nucleotide Transformer, NTv2) are frozen and fine-tuned with a three-layer CNN header using Adam at lr=1e-3 across three task types: four-class pathogenicity (1.3M ClinVar GVs), binary sQTL significance, and gene-KO fitness regression. The key evaluation insight is that while GFMs achieve ~65–74% AUROC on conventional pathogenicity classification, they perform near-random on cell-specific sQTL and gene-KO tasks, revealing a significant gap in current representation quality. For indexing, the paper measures whether top-10 FAISS neighbors of a query GV share its cancer-predisposition class label, showing that finetuning consistently improves indexing accuracy over pretrained encoders.

## Results

On the ClinVar 4-class pathogenicity task (1.3M GVs, 1024 bp context), DNABERT2 achieves the best AUROC of 73.87%, followed by NT-V2 (68.73%), NT-Human (65.75%), and HyenaDNA (65.05%), all substantially above random (50.59%). However, on the more challenging sQTL splicing-significance task, all models perform near random chance (50.50%), with NT-V2 marginally best at 54.10%. On the Gene-KO fitness regression task, all four GFMs converge to an identical MSE of 1.06 (vs. random 1.11), indicating the models fail to learn meaningful cell-type-specific representations. For GV indexing on clinician-verified variants, finetuned HyenaDNA achieves the highest accuracy (0.662), outperforming its pretrained counterpart (0.428) and all other finetuned models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four pre-trained GFMs (HyenaDNA, DNABERT2, Nucleotide Transformer, NT-V2) finetuned on GV-Rep across three tasks of increasing biological complexity. On the standard ClinVar pathogenicity classification, DNABERT2 clearly leads (73.87% AUROC), outperforming NT-V2 by ~5 points and HyenaDNA by ~9 points, suggesting its multi-species pre-training provides a stronger inductive bias for variant effect prediction. On the sQTL splicing task, all models are essentially at chance level (51–54% AUROC vs. 50.5% random), with NT-V2 showing the smallest edge; this indicates that current GFMs cannot capture tissue-specific regulatory effects of splicing variants. The Gene-KO regression task is the most revealing: all four models collapse to the same MSE (1.06), barely below random (1.11), implying the CNN head learns a trivial constant rather than encoding cell-type-specific gene fitness. For the indexing task on 155 clinician-verified GVs, finetuning consistently improves all models, with HyenaDNA achieving the best finetuned accuracy (0.662 vs. 0.428 pretrained), while NT-V2 actually degrades after finetuning (0.542 vs. 0.478), suggesting its representations are less amenable to task-specific adaptation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground as a dataset contribution rather than a model contribution, unifying seven previously fragmented GV databases (ClinVar, GTEx, Project Score, GWAS, MAVEDB, OLIDA, Cell Passport) into a single standardized format with 7.5M+ records. It departs from prior GV benchmarks (BEND, GUE, GUANinE) that predominantly focus on binary pathogenicity classification with short, fixed context lengths, by introducing cell-type-specific, tissue-specific, multi-variant interaction, and gene-knockout contexts alongside a clinician-verified anchor set. The paper repurposes existing GFMs (DNABERT2, HyenaDNA, Nucleotide Transformer) as evaluation tools rather than building new architectures, positioning the dataset as the primary contribution that exposes gaps in current representation learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset addresses a critical gap: the absence of a large-scale, multi-context, standardized GV benchmark that goes beyond binary pathogenicity. The finding that GFMs perform near-random on sQTL classification (~52-54% AUROC) and that Gene-KO predictions collapse to a trivial solution across three architectures reveals a fundamental limitation in current genomic representation learning for context-dependent regulatory effects. Open questions include how to incorporate epigenetic and epistatic signals, how to handle multi-variant interactions at scale, and how to ensure fairness across underrepresented populations. The dataset is likely to become a standard evaluation resource for the GFM community.
