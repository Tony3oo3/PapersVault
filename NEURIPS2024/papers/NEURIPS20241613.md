## Problem

Despite extensive theoretical work on decomposing predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components, no prior study has empirically verified whether these decompositions actually yield disentangled estimators in practice. Existing large-scale benchmarks evaluate methods on only one uncertainty component without testing for undesirable side effects on others, leaving it unknown which component(s) each method truly captures.

## Input / Output

All methods consume RGB images (ImageNet-1k at 224×224 with ResNet-50, CIFAR-10 at 32×32 with WideResNet-28-10) and produce either a second-order predictive distribution q(π|x) over class probability vectors (distributional methods) or a direct scalar uncertainty value u(x) (deterministic methods). Distributional methods are further aggregated into scalar estimates using 14 different aggregators (entropy, variance, disagreement, Dempster-Shafer, etc.), and decomposition formulas (IT, Bregman) split the second-order distribution into paired aleatoric and epistemic estimates. Evaluation spans 13 practical tasks including OOD detection AUROC, correctness prediction AUROC, abstained prediction AUAC, ECE calibration, and rank correlation with human-annotated aleatoric ground truths from ImageNet-ReaL and CIFAR-10H.

## Method

The paper reimplements 19 uncertainty quantification methods (Bayesian, evidential, ensemble, GP, and deterministic) in up to 14 aggregator configurations and evaluates each on 13 tasks across ImageNet-1k and CIFAR-10, using pretrained ResNet-50 backbones trained for 50 epochs with 5 seeds (1.5 GPU-years on RTX 2080 Ti). The central finding is that both the information-theoretical and Bregman decomposition formulas produce aleatoric and epistemic estimates with rank correlations ≥ 0.78 across all distributional methods, violating a necessary condition for disentanglement. The authors further show that the inevitable dataset-level correlation (measured via GT aleatoric vs. epistemic) is at most 0.45, confirming the high correlations are formula shortcomings rather than data properties. A promising path forward is combining separate specialized estimators—e.g., Mahalanobis for epistemic and CE baseline entropy for aleatoric—which achieves a low inter-estimate correlation of 0.15±0.01 while each performs well on its respective task.

## Results

The headline finding is that no decomposition formula (information-theoretical or Bregman) achieves uncertainty disentanglement in practice: the rank correlation between aleatoric and epistemic estimates is ≥ 0.78 for all 12 distributional methods on ImageNet (range [0.78, 0.99]) and ≥ 0.88 for 11/12 on CIFAR-10, far exceeding the inevitable dataset-level correlation of at most 0.45. On the OOD detection task (epistemic proxy), Mahalanobis achieves the best AUROC while DDU is worst (AUROC = 0.675). For aleatoric uncertainty, all methods cluster within a rank correlation of [0.37, 0.46] with ImageNet-ReaL ground-truth label entropies, indicating the task is far from saturated. Predictive uncertainty tasks (AUAC, correctness AUROC) show saturating performance: nearly all methods exceed AUAC 0.91 and fall within ±0.014 of the cross-entropy baseline on correctness prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mahalanobis [30] is the clear winner on OOD detection (epistemic task) due to its specialized training on ImageNet-C severity-2 corruptions, but it is uncorrelated with aleatoric uncertainty (rank corr. ≈ 0), making it a strong candidate for a disentangled pair with the CE baseline (combined rank corr. = 0.15 ± 0.01). DDU [37], which combines spectral normalization with a latent Gaussian mixture, performs worst on OOD detection (AUROC = 0.675) despite its similar distance-aware design. Evidential methods EDL [45] and PostNet [4] are considerably better than all other methods at correctness prediction (AUROC) but lose their advantage on abstained prediction (AUAC) because their lower classification accuracy drags down the metric. HET-XL [6] and other modern distributional methods do not outperform older approaches like Deep Ensemble [29] or MC Dropout [12] on predictive uncertainty tasks. Laplace Approximation [8] and Temperature Scaling [18] are only as good as the CE baseline on correctness AUROC but show drastically improved ECE, demonstrating that task-specific tailoring (using a validation set) matters more than the method's theoretical sophistication. Shallow Ensemble [31] is the best distributional method on OOD detection, while DUQ [53] could only be evaluated on CIFAR-10 due to memory and stability issues on ImageNet. The IT and Bregman decomposition components, when used as epistemic/aleatoric estimators, underperform the best-performing aggregators for the same second-order distributions, confirming that theoretically intuitive decompositions do not translate to practical disentanglement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by being the first systematic benchmark of uncertainty *disentanglement*—the question of whether a pair of estimators truly captures two distinct sources of uncertainty. Prior work on decomposition formulas (information-theoretical [11, 57] and Bregman [42, 19]) has been primarily theoretical or supported by small-scale qualitative experiments, while larger-scale benchmarks [13, 40] evaluated methods on only one uncertainty component without testing for side effects on others. This paper repurposes 19 existing methods (from Bayesian, evidential, ensemble, and deterministic families) under a unified 13-task evaluation protocol on ImageNet-1k and CIFAR-10, shifting the focus from 'which method is best overall' to 'which method captures which specific type of uncertainty, and do proposed pairs actually unmix?'.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no tested decomposition formula yields disentangled estimators (rank correlations ≥ 0.78 between the supposed aleatoric and epistemic components)—challenges the field's current theoretical direction and suggests that combining separate specialized methods (e.g., Mahalanobis for epistemic + CE baseline for aleatoric, achieving rank corr. 0.15) is a more promising path forward. The paper also reveals that predictive uncertainty tasks are saturated while specialized tasks (OOD detection, aleatoric alignment) remain far from solved, providing practitioners with concrete task-specific guidance. Key limitations include evaluation on only two classification datasets (constrained by the availability of multi-annotator ground truths for aleatoric uncertainty) and a focus on fully converged models, leaving open whether findings transfer to regression, unsupervised settings, or data-limited regimes where epistemic uncertainty is more pronounced.
