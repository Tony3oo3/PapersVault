## Problem

Large language models consistently fail at implicit reasoning over their parametric knowledge—composing internalized facts or comparing entity attributes—yet it remains unclear whether this reflects a fundamental transformer limitation or a training/data-distribution issue. This paper asks whether transformers can acquire such reasoning skills at all, under what training conditions they emerge, and what internal mechanisms underlie the observed (or absent) systematic generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243016/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For composition, the model receives a token sequence (head entity, first relation, second relation) and predicts the tail entity; for comparison, it receives (entity₁, attribute, entity₂) and predicts the comparative label (younger/older/contemporary). Each entity, relation, attribute, and value is assigned a unique token, and training data is a controlled mixture of atomic facts (triplets) and inferred facts deduced from them via latent rules, with a tunable inferred-to-atomic ratio φ. Evaluation splits into in-distribution (unseen inferred facts from known entities) and out-of-distribution (inferred facts from held-out entities) to separately measure rule acquisition and systematicity.

## Method

The paper trains standard GPT-2-style decoder-only transformers (8 layers, 768 hidden, 12 heads) from scratch on synthetic atomic/inferred fact mixtures and observes that robust generalization emerges only through grokking—extended training far beyond the point of overfitting. Using logit lens and causal tracing across training checkpoints, the authors identify distinct generalizing circuits: composition yields a sequential circuit where atomic facts are stored in separate lower and upper layer regions (preventing OOD generalization), while comparison yields a parallel circuit where facts are stored and retrieved in the same region (enabling systematicity). Controlled experiments show that the inferred/atomic ratio φ, not absolute data size, governs grokking speed, consistent with a circuit-efficiency explanation where the generalizing circuit becomes relatively cheaper as φ grows. Finally, on a complex reasoning task with a large search space requiring multi-step transitive inference, a fully grokked transformer achieves 99.3% accuracy while GPT-4-Turbo and Gemini-1.5-Pro (with or without retrieval augmentation and chain-of-thought prompting) score at or near random.

## Results

On the complex reasoning task with a large search space, the fully grokked transformer achieves 99.3% accuracy, while GPT-4-Turbo reaches only 33.3% (Direct+R) and 31.3% (CoT+R), and Gemini-1.5-Pro reaches at most 37.3% (Direct+R) — all at or near the 33.3% random-guess baseline. On the composition task, the grokked transformer achieves near-perfect in-distribution generalization after ~50× the steps needed to fit training data, but out-of-distribution accuracy remains at 0% even after 2M steps. On the comparison task, the grokked transformer achieves both ID and OOD (systematic) generalization, demonstrating that the architecture can support systematicity when the learned circuit stores atomic facts in a shared region.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares a fully grokked 8-layer transformer against GPT-4-Turbo and Gemini-1.5-Pro on a complex reasoning task requiring multi-step deduction over a large fact base. The grokked transformer achieves 99.3% accuracy, massively outperforming both LLMs by 62+ points. GPT-4-Turbo (tested only with retrieval augmentation due to context limits) scores 33.3% (Direct+R) and 31.3% (CoT+R), essentially at random-guess level. Gemini-1.5-Pro's best setting is Direct+R at 37.3%, only marginally above random, while its CoT settings collapse to 11.3–12.0%. Notably, chain-of-thought prompting makes Gemini perform worse (70.7% of CoT responses conclude the answer is undecidable), and most CoT rationales that happen to reach the correct answer contain hallucinated facts or logical errors. This demonstrates that non-parametric memory (long context or retrieval) is insufficient for deep multi-step reasoning over large knowledge bases, whereas parametric memory in a grokked transformer enables near-perfect performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the grokking phenomenon—originally discovered on small algorithmic tasks (Power et al., 2022) and subsequently explained via circuit efficiency (Varma et al., 2023)—into the domain of knowledge-based reasoning, where it had not been previously observed. It departs from prior grokking explanations by showing that the critical data *distribution* (specifically the inferred/atomic fact ratio), rather than critical data *size*, governs generalization speed, directly contradicting the CDS hypothesis of Liu et al. (2023) and Zhu et al. (2024). The mechanistic analysis builds on logit lens and causal tracing tools (nostalgebraist, 2020; Meng et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023) but applies them to track circuit formation *throughout* grokking, connecting the resulting circuit topology to the level of systematicity achieved. The composition and comparison tasks connect to the well-documented compositionality gap in LLMs (Press et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024) and the known failure of GPT-4 on entity comparison (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023), providing a mechanistic account for why these failures persist.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of *why* transformers fail to systematically generalize on composition (sequential circuit with duplicated knowledge across layers) but succeed on comparison (parallel circuit with shared knowledge), pointing toward concrete architectural fixes such as cross-layer parameter sharing or explicit recurrence. The correction of the critical-data-size hypothesis to a critical-data-distribution one has broad implications for how practitioners design training data to induce generalization. The demonstration that a small fully-grokked transformer (99.3%) outperforms GPT-4-Turbo and Gemini-1.5-Pro (≤37.3%) on a complex multi-hop reasoning task highlights the unique compressive power of parametric memory, though the synthetic setting limits direct transfer to open-domain LLMs. Open questions include how these findings scale to real-world knowledge graphs, how to optimally balance parametric and non-parametric memory, and whether the identified circuit configurations generalize beyond the two reasoning types studied.
