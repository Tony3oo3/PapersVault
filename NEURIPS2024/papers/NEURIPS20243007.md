## Problem

Previous theoretical analyses of Adam either showed that momentum worsens convergence or that Adam offers no advantage over SGD, failing to explain why Adam's core components—momentum, discounting factors, and adaptive learning rates—are effective in practice. Additionally, model EMA, widely used in training generative models and language models, lacked a theoretical justification in the nonconvex optimization setting. This paper provides a unified framework that explains the effectiveness of both Adam and EMA for nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization.

## Input / Output

The algorithm operates on a differentiable loss function F: R^d → R accessed only through a stochastic gradient oracle STOGRAD(x, r) with bounded variance σ², starting from an initial point x_0. The output is the model exponential moving average x̄_t, shown to be a (λ, ε)-stationary point (or (λ, ε)-L₁-stationary point in the coordinate-wise variant), where stationarity is defined via an infimum over all distributions p centered at the output of ‖E[∇F(y)]‖ + λ·E‖y − x‖². Unlike standard nonconvex analyses that randomly select an iterate as the final output, the guarantee is specifically on the EMA, which is the quantity actually deployed in practice.

## Method

The core mechanism is a discounted-to-nonconvex conversion where each increment z_t is chosen by an online learner minimizing discounted regret, with the step size randomized as α_t ~ Exp(1) to make the first-order Taylor approximation exact in expectation. The online learner is β-FTRL, a discounted version of scale-free FTRL that substitutes v_t ← β^{-t} g_t into the FTRL update, which algebraically yields the Clipped-Adam rule with β₁ = β and β₂ = β². The model EMA x̄_t emerges naturally from the discounting in the conversion, providing a theoretical explanation for its practical use. When applied coordinate-wise, the method automatically adapts to per-coordinate Lipschitz constants and variance, achieving a d-fold improvement in iteration complexity when coordinate scales are heterogeneous. The resulting guarantees are minimax optimal for both smooth (O(ε^{-4})) and nonsmooth (O(ε^{-7/2})) nonconvex settings.

## Results

The paper establishes that CLIPPED-ADAM with model EMA achieves the optimal iteration complexity of O((G+σ)²Δλ^(1/2)ε^(-7/2)) for finding a (λ,ε)-stationary point in the nonsmooth nonconvex setting (Corollary 12), matching the known lower bound. For smooth settings, this translates to the optimal O(ε^(-4)) complexity for L-smooth functions and O(ε^(-7/2)) for second-order smooth functions (Corollary 13). When G and σ are unknown, the gradient-adaptive regret bound yields O((G+σ)^(7/2)Δλ^(1/2)ε^(-7/2)), which is a factor of (G+σ)^(7/2) better than the non-adaptive approach of Zhang and Cutkosky [2024]. In the coordinate-wise heterogeneous setting, CLIPPED-ADAM achieves a factor-of-d improvement over the non-adaptive method (Section 6.3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's main comparison is theoretical rather than empirical. Against the non-adaptive approach of Zhang and Cutkosky [2024] (which uses online gradient descent in the discounted-to-nonconvex conversion), CLIPPED-ADAM's gradient-adaptive regret bound improves the dependence on (G+σ) from (G+σ)⁷ to (G+σ)^(7/2) when G and σ are unknown — a multiplicative factor of (G+σ)^(7/2) improvement. Against previous Adam convergence analyses (Reddi et al. 2018, Li et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023), this paper is the first to show that momentum and discounting factors are *essential* to the convergence guarantee rather than detrimental, directly contradicting prior results where Adam's rate worsens with momentum or matches SGD. In the coordinate-wise heterogeneous setting (Section 6.3), the coordinate-wise CLIPPED-ADAM (Theorem 18) achieves a factor-of-d improvement over applying the non-adaptive Theorem 11 with the L₁-to-L₂ reduction (Lemma 16), demonstrating the provable benefit of Adam's per-coordinate adaptivity when a few coordinates dominate the gradient scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes three recent theoretical threads: the online-to-nonconvex conversion framework of Cutkosky et al. (2023), the discounted-to-nonconvex conversion with random scaling of Zhang and Cutkosky (2024), and the scale-free FTRL perspective on Adam of Ahn et al. (2024b). The key conceptual move is plugging scale-free FTRL into the discounted-to-nonconvex conversion, which naturally yields clipped Adam with model EMA as the resulting optimizer. This departs sharply from prior Adam analyses (Reddi et al., Li et al., Wang et al.), which either showed momentum degrades convergence or that Adam offers no advantage over SGD, by demonstrating that momentum, adaptive learning rates, and EMA are all provably essential for achieving optimal nonconvex rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical analysis of Adam that correctly accounts for all its practical components—momentum, coordinate-wise adaptive learning rates, and model EMA—showing each is necessary for optimal convergence in nonconvex nonsmooth settings. The coordinate-wise adaptivity result (a factor-of-d improvement when gradient scales are heterogeneous) provides a clean theoretical justification for why Adam outperforms SGD in high-dimensional neural network training. Open questions include whether the specific parameter relationship β₁=β, β₂=β² can be reconciled with the standard practical choices (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999), whether the adaptive complexity O((G+σ)^{7/2}Δλ^{1/2}ε^{-7/2}) is optimal when G and σ are unknown, and whether more sophisticated online learners for discounted regret could yield algorithms that surpass Adam in practice.
