## Problem

Unsupervised visual concept learning faces a fundamental tension: continuous encodings are expressive but hard to interpret, while discrete encodings are interpretable but difficult to learn without supervision. No existing framework provides concept representations that are simultaneously learned without labels, structured at the object-factor level, and inherently inspectable and revisable by human stakeholders for trustworthy deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242292/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

NCB takes unlabeled RGB images (CLEVR-style synthetic scenes with geometric objects) and produces two complementary representations: continuous block-slot encodings (N_S slots × N_B blocks × D_B dimensions) and discrete concept-slot encodings (N_S × N_B integer concept IDs). For the CLEVR-Sudoku task, input is a partially filled 9×9 grid of single-object CLEVR images plus 1–10 example images per digit showing the attribute-to-digit mapping; output is the solved digit grid. The retrieval corpus—a structured set of (prototype/exemplar encoding, concept ID) tuples per block—serves as the inspectable, editable intermediate artifact between the two representation types.

## Method

NCB combines two binding stages: a soft binder (SysBinder block-slot attention) that learns continuous object-factor encodings via unsupervised image reconstruction (MSE loss), and a hard binder that discretizes these encodings through HDBSCAN clustering followed by retrieval-based nearest-neighbor lookup in a distilled corpus. Training proceeds in two steps: first optimizing the block-slot encoder for reconstruction, then fitting per-block HDBSCAN clusterings and extracting representative prototypes and exemplars into the retrieval corpus. At inference, each block-slot encoding is mapped to its nearest corpus entry, yielding a discrete concept ID per object-factor. The key architectural insight is that the retrieval corpus acts as an explicit, editable symbolic memory: humans or external models (e.g., GPT-4) can merge, delete, or add concept entries without retraining the encoder, and the continuous soft-binder space remains accessible for downstream tasks that require it.

## Results

On CLEVR-Easy and CLEVR, NCB's discrete concept-slot encodings achieve 99.02±1.00% and 97.26±2.67% object-property classification accuracy (N=2000 training samples), nearly matching the continuous SysBinder baseline (99.83% and 98.86%) while dramatically outperforming all other discrete methods. In the low-data regime (N=20), NCB retains 94.22% and 88.57% accuracy, whereas SysBinder (argmin) drops to 62.61% and 49.79%, and NLOTM to 37.05% and 41.52%. On CLEVR-Sudoku, NCB's unsupervised encodings enable solving ~50% of puzzles with 10 candidate examples, compared to 0% for SysBinder (unsupervised) and ~61% for the supervised slot attention baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the continuous SysBinder baseline, NCB's discrete encodings are slightly lower at full data (99.02% vs. 99.83% on CLEVR-Easy, 97.26% vs. 98.86% on CLEVR) but substantially more robust in the low-data regime, maintaining >94% accuracy at N=20 where SysBinder (cont.) drops to 64.88% and 79.11%. Against all discrete SysBinder variants (argmin, hard, step), NCB wins decisively: SysBinder (hard) is essentially non-functional (22.92%/36.46%), while SysBinder (argmin) and (step) trail NCB by 6–35 points depending on dataset and sample size. NLOTM is consistently the weakest baseline, achieving only 37–54% accuracy across settings. On CLEVR-Sudoku, NCB (unsupervised) solves ~50% of puzzles with 10 examples, trailing the supervised slot attention baseline (~61%) but vastly outperforming SysBinder (unsupervised) which solves 0%. On CLEVR-Hans3 classification, NCB+NN matches the supervised SA+NN baseline in accuracy while providing inherently inspectable concept-level explanations.

## Relation to Prior Work

NCB builds directly on SysBinder's block-slot attention mechanism (Singh et al., ICLR 2023) for continuous object-factor disentanglement and on HDBSCAN (Campello et al., 2015) for unsupervised clustering, but the key conceptual departure is the retrieval-based discretization step that bridges these two into a structured, symbolic concept corpus. Unlike prior unsupervised approaches (VAE, VQ-VAE, Slot Attention, SysBinder) that produce only continuous encodings, or supervised concept-bottleneck methods (CBM, NeSyCL) that require labels, NCB positions itself as the first framework to yield inherently inspectable and revisable discrete concepts from purely unlabeled images. The paper also contrasts with post-hoc interpretability methods by making inspectability a built-in architectural property rather than an after-the-fact analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in trustworthy AI: unsupervised models that learn concepts without labels need a mechanism for humans to verify and correct those concepts before deployment in high-stakes settings. The retrieval corpus provides a natural, low-friction interface for this human-in-the-loop interaction, and the CLEVR-Sudoku benchmark offers a concrete testbed for combining perception with symbolic reasoning. However, all evaluations are on synthetic CLEVR-style scenes, leaving open whether the approach scales to real-world images, video, or scenes with many overlapping objects. The paper also acknowledges that GPT-4-based revision can introduce harmful 'ill-informed' feedback, highlighting that the quality of the revisory agent remains an open problem.
