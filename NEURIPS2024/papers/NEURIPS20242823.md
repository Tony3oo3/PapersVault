## Problem

Computing the probability of formulas involving interventions in Causal Bayesian Networks (CBNs) is fundamentally ill-defined: Pearl's standard approach was shown to be incorrect (Beckers 2023), and without additional assumptions, interventional formulas yield a range of probabilities rather than a unique value. The paper asks whether simple, well-motivated independence assumptions on the CPTs can restore unique identifiability and, crucially, whether the resulting probabilities can be estimated from observational data alone.

## Input / Output

The method takes a CBN (a DAG with conditional probability tables for each node) and a formula in the causal language L or L+ (Boolean combinations of primitive events like X=x and intervention expressions like [X←x]φ). It produces a unique probability for the formula; for the key counterfactual quantities (probability of necessity, sufficiency, and necessity-and-sufficiency), the computation reduces to sums and products of observational conditional probabilities of simple (non-interventional) formulas, meaning no experimental data is required. In the worst case the number of complete combinations of conditional events (cCCCEs) is doubly exponential in the number of variables, though fixed-context cCCCEs and structural simplifications keep practical computation tractable.

## Method

The core contribution is formalizing Pearl's informal 'autonomy of mechanisms' assumption as probabilistic independence of CPTs, at two levels: (i) CPTs for different variables are independent, and (ii) entries within a single CPT for different parent settings are independent. The paper introduces complete combinations of conditional events (cCCCEs) and fixed-context cCCCEs (fccCEs) as the atomic units of probability, proves that a cCCCE determines the truth of any formula in L+ (Theorem 3.6), and defines i-compatible causal models in which the probability of any formula is invariant (Theorem 3.7). An explicit construction converts a CBN into an i-compatible probabilistic causal model by replacing each CPT with a response-function exogenous variable (following Balke and Pearl), making the independence assumptions concrete. Finally, Theorem 5.2 shows that PN, PS, and PNS for a child-parent pair reduce to sums over conditional probabilities of the form Pr(Y=y | X=x, Pa^X(Y)=c), all estimable from observational data, thereby eliminating the need for controlled experiments in many realistic settings.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical contribution is Theorem 5.2, which provides closed-form expressions for the probability of necessity (PN), probability of sufficiency (PS), and probability of necessity and sufficiency (PNS) in a CBN under CPT independence assumptions, showing these are uniquely identifiable from observational conditional probabilities alone. Theorem B.1 extends this to arbitrary interventional formulas, showing Pr(ψ) can be computed using O(m·(nr*+1)·2^(n(r*+1))) conditional probability calculations, where m is the number of DNF disjuncts, n is the number of variables, and r* is the maximum number of intervention conjuncts per disjunct. Example 4.1 concretely illustrates the impact: for a simple 3-variable CBN, dropping the independence assumption changes the probability of necessity from the unique value 1/4 to the range [0, 1/2], matching the Balke-Pearl/Tian-Pearl bounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The relevant contrast is with prior identifiability results: Pearl (2000) and Tian & Pearl (2000) show that PN and PS in a CBN are only partially identifiable, yielding ranges (bounds) rather than unique values; Balke & Pearl (1994) provide the foundational bounds. The paper demonstrates that adding the CPT independence assumption (formalized as i-compatibility) collapses these ranges to unique values, making PN, PS, and PNS identifiable from observational data alone. Beckers (2023) is cited as showing that Pearl's original implicit reduction from CBNs to functional causal models is incorrect, which motivates the paper's explicit formalization. The Richardson, Peters, and Halpern (2024) result is leveraged to justify the inter-variable independence component as equivalent to standard d-separation independencies, while the within-CPT independence (across parent settings) is an additional assumption whose appropriateness depends on whether all parents are observable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes and extends Pearl's (2000) informal 'autonomy' assumption for causal mechanisms into a precise probabilistic independence assumption on CPTs, both across different variables and across different parent settings for the same variable. It builds directly on Balke and Pearl's (1994) construction of response functions to convert CBNs to functional causal models, and on Richardson, Peters, and Halpern's (2024) result that inter-variable CPT independence is necessary and sufficient for d-separation independencies. The paper departs from the Balke-Pearl and Tian-Pearl tradition of computing only bounds on counterfactual probabilities by showing that the independence assumptions yield unique identification, and it responds to Beckers' (2023) demonstration that Pearl's earlier CBN probability calculation was incorrect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that probabilities of necessity and sufficiency—long treated as only boundable quantities—become uniquely computable from purely observational data under the independence assumptions, which is significant in settings where controlled experiments are infeasible (e.g., healthcare, manufacturing). The main limitation is that the within-CPT independence assumption (across different parent settings) is not always justified when latent exogenous variables create correlations between potential outcomes; the paper identifies when it is appropriate (all parents observable and screening off the variable) but acknowledges this is not universally satisfiable. Open questions include whether the doubly-exponential number of ccces can be handled more efficiently in practice, and whether weaker independence assumptions than full mutual independence suffice for unique identification.
