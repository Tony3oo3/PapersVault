## Problem

Natural behaviors exhibit substantial variability even when performance is high, yet standard neural network and reinforcement learning approaches suppress variability after learning to maximize task performance. This paper asks whether it is possible to generate maximal neural variability while simultaneously maintaining reliable performance, and what type of variability is compatible with avoiding detrimental (terminal) states.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240775/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The controller reads the N=100-dimensional RNN activity state x (optionally augmented with a C-dimensional one-hot context vector) and produces an M=8-dimensional binary action vector a_k ∈ {-1,1}, which is expanded via a fixed random matrix K ∈ U(0,1) into N-dimensional currents injected into the RNN. The value function is approximated by a single-hidden-layer FFN (256 ReLU units) whose input can be reduced to N_inp=20 randomly selected activities. The low action dimensionality M≪N makes this a genuinely underactuated control problem, and the policy is computed exactly (not approximated) via a Boltzmann distribution over the 2^M discrete actions.

## Method

NeuroMOP extends the Maximum Occupancy Principle to neural activity by defining an intrinsic reward of -ln π(a|x) for each action, so the agent maximizes the discounted cumulative future action entropy rather than an extrinsic reward. The optimal policy takes the closed-form Boltzmann expression π*(a|x) = Z(x)^{-1} exp(γ Σ_{x'} p(x'|x,a) V*(x')), with the value function V*(x) = ln Z(x) satisfying a log-sum-exp Bellman consistency equation. Training minimizes the squared Bellman error over batches of trajectories using a 1-hidden-layer FFN, with terminal states (absorbing 'dead' states where only 'do nothing' is available) enforcing V=0. The key mechanism is that the value function naturally encodes proximity to terminal states, causing the policy to reduce its effective action dimensionality (projecting noise onto a null space) near boundaries while maintaining full stochasticity far from them, thereby flexibly switching between stochastic and deterministic regimes without any explicit reward specification.

## Results

In the energy constraint task, the NeuroMOP network avoids terminal states for the full episode duration (T=1000) while maintaining maximum effective action dimensionality (ED_a ≈ M=8) far from the energy threshold, reducing it to ED_a ≈ 7 only in proximity to the boundary. In the constrained neural space task, NeuroMOP fills the entire available square region in the (x₁, x₂) readout space, whereas the R network only traces one side of the square. In the context-dependent task, a single network successfully draws 6 different shapes (square, plus, circle, diamond, oval, heart) with context-appropriate action entropy modulation. Across all tasks, NeuroMOP produces larger average standard deviation of neural trajectories (⟨σ⟩) than the R network while achieving comparable or superior lifetimes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between NeuroMOP and an R network using an ε-greedy reward-maximizing policy (reward = 1 for surviving, 0 for entering terminal states). NeuroMOP consistently outperforms the R network on behavioral richness: it occupies the full available activity space (e.g., filling a square) while the R network collapses to a single trajectory (one side of the square) due to its inherent greediness. The R network achieves comparable lifetimes only by quenching its stochasticity (ε → 0) or using an exponentially decaying ε schedule, effectively eliminating the variability it was designed to produce. NeuroMOP maintains high effective action dimensionality (ED_a ≈ M) in unconstrained regions and flexibly reduces it near terminal states, whereas the R network exhibits uniformly low ED_a < M by preferring inhibitory currents and driving the RNN toward minimum energy. In the context-dependent task, the R network's fixed ε cannot adapt to varying shape difficulties, while NeuroMOP modulates action entropy both state-dependently and context-dependently. The paper also shows that adding an extrinsic reward to the MOP framework (Appendix I) causes behavioral collapse into the rewarded region, confirming that pure entropy maximization preserves the full behavioral repertoire.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Maximum Occupancy Principle (MOP) [44, 45], originally formulated for behavioral action-state space, to neural activity by introducing NeuroMOP, which posits that the nervous system maximizes future cumulative action-state entropy in neural dynamics. It departs from the standard RL paradigm where stochasticity is used only during learning and suppressed at execution (e.g., SAC [36], deterministic policies in state-of-the-art RL [37]), instead treating maximal variability as the primary objective with performance maintained through terminal-state avoidance. The architecture also diverges from typical RNN control by keeping recurrent weights fixed and random, using an external stochastic current generator as the agent, rather than training the network itself. The Bellman-based value function approximation and Boltzmann optimal policy connect to the broader maximum-entropy RL and reward-free exploration literature [37, 46, 47], but the specific formulation of action-path entropy as the sole intrinsic reward is novel in the neural control setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NeuroMOP offers a unified theoretical framework reconciling stochastic and deterministic neural behaviors, predicting that variability persists after training but decreases near 'dangerous' states — a testable prediction for neuroscience. The finding that reward-maximizing agents collapse to stereotyped, low-dimensional behavior while entropy-maximizing agents maintain rich, high-dimensional activity challenges the assumption that performance requires suppressing variability. Key limitations include the reliance on an oracle policy (no policy learning), the fixed-weight RNN assumption, and the computational cost of evaluating all 2^M actions per step; extending to learned policies, spiking dynamics, and learning the structure of terminal states are important open directions. The framework's practical impact on DL/CV is currently limited, but the conceptual insights about variability-performance tradeoffs and state-dependent stochasticity could inform more biologically plausible network design.
