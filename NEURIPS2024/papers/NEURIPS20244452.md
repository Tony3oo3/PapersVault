## Problem

Existing multi-objective RL and safe RL methods require human experts to handcraft target preferences or safety thresholds before deployment, a process that scales poorly with the number of objectives and is difficult to validate. This paper asks whether a small set of behavioral demonstrations can implicitly encode the desired preference and safety constraints, enabling offline adaptation without any explicit preference or threshold specification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244452/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes a large offline dataset of (state, action, next-state, reward, cost, preference) tuples generated by diverse behavior policies. At deployment, it takes only a small demonstration set of M state-action-reward-cost transitions (default M=128 from K=2 trajectories) that implicitly indicate the target preference and/or safety threshold, with no access to the true values. The output is a normalized adapted preference vector (L1-norm = 1) over N+K objectives and the corresponding preference-conditioned policy; for constrained objectives, the preference weights are replaced by CVaR-based conservative estimates before normalization.

## Method

PDOA operates in two phases: first, it trains a set of preference-conditioned policies and value functions using existing offline MORL algorithms (MODF or PEDA/MORvS) on the offline dataset; second, during deployment, it infers the target preference by fitting a Gaussian posterior over preferences via Bayesian inference, where the likelihood is approximated by a TD-error surrogate (measuring how in-distribution the demonstrations are under each preference's value model) plus the action likelihood from the learned policy, regularized by a Gaussian prior from the training data. For constrained settings, the method converts the constrained problem to an unconstrained one via Lagrangian duality by extending the reward vector to include negative costs, then applies a CVaR-based conservative estimate on the preference weights for constrained objectives (parameterized by α) to mitigate constraint violations when the adapted preference underestimates the true safety weight. The entire adaptation is performed by gradient descent on the Gaussian parameters (μ, σ) over 1000 steps, requiring no policy retraining.

## Results

On D4MORL MORL tasks, PDOA [MODF] matches the oracle performance of preference-informed MODF and MORvS in both average utility and Hypervolume, while producing a broader Pareto front than preference-agnostic baselines. On DSRL safe RL tasks, PDOA [MODF] achieves constraint satisfaction closely matching the threshold-informed oracle CDT, even under very tight safety thresholds, with reward performance comparable to or exceeding CDT. On the novel CMO constrained MORL tasks (2 unconstrained + 1 constrained objective), PDOA [MODF] approaches oracle performance with high average utility, Hypervolume, and few constraint violations. Ablation studies confirm that even with as few as 16 demonstration transitions, adapted policies retain sufficient diversity and safety, and increasing the CVaR conservatism parameter α further reduces constraint violations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BC-Finetune, PDOA [MODF] substantially outperforms on all three task families: BC-Finetune's adapted policies cluster tightly around the behavior-cloned policy, yielding narrow Pareto fronts, low Hypervolume, and frequent constraint violations on safe/CMO tasks. Against Prompt-MODT, PDOA again wins decisively; Prompt-MODT's preference-based task partitioning leads to either data insufficiency (fine-grained) or mixed behaviors (coarse-grained), resulting in restricted Pareto fronts and weak correlation with safety thresholds. Against the oracle baselines (MODF, MORvS for MORL; CDT for safe RL), PDOA [MODF] matches or closely approaches their performance without requiring access to the true target preference or safety threshold, which is the core contribution. PDOA [MORvS] underperforms on DSRL datasets specifically because MORvS requires accurate target-return predictions that are unreliable with the abundant suboptimal trajectories in DSRL. In the appendix comparison with the offline IL baseline DWBC, PDOA [MODF] shows more stable and consistently superior performance, while DWBC struggles with demo sufficiency (128 transitions vs. millions in the dataset) and exhibits high constraint violations on safe tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on offline MORL algorithms (MODF, PEDA/MORvS) that learn preference-conditioned policies, and on Bayesian offline adaptation methods (Ghosh et al. 2022, Hong et al. 2022) that use TD-error surrogates to approximate likelihoods without explicit dynamics models. The key departure is formulating a new offline adaptation problem for constrained MORL where the target preference and safety threshold are unknown and must be inferred from a few demonstrations via a Gaussian posterior over preferences, rather than being handcrafted by human experts. The constrained-to-unconstrained conversion leverages the zero-duality-gap result of Paternain et al. (2019), and the CVaR-based conservative preference estimation for constrained objectives is a novel safety mechanism not present in prior offline MORL or safe RL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework removes a significant practical bottleneck in MORL and safe RL deployment: the need for domain experts to manually design preference weights and safety thresholds from extensive observation of demonstrations. By showing that a simple Bayesian posterior over preferences (fitted with only 128 transitions) can match oracle performance, the paper demonstrates that preference identification is tractable in offline settings. However, the adaptation phase incurs computational latency from gradient updates, and the method still requires collecting demonstrations (which may involve human expertise), limiting its applicability to rapidly changing or data-scarce environments. Open questions include scalability to many more objectives, handling non-linear preference functions, and whether the Gaussian posterior assumption holds in highly multimodal preference landscapes.
