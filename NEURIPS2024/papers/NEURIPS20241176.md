## Problem

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is the standard training algorithm for neural networks, but when applied to analog in-memory computing (AIMC) accelerators with resistive crossbar arrays, it converges inexactly due to the asymmetric nature of physical weight updates. Prior work lacked a theoretical foundation explaining why Analog SGD fails to match digital SGD's convergence behavior, and no algorithm was known to eliminate the resulting asymptotic error. This paper asks: what is the correct dynamic for gradient-based training on analog devices, and can the fundamental performance limit of Analog SGD be overcome?

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard supervised training data (MNIST grayscale 28×28, CIFAR-10 RGB 32×32) in mini-batches, along with device-specific parameters (the asymmetry parameter τ of the asymmetric linear device) and initial model weights. The output is trained model weights stored as conductance values in analog crossbar arrays, accompanied by rigorous convergence guarantees (average squared gradient norm bounds) and test accuracy. A key unusual aspect is that weight updates are physically implemented via sequences of electrical pulses whose count is proportional to the update magnitude, and the update response depends on the current weight value, making the dynamics state-dependent in a way absent from digital training.

## Method

The paper first derives a discrete-time dynamic for Analog SGD that includes an extra asymmetric bias term (−α/τ |∇f+ε| ⊙ W) arising from the pulse-update mechanism on resistive crossbars, where increasing and decreasing a weight produce different magnitudes of change. It then proves that this bias, interacting with stochastic gradient noise, creates a fundamental asymptotic error of order σ²S_K that does not vanish with iterations, and establishes a matching lower bound showing this limit is intrinsic. To eliminate the error, the paper provides the first rigorous convergence proof for the Tiki-Taka algorithm, which maintains an auxiliary analog array P_k to estimate the gradient before transferring it to the main weight array W_k. The key mechanism is that Tiki-Taka reduces the expected weight drift from O(α) to O(αβ) by routing noise through the auxiliary array first, where the asymmetric update causes P_k to decay back toward zero, thereby decoupling the noise from the main weight and achieving exact convergence to a critical point at rate O(√(σ²L/K)).

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that Analog SGD suffers from a non-vanishing asymptotic error of O(σ²S_K) due to asymmetric updates, while Tiki-Taka achieves exact convergence to a critical point with zero asymptotic error. On CIFAR-10 ResNet (100 epochs), Tiki-Taka reaches 93.74%, 95.15%, and 95.54% test accuracy for ResNet-18/34/50, matching or exceeding Digital SGD (93.03%, 93.44%, 95.92%). On MNIST CNN, Analog SGD drops by >6% accuracy relative to Digital SGD, while Tiki-Taka stays within <1% of the digital baseline across all τ values tested.

|---|---|---|---|
| Digital SGD | 93.03 | 93.44 | 95.92 |
| Analog SGD | 93.58 | 93.58 | 95.51 |
| Tiki-Taka | **93.74** | **95.15** | 95.54 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Digital SGD, Tiki-Taka matches or slightly exceeds accuracy on CIFAR-10 ResNet (e.g., 95.15% vs 93.44% for ResNet-34), with the authors conjecturing that analog device noise acts as implicit regularization. Against Analog SGD, Tiki-Taka shows a decisive advantage on MNIST: for the CNN architecture, Analog SGD drops from 99.16% (Digital SGD) to 88.99–93.45% depending on τ, while Tiki-Taka maintains 98.84–98.87%, a gap of over 6 percentage points. On MNIST FCN, the gap is smaller (Analog SGD reaches 97.71–97.78% vs Tiki-Taka's 97.50–97.64%), but Analog SGD converges much more slowly. The theoretical comparison (Table 1) further shows that Tiki-Taka eliminates the asymptotic error term σ²S_K that plagues Analog SGD, while both methods share the same O(√(σ²/K)) convergence rate up to a saturation-dependent constant factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the pulse-update framework for analog SGD (Gokmen & Vlasov, 2016) and the Tiki-Taka heuristic algorithm (Gokmen & Haensch, 2020), both of which were developed empirically without convergence guarantees. The key departure is providing the first rigorous theoretical foundation: a discrete-time dynamic that captures the asymmetric update physics of resistive crossbar devices, a convergence proof showing Analog SGD has an irreducible asymptotic error with a matching lower bound, and an exact convergence guarantee for Tiki-Taka. The analysis connects to standard SGD convergence theory (Bottou et al., 2018) and stochastic optimization lower bounds (Arjevani et al., 2023), but demonstrates that the standard digital SGD framework fundamentally fails to model analog training dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes that the accuracy gap between analog and digital training is not merely a practical nuisance but a fundamental performance limit of SGD on asymmetric hardware, which redirects algorithmic research toward noise-aware methods like Tiki-Taka. The practical impact is significant for the growing analog computing ecosystem (IBM's Albany chip, ReRAM/PCM arrays) by providing design principles: the saturation degree W_max/τ governs both convergence speed and error floor. A key limitation is that the analysis is restricted to the asymmetric linear device model; extending to power-step or exponential-step devices (which better match real ReRAM/ECRAM materials) remains open. The surprising CIFAR10 result where analog methods outperform digital SGD (attributed to noise-induced regularization) is intriguing but underexplored.
