## Problem

Existing multimodal mathematical reasoning benchmarks like MathVista suffer from two critical limitations: (1) narrow subject coverage, predominantly focusing on plane geometry while neglecting solid geometry, graph theory, topology, and transformation geometry, and (2) highly repetitive, template-driven questions (e.g., 'Subtract all X objects. How many are left?' appears 400 times) that test visual recognition rather than mathematical reasoning. This creates a misleading picture of LMM capabilities, as models surpass human scores on MathVista yet still fail on straightforward elementary-school problems.

## Input / Output

Each problem pairs an abstract math diagram (no real-life photographs) with a text question averaging 42.3 words—nearly 3× longer than MathVista's 15.6—reflecting the richer context from real competition problems. The 3,040 problems split evenly between 1,532 five-option multiple-choice and 1,508 open-ended questions, all in English. A 304-question testmini subset is provided for rapid evaluation, and all problems are tagged with one of 16 subject labels and one of 5 difficulty levels (1–5, mapped to competition grade requirements).

## Method

MATH-V is built through a holistic collection pipeline: questions and their accompanying images are jointly sourced from 19 real math competitions (Math Kangaroo, UKMT, AMC/AIME), eliminating the post-hoc annotation that produces template questions in prior benchmarks. A four-stage curation process with 10 science-major annotators verifies text-image alignment, removes duplicates via lexical overlap and Levenshtein distance, assigns subject labels through three independent annotator groups plus GPT-4V/Gemini Pro advice consolidated by majority voting, and grades difficulty from the original competition's recommended grade. The dataset introduces 8 previously uncovered subjects (Analytic Geometry, Combinatorial Geometry, Combinatorics, Descriptive Geometry, Graph Theory, Solid Geometry, Topology, Transformation Geometry) and subdivides plane geometry into angle, area, and length. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot across 14 LLMs and LMMs with and without Chain-of-Thought prompting, supplemented by a 100-annotator human baseline and a fine-grained error taxonomy (reasoning, vision recognition, knowledge, calculation) applied to GPT-4V's 232 wrong answers on testmini.

## Results

On MATH-Vision (3,040 problems, 16 subjects, 5 difficulty levels), the best-performing model GPT-4o achieves only 30.39% zero-shot accuracy, far below the human baseline of 68.82%. The best open-source model, InternVL-Chat-V1-2-Plus, reaches 16.97%, while random chance is 5.86%. GPT-4o shows the strongest performance in arithmetic (49.3%) and statistics (44.8%), but struggles most in transformation geometry (17.9%) and topology (34.8%), where humans score 69.0% and 99.8% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o (30.39%) narrowly edges out GPT-4-turbo (30.26%) overall, with the largest gap in algebra (+4.3 pts) and topology (+17.4 pts), while GPT-4-turbo leads in transformation geometry (23.2% vs. 17.9%). Closed-source models dominate open-source ones by a wide margin: the best open-source model InternVL-Chat-V1-2-Plus (16.97%) trails GPT-4o by 13.4 points, a reversal of the MathVista ranking where InternVL-Chat-V1-2-Plus (59.9%) outperformed GPT-4V (49.9%), suggesting the open-source model benefited from MathVista training data. All models fall dramatically short of the human baseline (68.82%), with even GPT-4o scoring less than half the human accuracy. Chain-of-Thought prompting yields inconsistent gains: GPT-4V improves by +1.22 pts, but Gemini Pro (−3.32) and Qwen-VL-Max (−2.96) actually degrade, indicating immature step-by-step reasoning in LMMs. Text-only GPT-4 with image captions (13.10%) performs comparably to mid-tier LMMs, confirming that MATH-V questions genuinely require visual input rather than being solvable from text alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in multimodal mathematical reasoning benchmarks by departing from the dominant data collection paradigm. Prior benchmarks like MathVista and MMMU rely on post-hoc question annotation over existing images, which the authors show produces repetitive, recognition-driven questions concentrated in a narrow set of subjects (mainly plane geometry, counting, and measurement). MATH-V instead uses a holistic approach, jointly collecting questions and images from 19 real math competitions, which naturally yields longer, more diverse questions (avg. 42.3 words vs. 15.6) and covers 8 previously absent subjects including topology, graph theory, and transformation geometry. The paper also positions against earlier geometry-specific benchmarks (Geometry3K, GeoQA+, GEOS, UniGeo) that are limited to plane geometry, and against MMMU which targets college-level problems with sophisticated domain knowledge rather than general visual-mathematical reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark ~38-point gap between the best LMM (GPT-4o at 30.39%) and human performance (68.82%), directly challenging the narrative that LMMs have surpassed human capabilities in multimodal math reasoning as suggested by MathVista scores. The error analysis (42.2% reasoning errors, 31.9% vision recognition errors, only 1.3% calculation errors) provides actionable diagnostic information for model developers, and the finding that CoT prompting degrades performance for several models (e.g., Gemini Pro drops 3.3 points) highlights immaturity in step-by-step reasoning. Key limitations include English-only questions and exclusively abstract/diagrammatic images (no real-life photos), leaving open whether the gap persists in more natural visual settings. The benchmark is likely to become a standard evaluation tool given its scale, subject diversity, and difficulty stratification.
