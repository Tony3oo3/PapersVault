## Problem

Airborne Laser Scanning has revealed vast hidden archaeological landscapes, but the absence of large-scale, expert-annotated, open-access datasets has prevented the field from leveraging modern deep learning. Existing ALS archaeology datasets are either closed, small in extent, or lack high-resolution RGB imagery, leaving a gap between the geospatial revolution in archaeology and the capabilities of contemporary computer vision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240813/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each input is a 4-channel GeoTIFF at 0.5 m ground sampling distance combining RGB orthophotography with a normalized digital terrain model (nDTM) derived from 10 billion LiDAR points; georeferencing and absolute elevation are stripped to prevent site identification. The model produces a pixel-level semantic segmentation mask over five classes (temple, mound, hydrology, void, background), with void pixels excluded from supervision and evaluation. Inference is performed on a sliding grid at 224×224 or 512×512 pixels (112 m or 256 m ground extent) with 25% overlap, keeping only the central portion of each prediction.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the Archaeoscape dataset (888 km², 31,411 annotated features in Cambodia) and a systematic benchmark of 12+ segmentation architectures spanning CNNs (U-Net, DeepLabv3), standard ViTs (ViT-S/B, HybViT, DOFA), and hierarchical ViTs (SWIN, PCPVT, PVTv2), all fine-tuned from diverse pre-training (ImageNet-1K/21K, DINOv2, CLIP, ScaleMAE, SatLas). A key adaptation is adding the elevation channel to the first convolutional layer with small random initialization (N(0, 0.01)) while retaining pre-trained RGB weights. The central empirical finding is that standard ViTs and foundation models pre-trained on natural RGB images underperform CNNs and hierarchical ViTs, because the most informative signal is the subtle elevation pattern rather than radiometry—RGB-only models drop to ~30% mIoU versus ~52% with elevation. The best result (PVTv2-b1 at 512×512, 52.2 mIoU) still reveals that detecting faint, kilometer-scale features under dense canopy remains an open challenge for current architectures.

## Results

On the Archaeoscape benchmark (888 km², 31,411 annotated features), the best model at 224×224 input is PVTv2-b1 (ImageNet1K pre-trained) with 52.1% mIoU and 88.7% overall accuracy. Scaling input to 512×512 improves U-Net to 52.8% mIoU / 89.6% OA and PVTv2 to 52.2% mIoU / 89.9% OA. Notably, CNN-based U-Net (50.5% mIoU at 224) outperforms most Vision Transformer variants, while foundation models (DINOv2, CLIP, ScaleMAE) all fall below 42% mIoU, indicating that pre-training on natural or satellite RGB images does not transfer well to elevation-based archaeological segmentation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PVTv2-b1 (ImageNet1K) achieves the best 224-resolution mIoU at 52.1%, narrowly edging out SWIN-S (51.9%) and PCPVT-S (51.7%), while U-Net (50.5%) and HybViT-S (50.4%) trail by roughly 1.5–2 points. The key finding is that standard CNNs (U-Net) outperform most pure Vision Transformers, with the exception of hierarchical ViTs (SWIN, PCPVT, PVTv2) that incorporate local convolutional processing. Foundation models pre-trained on large-scale natural or satellite imagery—DINOv2 (41.9%), CLIP (30.3%), LAION-2B (32.4%), and ScaleMAE (30.4%)—all underperform significantly, suggesting their RGB-centric pre-training does not transfer to the elevation-dominant signal in ALS archaeology. DOFA, designed for multi-sensor generalization, also lags at 39.6% mIoU. At 512×512 input, U-Net reaches 52.8% mIoU and PVTv2 reaches 52.2% with 89.9% OA, confirming that larger context helps but does not close the gap to human-level detection of subtle archaeological features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first open-access, large-scale ALS archaeology dataset for Southeast Asia, departing from the closed-access norm that has characterized prior LiDAR archaeology benchmarks (Arran, AHN, Chactún, etc.). Methodologically, it repurposes the full spectrum of modern semantic segmentation architectures—CNNs (U-Net, DeepLabv3), ViTs, and hierarchical ViTs (PVTv2, SWIN, PCPVT)—that were developed for natural image understanding, adapting them to the dual-channel input of RGB orthophotography and LiDAR-derived elevation. The paper positions itself as a bridge between the U-Net-dominated archaeological literature and the broader computer vision community, explicitly benchmarking foundation models (DINOv2, CLIP, ScaleMAE) that have not previously been evaluated on this task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset's scale (888 km², 31,411 instances) and open-access policy make it a critical resource for the archaeological CV community, enabling reproducible research that was previously impossible. The benchmark reveals that even state-of-the-art segmentation models achieve only ~52% mIoU, and that foundation models pre-trained on natural or satellite imagery fail to transfer effectively—highlighting a genuine gap between current deep learning capabilities and the demands of subtle, large-scale elevation pattern recognition. Open questions include how to incorporate multi-kilometer spatial context, whether domain-specific pre-training on elevation data could close the performance gap, and whether findings generalize beyond the Khmer cultural context. The paper also raises important ethical questions about open-access archaeological data that the community must continue to address.
