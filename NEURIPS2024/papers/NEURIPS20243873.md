## Problem

In multi-fidelity best-arm identification, prior lower bounds on the cost complexity were loose and existing algorithms either required additional structural assumptions on the relationship between costs and precision bounds, or needed prior knowledge of the optimal arm's mean. This paper closes the gap by proving a tight instance-dependent lower bound and designing an algorithm that matches it asymptotically without any extra assumptions on the bandit instance.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a risk parameter δ ∈ (0,1) and interacts sequentially with a K×M multi-fidelity bandit, choosing at each round an arm and a fidelity level. It knows the per-fidelity costs λ_m (strictly increasing) and precision bounds ξ_m (strictly decreasing to 0), which define the maximum bias of lower-fidelity observations relative to the highest fidelity. The output is the identified best arm (argmax of μ_{a,M}), a stopping time τ_δ, and the total cost incurred, where cost is the sum of λ_m per pull at fidelity m rather than a uniform per-sample count.

## Method

MF-GRAD operates in the space of cost proportions ω ∈ Δ_{K×M} rather than pull proportions, exploiting the fact that the lower-bound objective F(ω,μ) is concave in ω but not in the corresponding pull proportions. At each iteration, it updates ω via an exponential-weights (mirror-descent) step using a sub-gradient of F, which is computed efficiently by solving a one-dimensional fixed-point equation per arm pair (exploiting the piecewise structure of KL divergences in exponential families), yielding O(K²M²) per-iteration complexity. Cost proportions are converted to pull proportions, a small forced-exploration component is added, and a cumulative-tracking rule selects the next arm-fidelity pair. Stopping is governed by a generalized likelihood ratio test on the pairwise dissimilarity functions f_{i,j}, with a threshold calibrated to guarantee δ-correctness. The resulting cost complexity matches the lower bound C*(μ)·log(1/δ) asymptotically as δ→0.

## Results

On a 4×5 multi-fidelity Gaussian bandit (δ=0.01), MF-GRAD achieves the lowest empirical cost complexity among all three methods, with a tighter and lower distribution than both IISE and GRAD (Figure 1). On a structured 5×2 instance where the optimal cost proportions are ω_i*=[0.09621, 0] for arms 1–4 and ω_5*=[0, 0.61516], MF-GRAD again obtains the best cost complexity, while its empirical cost proportions converge to the theoretical optimum ω* over 10⁵ iterations (Figures 2–3). The paper also proves that the new lower bound improves over the prior bound of [25] by a factor of λ_M/λ_m (arbitrarily large) on certain 2-arm, 2-fidelity instances, and by a factor of 8 in the single-fidelity Gaussian case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MF-GRAD outperforms both baselines on all tested instances. Against IISE [25], MF-GRAD wins decisively: IISE's notion of optimal fidelity (which selects fidelity m=2 for all arms in the 5×2 example) is provably sub-optimal relative to the tight lower bound, causing IISE to waste budget on expensive samples and discard sub-optimal arms only after an initial unproductive phase at fidelity m=1. Against GRAD [22] (the gradient-ascent method applied exclusively at the highest fidelity), MF-GRAD also wins because GRAD never samples the cheaper fidelity m=1 for arms 1–4, even though the optimal allocation assigns all their cost to that fidelity (ω_i*=[0.09621, 0]). The 5×2 example makes this gap particularly stark: the optimal strategy spends most budget on fidelity 2 for arm 5 (ω_5,2*=0.61516) but all budget on fidelity 1 for arms 1–4, a pattern neither baseline captures. The authors also note that the multi-fidelity algorithm of [31] was excluded from empirical comparison due to implementation issues and a proof error identified in Appendix D.6.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-ascent approach of Ménard (2019) to the multi-fidelity, cost-aware setting, adapting it to handle the non-concave structure that arises when optimizing over cost proportions rather than pull proportions. It builds on the Track-and-Stop framework of Garivier & Kaufmann (2016) for the cumulative tracking and GLR-based stopping rule, but departs from prior multi-fidelity BAI work (Poiani et al. 2022, Wang et al. 2024) by eliminating their additional structural assumptions and prior-knowledge requirements. The key conceptual departure is showing that the intuitive 'optimal fidelity' notions from those prior works are provably suboptimal, and that a tight lower bound can be expressed as a max-min game over cost proportions with a sparsity structure that was previously unrecognized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first result to close the gap between lower and upper bounds on cost complexity for multi-fidelity BAI without additional assumptions, providing a complete characterization of the statistical difficulty of the problem. The sparsity result (one optimal fidelity per arm in almost all instances) and the finding that the algorithm achieves optimality without explicitly identifying those fidelities offer practical guidance for expensive-evaluation scenarios like physics simulations and hyperparameter tuning. Open questions include whether the moderate-confidence regime admits finite-time guarantees, whether the sparsity pattern can be exploited to reduce exploration overhead, and whether a worst-case O(KM) scaling emerges when all fidelities must be explored.
