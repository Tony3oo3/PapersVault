## Problem

Gradient-based adversarial prompt generation (e.g., GCG) suffers from limited white-box attack success rates against safety-aligned LLMs because the discrete nature of text means gradients w.r.t. one-hot token vectors poorly approximate the actual loss change from a token replacement. This paper identifies that this gradient-to-effect gap is structurally analogous to the substitute-to-victim model gradient gap in transfer-based image attacks, and shows that two established transfer-attack techniques—Skip Gradient Method and Intermediate Level Attack—can be adapted to close the gap without additional computational cost.

## Input / Output

The method takes a harmful user query (a natural-language instruction from AdvBench or HarmBench) and produces a 20-token adversarial suffix appended to the query, optimized to make the target LLM emit a specific affirmative phrase (e.g., 'Sure, here's...'). For query-specific attacks, one suffix is generated per query; for universal attacks, a single suffix is optimized on 10 queries and evaluated on the remaining 510. The output is a discrete token sequence, and evaluation uses both exact-match rate against the target string and a model-based HarmBench evaluator for attack success rate.

## Method

The paper introduces two drop-in modifications to the GCG optimization loop. First, Language SGM (LSGM) scales down backpropagated gradients from residual modules (MLP and attention sub-layers) by a decay factor γ=0.5, effectively letting skip-connection gradients dominate; ablation shows skip-connection gradients better predict the true loss effect of token swaps because residual-module gradients point in conflicting directions. Second, Language ILA (LILA) replaces the intermediate-layer gradient at a single token position (the first target-phrase token at the mid-layer) with a re-normalized directional guide computed as the difference between the initial and current intermediate representations at that position, rather than replacing the gradient across the entire intermediate representation as in the original ILA. The combined GCG-LSGM-LILA pipeline achieves 87% exact-match rate and 68% ASR on Llama-2-7B-Chat (vs. 54% and 38% for GCG) while using only 4% of GCG's default computational budget.

## Results

On AdvBench, GCG-LSGM-LILA achieves a match rate of 87% and an attack success rate of 68% for query-specific adversarial suffix generation against Llama-2-7B-Chat, compared to 54% MR and 38% ASR for the GCG baseline. For universal adversarial suffix generation on the same model, the combination achieves an average ASR of 60.32% (vs. 26.68% for GCG), with worst-case ASR improving from 0.40% to 35.22%. The gains are consistent across Llama-2-13B-Chat (+19% ASR), Mistral-7B-Instruct (+19% ASR), and Phi3-Mini-4K-Instruct (+21% ASR), while maintaining the same ~3-minute time cost per suffix as GCG (vs. 85 minutes for GCG with its default 512-candidate setting).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is GCG (Zou et al., 2023), the state-of-the-art gradient-based adversarial suffix generator. GCG-LSGM-LILA outperforms GCG on every model and every metric: on Llama-2-7B-Chat it improves match rate from 54% to 87% (+33%) and ASR from 38% to 68% (+30%); on Llama-2-13B-Chat from 37% to 62% MR and 33% to 52% ASR; on Mistral-7B-Instruct from 73% to 94% MR and 74% to 93% ASR; and on Phi3-Mini-4K-Instruct from 60% to 81% MR and 59% to 68% ASR. Even against GCG with its full 512-candidate default setting (GCG*), the combination still achieves higher MR and ASR on Llama-2-7B-Chat (87% vs. 60% MR) while using only 4% of the time. The secondary baseline, AutoPrompt (Shin et al., 2020), is used in Figures 2 and 7 to demonstrate that both LSGM and LILA individually improve over AutoPrompt as well, confirming the gains are not specific to GCG's optimization loop. For universal suffix generation, GCG-LSGM-LILA also dominates GCG across all four models on both AdvBench and HarmBench, with the largest absolute gain being +33.64% AASR on Llama-2-7B-Chat (26.68% → 60.32%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a conceptual bridge between two previously separate research threads: gradient-based adversarial prompt generation for LLMs (exemplified by GCG, AutoPrompt, PEZ) and transfer-based adversarial attacks on image classification models (SGM, ILA). The key insight is that the gap between one-hot input gradients and actual token-replacement effects in LLMs is structurally analogous to the gap between substitute-model gradients and victim-model effects in transfer attacks, allowing techniques from the latter to be repurposed for the former. The authors adapt SGM into LSGM (decaying residual-module gradients) and ILA into LILA (replacing intermediate gradients with directional guides at specific token positions), with the latter requiring a non-trivial adaptation after direct application of ILA failed. Mechanistic analyses via cosine similarity and causal tracing provide new evidence that skip connections, not residual modules, are the primary carriers of adversarial information in transformer LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The +30% absolute ASR gain over GCG on Llama-2-7B-Chat (38%→68%) is practically significant for red-teaming and robustness evaluation of safety-aligned LLMs, and the skip-connection insight may generalize to other discrete optimization problems in transformers such as prompt tuning. However, the evaluation relies on a model-based evaluator (HarmBench) with ~93% human agreement, and the white-box setting limits immediate practical deployment. Open questions include whether the skip-connection dominance holds across architectures and scales, whether the LILA token-level adaptation generalizes beyond the first target token, and whether these gradient-refinement strategies can be combined with emerging black-box or multi-turn jailbreak paradigms.
