## Problem

Existing jailbreak defenses for LLMs either require expensive parameter fine-tuning or rely on manually designed prompt heuristics that lack alignment with the model's training distribution, leading to high false-positive rates. How to achieve intrinsic robustness against adversarial jailbreak prompts through learned prompt optimization—without modifying model weights—remained an open problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242048/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference time, the method prepends a learned 15-token defense control prefix to the user's prompt (which may itself contain a 20-token adversarial attack suffix) before feeding the concatenated sequence to the LLM. The output is the model's standard text response, which should be a refusal for malicious inputs and a helpful answer for benign ones. The defense prefix is a fixed, model-specific token sequence that can be generated once and applied as a plug-and-play guard across multiple models, incurring only negligible token overhead.

## Method

PAT formulates jailbreak defense as a min-max prompt optimization problem: an attack control (20 tokens) is optimized to minimize the loss toward a malicious target (e.g., 'Sure, here is how to build a bomb'), while a defense control (15 tokens) is simultaneously optimized to minimize a weighted combination of the loss toward a safe refusal target and the loss toward a benign answer target (weighted by α). The two controls are updated alternately using a greedy coordinate gradient strategy over discrete tokens—computing one-hot token gradients, selecting top-k replacement candidates per position, generating B random candidate sequences, and keeping the one with the lowest validation loss. This adversarial co-optimization is performed over 25 harmful prompts and 100 benign MS MARCO questions for 100 epochs. A multi-model extension aggregates gradients and losses across surrogate models (e.g., Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B) to produce a single defense prefix that transfers to both open-source and closed-source targets like GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.

## Results

On the AdvBench dataset, PAT achieves an average Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 1.8% on Vicuna-7B and 0.8% on Llama-2-7B, reducing advanced jailbreak attacks (GCG, AutoDAN, ICA, PAIR, TAP) to near-zero success. Crucially, PAT also attains the highest benign utility scores: 6.68 on MT-bench for Vicuna-7B (exceeding the unguarded model's 6.55) and 6.78 for Llama-2-7B (exceeding 6.75), while MMLU scores remain competitive at 50.9 and 50.2 respectively. In the black-box transfer setting, a defense control crafted on Llama-3-8B reduces GCG's ASR on Vicuna-7B from 92% to 2%, and on closed-source GPT-4, PAT achieves 0% ASR against GCG, AutoDAN, and ICA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAT outperforms all six baselines on average ASR for Vicuna-7B (1.8% vs. 4.2% for SafeDecoding, 10.0% for DRO, 16.8% for ICD, 24.8% for SmoothLLM, 52.4% for PPL, and 70.8% for Self-reminder). On Llama-2-7B, PAT ties SafeDecoding at 0.8% average ASR but substantially outperforms DRO (1.0%), ICD (1.2%), Self-reminder (1.4%), SmoothLLM (2.2%), and PPL (25.4%). The critical differentiator is benign utility: PAT achieves the highest MT-bench score on both models (6.68 and 6.78), exceeding even the unguarded models, whereas SmoothLLM drops MT-bench to 4.55/5.81 and MMLU to 39.3/38.9, and ICD drops MT-bench to 5.98 on Llama-2-7B. PPL is only effective against GCG (0% ASR) but completely fails against PAIR (79% on Vicuna-7B) and AutoDAN (72%), while Self-reminder provides no protection on Vicuna-7B (identical 70.8% average ASR as no defense). On closed-source GPT-4, PAT achieves 0% ASR against GCG, AutoDAN, and ICA while maintaining an MT-bench score of 8.77 (vs. 9.32 unguarded), outperforming ICD (6.67), Self-reminder (6.28), and SmoothLLM (7.56) on utility.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAT repurposes the min-max adversarial training paradigm from the image domain (Madry et al., 2017) to the LLM jailbreak defense setting, but operates entirely at the prompt level rather than modifying model weights. It unifies two previously separate defense families—training-based fine-tuning (which is computationally expensive) and heuristic prompt-based defenses like Self-reminder and DRO (which lack alignment with the model's training distribution)—into a single optimization loop that alternately updates an attack control and a defense control. The optimization mechanism (greedy coordinate gradient over discrete tokens) is directly inherited from the GCG attack framework, but the objective is inverted: instead of maximizing harmful output likelihood, the defense control minimizes it while simultaneously preserving benign utility through a weighted loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PAT offers a practically deployable, plug-and-play defense (a 15-token prefix) that requires no model retraining and transfers across open-source and closed-source architectures, making it attractive for model providers who cannot or will not fine-tune their systems. However, the adaptive attack experiments reveal that when the defense prefix is known to the attacker, ASR recovers substantially (e.g., 23% for GCG on Vicuna-7B vs. 1% under grey-box), indicating the defense is not truly robust in a white-box threat model. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to rapidly evolving attack taxonomies, how it interacts with multi-turn jailbreak strategies, and whether the 'mojibake' nature of the prefix (which aids cross-language transferability) introduces fragility against future attacks specifically targeting non-English token patterns.
