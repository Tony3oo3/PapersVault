## Problem

Single-task shallow ReLU networks trained with weight decay have non-unique solutions that live in a non-Hilbertian Banach space (BV²), making it impossible to predict which interpolant a given training run will produce. It was unknown whether multi-task training—where a single network fits multiple label sets on the same data—changes this picture, and whether the resulting solutions relate to the well-understood kernel method framework. This paper shows that multi-task training almost always yields a unique solution that coincides with a minimum-norm kernel (RKHS) interpolant, revealing a fundamentally different inductive bias from single-task learning.

## Input / Output

The method takes N data points x_i ∈ R^d with T-dimensional label vectors y_i ∈ R^T (i.e., T different regression tasks on the same inputs) and a fixed network width K ≥ N², and produces a multi-output function f: R^d → R^T. In the univariate case (d=1), the output is the connect-the-dots linear spline for each task, which on [x₁, x_N] equals the minimum-norm interpolant in the first-order Sobolev space H¹. The network includes an unregularized residual (skip) connection Ax + c, and weight decay is applied only to the neuron input/output weights, not biases or the skip connection.

## Method

The core result (Theorem 3.1) proves that for univariate inputs, the multi-task weight decay interpolation problem has a unique solution—the connect-the-dots interpolant—except on a Lebesgue-measure-zero set where consecutive slope-change vectors s_i − s_{i−1} and s_{i+1} − s_i are both nonzero and aligned. The proof hinges on a key lemma (Theorem 3.2) showing that removing any neuron (knot) located between data points never increases the representational cost ∑‖v_k‖₂, and strictly decreases it unless the adjacent slope-change vectors are aligned, via a triangle-inequality argument in R^T. This establishes that the individual task outputs coincide with the minimum-norm H¹ (Sobolev RKHS) interpolant, in contrast to single-task solutions which are minimum-norm in the non-Hilbertian BV² space. For multivariate inputs, a Taylor-expansion argument shows that as T grows, the per-task regularizer ‖v_k‖₂ ≈ ‖v_{k\s}‖₂ + v_{ks}²/(2‖v_{k\s}‖₂), making the individual-task subproblem approximately a weighted ℓ² (ridge) regression over a fixed set of ReLU features, i.e., a minimum-norm problem in a finite-dimensional RKHS with kernel κ(x,x') = Σ φ_k(x)φ_k(x')/‖v_k*‖₂.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that for univariate-input multi-task ReLU networks (T > 1, N > 1), the connect-the-dots interpolant is the unique solution to the weight decay interpolation problem with probability one under any absolutely continuous data distribution. This solution coincides on [x₁, x_N] with the minimum-norm interpolant in the first-order Sobolev space H¹, a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) with kernel k(x,x′) = 1 − (x−x′)₊ + (x−x₁)₊ + (x₁−x′)₊. In the multivariate case with large T, the individual task solutions are shown to be approximately minimum-norm in a finite-dimensional RKHS determined by the optimal neurons (Theorem 4.1), with the approximation error tending to zero as T grows. Empirically, single-task training (T=1) on a 2D dataset yields three visually distinct global minimizers, while multi-task training (T=101) produces nearly identical, smoother solutions across random initializations that closely match the RKHS approximation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between single-task (T=1) and multi-task (T>1) training paradigms for the same weight decay regularized ReLU network. In the univariate setting, single-task solutions are generally non-unique and correspond to minimum-norm interpolants in the non-Hilbertian Banach space BV² (per Parhi & Nowak 2021), whereas multi-task solutions are almost always unique and correspond to minimum-norm interpolants in the Hilbert space H¹ (an RKHS). In the multivariate setting, single-task solutions minimize an ℓ¹ penalty over the optimal neurons (Eq. 18), while multi-task solutions with large T minimize a weighted ℓ² penalty (Eq. 17), which is equivalent to a kernel ridge regression problem over a fixed RKHS. Empirically on a 2D dataset with 8 points, single-task training produces three qualitatively different global minimizers (Fig. 4a–c), while multi-task training with T=101 tasks yields a single smooth solution (Fig. 4d) that closely matches the RKHS solution (Fig. 4e). In a high-dimensional student-teacher experiment (d=5, 25 tasks), single-task networks recover the ground-truth single ReLU neuron per task with ~5 active neurons, while the multi-task network exhibits full neuron sharing (~150 neurons active for all outputs) and produces functions that deviate from the single-index ground truth, confirming the ℓ² vs. ℓ¹ inductive bias shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work characterizing weight-decay-regularized ReLU network solutions from the single-task to the multi-task setting. Parhi and Nowak (2021) and Hanin (2021) established that single-task univariate solutions are minimum-norm interpolants in the non-Hilbertian Banach space BV², while Shenouda et al. (2024) showed that multi-task training encourages neuron sharing. The key departure here is that the authors prove multi-task solutions almost always live in a Sobolev RKHS (H¹) rather than a Banach space, and that this holds even when tasks are completely unrelated—contrasting with the traditional multi-task learning literature (Caruana 1997, Ben-David & Schuller 2003) which assumes task similarity as a prerequisite for benefit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result reveals a previously unknown mechanism by which multi-task training changes the inductive bias of neural networks: it effectively converts a non-unique Banach-space optimization into a unique kernel regression problem, potentially explaining why multi-task models exhibit greater robustness and consistency across initializations. The univariate result is fully proven with a clean measure-zero argument, but the multivariate extension relies on asymptotic approximations (large T) and empirical evidence, leaving the precise kernel characterization open. Whether gradient descent actually converges to these global solutions, and whether the phenomenon extends to deep architectures, remain important open questions for connecting this theory to practical training dynamics.
