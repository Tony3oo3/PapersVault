## Problem

Graph self-supervised learning models (e.g., GraphMAE, GRACE) have achieved strong downstream performance, but it remains unexplored whether all learned parameters are actually useful. This matters practically because the large parameter count makes full fine-tuning of both the GNN and prediction layer infeasible, forcing practitioners to rely on linear probing alone, which limits achievable performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243129/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained GNN (from GraphMAE, GRACE, or MaskGAE) together with a graph defined by its adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N} and node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×d}. It first randomly prunes neurons and layers from the pre-trained GNN to produce a slim variant, then jointly fine-tunes the slim GNN and a linear prediction layer while simultaneously optimizing learnable per-node de-correlation weights. The output is node class labels for the downstream task, with the intermediate node embeddings H ∈ R^{N×d_h} being explicitly de-correlated across dimensions during training.

## Method

SLIDE (SLImming DE-correlation Fine-tuning) combines two mechanisms. First, model slimming randomly removes 50% of neurons in each layer and retains only the first two layers of the pre-trained GNN, reducing fine-tunable parameters by ~70% while preserving over 96% of downstream performance. Second, during fine-tuning, a de-correlation module computes reweighted partial cross-covariance matrices between pairs of embedding dimensions using Random Fourier Features and learnable node weights W; minimizing the squared Frobenius norm of these matrices (an HSIC analogue) encourages the slim GNN to produce less redundant, more informative embeddings. The optimization alternates between updating the slim GNN and prediction layer via weighted cross-entropy loss and updating W via the de-correlation objective, enabling full fine-tuning of both components despite the reduced parameter budget.

## Results

SLIDE (using 2-half GNNs) consistently outperforms both linear probing (LP) and full fine-tuning (FT) across all three pre-training frameworks (GraphMAE, GRACE, MaskGAE) on six node classification benchmarks. With GraphMAE on Amazon-Computers, SLIDE achieves 90.85% Micro-F1 and 89.88% Macro-F1, surpassing FT (90.61/89.61) and LP (90.44/89.24) while reducing fine-tunable parameters by ~70%. On the large-scale Ogbn-arxiv dataset, SLIDE achieves 72.27% Micro-F1 and 53.03% Macro-F1 with GraphMAE, whereas FT simply runs out of memory. The model redundancy analysis shows that even randomly removing 51.6% of parameters retains at least 96.2% of original performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SLIDE is compared against two standard fine-tuning baselines: Linear Probing (LP, which freezes the GNN and only trains a prediction head) and Full Fine-tuning (FT, which trains all GNN parameters plus the prediction head). SLIDE outperforms LP on every dataset and metric across all three pre-training frameworks, with an average improvement of ~0.75% Micro-F1 and ~0.69% Macro-F1 on Amazon-Computers, because it can simultaneously fine-tune the slim GNN and the prediction layer. Against FT, SLIDE also wins or matches on most datasets (e.g., 90.85 vs 90.61 Micro-F1 on Computers with GraphMAE; 88.82 vs 88.66 with GRACE), while using roughly 70% fewer fine-tunable parameters. The most striking advantage is on Ogbn-arxiv, where FT is infeasible due to out-of-memory, yet SLIDE achieves 72.27% Micro-F1. In the appendix, SLIDE is also shown to be orthogonal to LoRA: SLIDE-LoRA (which only adjusts LoRA modules on a frozen slim GNN) slightly outperforms LoRA applied to the full GNN, confirming the de-correlation benefit is independent of the fine-tuning strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of graph self-supervised learning (GraphMAE, GRACE, MaskGAE) and the growing body of work on model redundancy in other domains (BERT, LLMs, vision transformers). It departs from prior GNN efficiency work (graph sparsification, lottery ticket hypothesis) by showing that redundancy in self-supervised GNNs is so pervasive that *random* neuron and layer removal suffices, without any structured pruning criterion. The de-correlation fine-tuning component is adapted from HSIC-based methods in vision [21, 20], repurposed here to reduce inter-dimensional correlation in node embeddings during the fine-tuning phase, and is explicitly positioned as orthogonal to parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like LoRA and Adapter Tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that over 50% of parameters in self-supervised GNNs are dispensable (with CKA scores >85% between slim and full representations) provides a concrete efficiency guideline for the graph ML community and makes full fine-tuning of both GNN and prediction layer feasible on large graphs where it previously caused OOM. However, the paper acknowledges the lack of theoretical grounding for *why* this redundancy arises, and the practical gains from SLIDE are modest (0.2–0.4% F1 improvements). Open questions include whether the redundancy is an artifact of the specific pre-training objectives (masking, contrastive augmentation), whether structured or learned pruning could push the reduction further, and whether the de-correlation objective generalizes beyond node classification to other downstream tasks.
