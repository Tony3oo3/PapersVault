## Problem

While semi-supervised learning empirically outperforms supervised and unsupervised learning on many tasks, a fundamental open question is whether there exist settings where combining labeled and unlabeled data is *provably* necessary for accurate classification. For high-dimensional sparse Gaussian classification, prior work established statistical-computational gaps in both the supervised and unsupervised settings separately, but no rigorous result demonstrated a regime where SSL is computationally advantageous over either SL or UL alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240636/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes L labeled pairs (x_i, y_i) and n unlabeled vectors x_i drawn from a balanced two-component Gaussian mixture with k-sparse mean difference, along with the known sparsity k and a screening factor β̃ ∈ (0,1). The labeled data is used exclusively for a one-shot variable-screening step (computing a difference-of-means vector), while the unlabeled data drives the subsequent PCA. The output is a k-element support estimate Ŝ and a unit-norm linear classifier vector v̂ whose sign is determined by the labeled data.

## Method

LSPCA (Label Screening PCA) operates in two stages. First, it computes w_L = (1/L₊)Σ_{y_i=1} x_i − (1/L₋)Σ_{y_i=−1} x_i and retains only the top p^(1−β̃) coordinates by absolute value, forming a reduced index set S_L that contains nearly all true support indices even when L is too small for supervised recovery. Second, it computes the sample covariance matrix restricted to S_L using the n unlabeled samples, extracts the leading eigenvector, and selects its top-k entries as the support estimate Ŝ; the final classifier is the leading eigenvector of the covariance restricted to Ŝ. The key insight is that a sub-threshold number of labeled samples suffices to reduce the ambient dimension from p to p^(1−β̃), making the unlabeled-data PCA step asymptotically consistent (since n/|S_L| → ∞), thereby bridging the statistical-computational gap that persists in both pure SL and pure UL. The paper proves that in the regime β ∈ (1−γα, 1−α) with 1 < γ < 2, LSPCA achieves near-perfect support recovery and vanishing excess risk, while under the low-degree likelihood hardness conjecture no polynomial-time SL or UL method can succeed in this region.

## Results

The main contribution is theoretical: in the blue region of the (γ, β) parameter plane (β ∈ (1−γα, 1−α) and 1 < γ < 2), the proposed LSPCA algorithm provably recovers the support of Δμ with probability tending to 1 and achieves zero excess classification risk as p→∞. In the orange region (β < 1−α and 1 < γ < 2), a low-degree likelihood ratio argument shows no polynomial-time algorithm can succeed. In simulations with p=10⁵, k=100, λ=3, and L=200 labeled samples, LSPCA and LS²PCA approach the Bayes-optimal classification error of ≈0.042, while all SL and UL baselines exhibit substantially higher errors and lower support recovery accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LSPCA and LS²PCA against five baselines: two UL methods (ASPCA and IPU), two SSL methods (LSDF and self-training), and one SL method (Top-K Labeled). In the regime where L=200 labeled samples are insufficient for supervised recovery (β < 1−α), all SL and UL methods fail to recover the support or achieve low classification error, while LSPCA and LS²PCA succeed by leveraging both labeled and unlabeled data. LS²PCA (which replaces vanilla PCA in step 2 with the IPU sparse-PCA procedure) consistently achieves the best support recovery accuracy and lowest classification error among all methods, approaching the Bayes error of ≈0.042. The self-training SSL baseline and LSDF show intermediate performance but remain well below LSPCA/LS²PCA, particularly as the number of unlabeled samples increases. The key empirical finding is that adding n=1000 unlabeled samples to a small labeled set (L=200) dramatically improves both support recovery and classification accuracy compared to using either data type alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the low-degree likelihood ratio hardness framework (Kunisky et al., 2022; Löffler et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2018), which was previously applied only to unsupervised detection of sparse Gaussian mixtures, to the semi-supervised setting. It also builds on the information-theoretic lower bound tradition for sparse normal means (Donoho & Jin, 2004; Ingster, 1997) and the spiked covariance / sparse PCA literature (Deshpande & Montanari, 2014; Birnbaum et al., 2013), combining them via a general data-fusion theorem (Theorem 2.3) that treats labeled and unlabeled samples as two measurement modalities. The LSPCA algorithm itself is a natural composition of Sure Independence Screening (Fan & Lv, 2008) for the labeled stage and PCA on the reduced dimension for the unlabeled stage, but the paper is the first to prove that this composition provably bridges a computational-statistical gap that neither stage can close alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous proof that semi-supervised learning provides a *computational* (not merely statistical) advantage in high-dimensional classification: there exists a parameter regime where no polynomial-time supervised or unsupervised algorithm can succeed, yet a polynomial-time SSL algorithm can. The result sharpens the understanding of when and why unlabeled data helps, moving beyond heuristic cluster/manifold assumptions to a clean information-theoretic and computational characterization. Limitations include the restriction to two-component spherical Gaussians, a fixed separation λ = O(1), and the reliance on the low-degree hardness conjecture rather than unconditional lower bounds. Open questions include extending the analysis to multi-component mixtures, non-spherical covariances, and identifying regimes where SSL improves error rates (not just computational feasibility) over both SL and UL.
