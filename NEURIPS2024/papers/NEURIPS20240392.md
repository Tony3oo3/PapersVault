## Problem

Online RLHF (e.g., PPO) and offline contrastive methods (e.g., DPO, IPO) were previously considered theoretically equivalent since both derive from the same minimum relative entropy objective, yet empirical work consistently shows online methods outperform offline ones. This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical separation between the two, showing that the difference is rooted in dataset coverage: offline contrastive methods require a strong global coverage condition to guarantee convergence, while online RL methods only need a weaker partial (local KL-ball) coverage condition. This explains why offline methods fail when the preference data lacks sufficient diversity, and motivates a new hybrid algorithm that recovers the benefits of online data while preserving offline computational efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two types of data: (1) offline preference triplets (x, y⁺, y⁻) where a context x is paired with a preferred and rejected response, and (2) online unlabeled samples (x, y) where y is generated by the current policy itself—no reward labels are needed for the online component. The output is a fine-tuned LLM policy that maps prompts to response distributions. A key distinction from pure DPO is that the online samples are drawn from the evolving policy during training (making the KL term on-policy), while the offline triplets remain static throughout training. The method is applied in a contextual bandit formulation where the entire generated token sequence is treated as a single action.

## Method

The paper's core theoretical contribution proves that global coverage (the reference policy must assign non-negligible probability to every response the optimal policy might use) is both necessary and sufficient for DPO/IPO to converge, while a weaker local KL-ball coverage condition suffices for RLHF. The mechanism behind the separation is that DPO's implicit reward model (β log(π/π_ref·Z)) is unbounded, so under partial coverage the reverse KL of the DPO policy can diverge to infinity, whereas RLHF's explicit bounded reward model guarantees bounded reverse KL. Motivated by this, the authors propose Hybrid Preference Optimization (HyPO), which optimizes the DPO contrastive loss on offline triplets while simultaneously minimizing an explicit reverse KL penalty computed on online unlabeled samples from the current policy. The KL term is optimized via RLOO (Reinforce with Leave-One-Out) with a stop-gradient on the log-ratio, making it a practical single-stage algorithm that avoids training a separate reward model or value network. Theoretically, HyPO inherits the partial-coverage guarantee of RLHF (Theorem F.1), and empirically it outperforms DPO on GPT-4 win-rate, reduces reverse KL by 30–40%, and mitigates the overfitting observed when training DPO for multiple epochs.

## Results

On the TL;DR summarization task, HyPO outperforms DPO on GPT-4 winrate by 4.3 points (46.44% vs. 42.17%) for the 1.4B model and 6.1 points (50.50% vs. 44.39%) for the 2.8B model, while simultaneously reducing reverse KL to the reference policy by ~40% (27.07 vs. 44.90 for 1.4B; 48.98 vs. 68.95 for 2.8B). On general chat (Llama-3-8B-Instruct fine-tuned on UltraFeedback), HyPO achieves 30.7 LC win rate and 32.2 win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, surpassing DPO (28.4 / 30.9) and the base model (26.0 / 25.3), while matching the base model on MT-Bench average (8.09 vs. 8.10). On Open LLM Leaderboard tasks, HyPO achieves the highest average score (65.51) compared to DPO (65.34) and the base model (65.07).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPO, HyPO consistently wins on all three TL;DR metrics: GPT-4 winrate (+4.3 to +6.1 points), reward model score, and reverse KL (reduced by 40–29%). On the general chat benchmark (Llama-3-8B-Instruct), HyPO beats DPO on AlpacaEval 2.0 LC win rate (30.7 vs. 28.4) and overall win rate (32.2 vs. 30.9), while DPO actually degrades MT-Bench performance relative to the base model (7.75 vs. 8.10 average) whereas HyPO preserves it (8.09). Against the unmodified Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct base model, HyPO improves AlpacaEval LC win rate by 4.7 points (30.7 vs. 26.0) and overall win rate by 6.9 points (32.2 vs. 25.3), while matching MT-Bench (8.09 vs. 8.10) and slightly improving the Open LLM Leaderboard average (65.51 vs. 65.07). The paper also notes that HyPO's winrate remains below that of full online RLHF (PPO), but HyPO avoids the need for a separate reward model and value network, preserving DPO's computational simplicity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard RLHF pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022) and the DPO reparameterization (Rafailov et al., 2023), which prior work had treated as theoretically equivalent. It departs from that equivalence narrative by importing the coverage framework from offline RL (Munos & Szepesvári, 2008; Zhan et al., 2022) to prove a formal separation: global coverage is necessary for DPO/IPO but only partial (KL-ball) coverage suffices for online RLHF. The proposed HyPO algorithm draws on hybrid RL ideas (Xie et al., 2021; Song et al., 2022) and the stop-gradient KL trick common in PPO-style training, but applies them to the contrastive DPO objective rather than a full RL loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The coverage-based separation provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirically observed performance gap between online RLHF and offline DPO, moving the discussion beyond anecdotal evidence. HyPO offers a practical middle ground that preserves DPO's computational simplicity while adding an explicit online KL term, though it still trails full PPO in winrate. Open questions include whether KL-ball coverage is truly necessary for online methods (vs. single-policy coverage with pessimism), whether the learned reward model in RLHF provides additional generalization benefits beyond what the theory captures, and whether these results extend to multi-turn or intransitive preference settings.
