## Problem

When an accurate feature-restricted model (e.g., a Kneser-Ney bigram) is available, existing methods like data noising and reverse knowledge distillation incorporate it by comparing the full model directly to the restricted model, which is a mismatched comparison. The paper shows this mismatch can make these methods inconsistent — failing to recover the true model even in the infinite-data regime — and asks what the correct way to align a restricted model's knowledge with a full-featured model is.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242001/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes full-featured context-prediction data (e.g., token sequences for next-word prediction) together with a pre-trained target restricted model (e.g., a Kneser-Ney bigram over the same vocabulary). The restricted model is used purely as a regularizer target during training; it is never fine-tuned or updated. The output is a standard full-featured model (LSTM, BERT, or logistic regression) whose predictions on the full context are improved, with the side effect that its induced (marginalized) predictions on the restricted context also align more closely with the target.

## Method

IMM introduces a secondary loss that matches the *induced* model Q̄ — the full model Q marginalized over the extended context given the short context — to the target restricted model P̂, rather than matching Q itself. The training objective is Cross-Entropy(Q) + λ·IMM(Q), where IMM(Q) is the cross-entropy between P̂ and the empirical induced model Q̂ computed by averaging Q's predictions over all extended contexts sharing the same short context. For tractability, Sampled IMM approximates Q̂ with k random samples of extended contexts (using a crosstalk-weighted gradient decomposition to avoid k-fold memory overhead), while Serialized IMM delegates the averaging to the primary data pass via a Radon-Nikodym correction factor, achieving O(1) overhead. The paper proves IMM is consistent for all λ in the realizable case, whereas noising and reverse-KD (identified as single-sample IMM) minimize an upper bound on the IMM risk and can be inconsistent.

## Results

On Penn TreeBank with a 1500-dim 2-layer LSTM, IMM with a Kneser-Ney bigram achieves a test perplexity of 73.3, improving over the no-noising baseline (77.5) and the KN noising baseline (73.9). On GLUE benchmarks with BERT_BASE, adding IMM to the MLM fine-tuning objective yields CoLA 60.0, MRPC 90.0, QNLI 93.5, and RTE 71.0, outperforming both the BERT_BASE baseline and the +MLM-only variant on all four tasks. In the logistic regression toy example, IMM consistently improves test accuracy and reduces variance across dataset sizes from 2 to 50, with gains equivalent to roughly 30% extra data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against KN noising (Xie et al., 2017), IMM achieves a lower test perplexity on PTB (73.3 vs. 73.9) and a lower validation perplexity (76.0 vs. 76.7), confirming that matching the induced model is strictly better than the single-sample approximation that noising implicitly performs. Against the BERT_BASE baseline (Devlin et al., 2018), IMM-augmented fine-tuning improves CoLA by 7.9 points (60.0 vs. 52.1), MRPC by 1.1 points (90.0 vs. 88.9), QNLI by 3.0 points (93.5 vs. 90.5), and RTE by 5.0 points (71.0 vs. 66.0), with the +MLM-only variant providing only marginal gains in comparison. Against vanilla REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) on the toroidal grid RL task, IMM-augmented REINFORCE achieves higher average reward at every dataset size and reduces reward variance, with the gap largest when the number of epochs is comparable to the number of policy parameters. The paper also shows that noising becomes inconsistent (underperforming even the baseline) when the regularization weight λ is held fixed, whereas IMM remains consistent, providing a theoretical advantage over both noising and reverse knowledge distillation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a principled unification and correction of two existing approaches: noising-based data augmentation (Xie et al., 2017) and reverse knowledge distillation from weak teachers (Yuan et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2021). The key conceptual departure is that both prior methods compare the full model Q directly against the restricted target model, whereas IMM compares the *induced* (marginalized) version of Q against the target, properly accounting for the fact that the teacher's weakness stems from restricted context. The paper shows that noising and reverse-KD are equivalent to a single-sample approximation of IMM, and provides a counterexample proving they can be inconsistent even in the infinite-data regime, while IMM is always consistent. The restricted models used (Kneser-Ney bigrams) build on the long line of N-gram language modeling work (Kneser & Ney, 1995; Chelba et al., 2017), and the RL experiment extends REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) with the IMM regularizer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The inconsistency result for noising and reverse-KD (Proposition 6.1) is a meaningful theoretical correction to widely-used techniques, showing that their empirical success depends on carefully decaying the regularization weight λ with data size—a practical caveat not previously formalized. The IMM framework is general (demonstrated on logistic regression, LSTM language modeling, BERT fine-tuning, and RL), but its practical impact is currently modest: perplexity improvements of ~0.6 on PTB and a few points on GLUE tasks. Key limitations include the computational overhead of computing the induced model (mitigated by sampled and serialized variants) and the requirement for an accurate restricted model. Open questions include whether IMM can meaningfully help scale context length in modern LLMs (speculated in the conclusion), whether the finite-sample advantage can be characterized analytically, and whether the method scales to very large transformer models without prohibitive cost.
