## Problem

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the leapfrog integrator is the standard tool for Bayesian neural network inference, but its theoretical guarantees (local error O(ε³), optimal acceptance 0.651, step-size scaling d^{-1/4}) all assume smooth potential energy functions. For ReLU-based networks, the non-differentiability at zero was assumed to be a measure-zero technicality with no practical impact. This paper shows that assumption is wrong: crossing a ReLU activation boundary inflates the local error to Ω(ε), fundamentally degrading HMC efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a Bayesian feed-forward network with a specified architecture (width, depth, activation function), a prior (standard normal), a dataset (synthetic 100-point regression or UTKFace 32×32 grayscale images flattened to 1024-dim vectors with age labels), and HMC hyperparameters (step size ε, number of leapfrog steps L, travel time T). It produces posterior parameter samples via Metropolis-accept/reject proposals, from which acceptance rates, efficiency curves (ε·A vs. acceptance rate), and posterior predictive MSEs are computed. The key comparative output is the efficiency gap between sigmoid (analytic) and ReLU/leaky-ReLU networks under identical tuning protocols.

## Method

The paper proves that leapfrog HMC on ReLU networks is correct (Theorem 3.1) whenever derivatives are well-defined up to second order and compatible with the chain rule, but then shows the efficiency is severely degraded. The central result (Theorem 3.3) derives that a single leapfrog step crossing k non-differentiability surfaces incurs a local Hamiltonian error of p_{1/2}·Σ(ε/2−εᵢ)(∂U⁺/∂q(zᵢ)−∂U⁻/∂q(zᵢ)) + O(ε²), which is generically Ω(ε) rather than the classical O(ε³). Lemma 3.4 shows the set of starting points where the crossing happens at the 'lucky' time ε/2 (making the error cancel) has Lebesgue measure at most (CaMε)^{1/α}, so the error is unavoidable for almost all trajectories. From this, Proposition 3.5 derives new optimal tuning guidelines for piece-wise affine log-densities: step size scales as d^{-1/2} (vs. d^{-1/4} for smooth cases), optimal acceptance probability drops to ~0.45 (vs. 0.651), and computational cost grows as d^{3/2} (vs. d^{5/4}). Experiments on synthetic data and UTKFace confirm that ReLU networks exhibit much steeper acceptance-rate decay with L and ε, and are 4× less efficient than sigmoid networks at their respective optimal operating points.

## Results

The paper proves that leapfrog HMC on ReLU networks incurs a local Hamiltonian error of Ω(ε) (vs. the classical O(ε³) for smooth networks), leading to a global error rate of O(ε) and an optimal acceptance probability of ~0.45 (vs. 0.651 for smooth targets). On the UTKFace dataset, the sigmoid network achieves an average acceptance rate of 0.5451 and average MSE of 0.0552, while ReLU and leaky ReLU networks achieve only 0.2239 and 0.1757 acceptance rates with MSEs of 0.1153 and 0.1151 respectively. At their respective best acceptance rates, all three activation functions yield nearly identical MSEs (~0.053–0.054), indicating the inefficiency is in sampling cost rather than final model quality. The step size must scale as d^(−1/2) for ReLU networks (vs. d^(−1/4) for smooth targets), increasing computational cost from O(d^(5/4)) to O(d^(3/2)).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares HMC performance across three activation functions (sigmoid, ReLU, leaky ReLU) rather than different sampling algorithms. Sigmoid networks dominate on efficiency: on the synthetic dataset, at the empirically optimal acceptance rate (~0.75), ReLU networks require more than 4× the computational cost of sigmoid networks per accepted sample. On UTKFace, sigmoid achieves a 0.5451 average acceptance rate versus 0.2239 (ReLU) and 0.1757 (leaky ReLU), a gap of roughly 2.4× and 3.1× respectively. However, when each network is tuned to its own best acceptance rate, the resulting MSEs converge to nearly identical values (0.0533–0.0539), confirming that the ReLU penalty is purely in sampling efficiency, not in the quality of the posterior approximation. The theoretical analysis further shows that the step size must shrink as d^(−1/2) for ReLU (vs. d^(−1/4) for smooth targets), making high-dimensional ReLU BNN inference substantially more expensive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical HMC error analysis of Neal (2011) and the optimal tuning framework of Beskos et al. (2013) and Betancourt et al. (2014) to the non-smooth setting of ReLU neural networks. While prior work assumed smooth potential functions yielding O(ε³) local errors and d^{-1/4} step-size scaling with a 0.651 optimal acceptance rate, this paper shows that the ReLU non-differentiability degrades the local error to Ω(ε), necessitating d^{-1/2} scaling and a lower optimal acceptance rate of 0.45. The paper also departs from prior work on discontinuous energy functions (Pakman & Paninski, 2013; Nishimura et al., 2020) by addressing the continuous-but-non-differentiable case specific to neural networks, where the potential is continuous but its gradient has jumps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This result challenges the widespread assumption that ReLU's non-differentiability at zero is a harmless measure-zero technicality, showing it fundamentally undermines the efficiency of the most natural MCMC method for Bayesian neural network inference. Practitioners using HMC on ReLU networks should expect significantly lower acceptance rates and higher computational costs (d^{3/2} vs. d^{5/4} scaling) than classical theory predicts. Open questions include how the number of non-differentiability crossings N scales with network architecture (width, depth), whether similar issues affect other non-smooth components like max-pooling in CNNs, and whether algorithmic modifications (surrogate functions, momentum adjustments) can recover the classical efficiency.
