## Problem

State-of-the-art reinforcement learning for robotics is gated behind expensive hardware (industrial arms at $10,000–$200,000+, quadrupeds at $74,500) and steep software learning curves (ROS, custom control stacks), making real-world RL training inaccessible to most researchers, educators, and hobbyists. Even low-cost DIY alternatives require 3D printers, workshops, and non-reusable parts, and none provide a unified interface between physical robot control and modern RL training frameworks.

## Input / Output

Observations are 5–8 dimensional vectors (motor angles in 0–360°, IMU pitch/roll in ±90°, ultrasonic distance in 0–2000 mm, and goal angles for the RoboArm) plus an optional 64×64 grayscale camera image in the mixed-observation setting. Actions are continuous values in [−1, 1] that are linearly mapped to motor-specific ranges (e.g., [−100, 100] for wheel motors, [−30, 30] for the RoboArm low motor) before being serialized as 4-byte floats over BLE stdin/stdout at up to 11 Hz (2 Hz for the Walker). The trained output is a policy network (3-layer MLP, 256 units, with a CNN encoder for image observations) that produces continuous motor commands in real time.

## Method

BricksRL bridges Pybricks (MicroPython on the LEGO hub, controlling motors/sensors over BLE) and TorchRL (agent, environment, and replay-buffer modules) through a custom PybricksHub class that manages bidirectional byte-stream communication via stdin/stdout, abstracting the asynchronous BLE link into TorchRL's synchronous EnvBase step/reset loop. Three LEGO robots (2Wheeler, Walker, RoboArm) are each paired with a dedicated client script that parses incoming action bytes, applies them to motors, reads sensor/IMU data, and packs the state back out. Training uses off-policy algorithms (TD3, SAC, DroQ) online on the physical robot, with simulation environments (WalkerSim-v0, RoboArmSim-v0) enabling fast pre-training and sim-to-real transfer, and offline datasets (expert SAC rollouts + random policies) supporting BC, IQL, and CQL. The platform's modularity is demonstrated by integrating a non-LEGO USB webcam as an additional observation channel, and by reusing the same LEGO parts across different robot configurations and tasks.

## Results

Across all seven environments, all three RL algorithms (TD3, SAC, DroQ) substantially outperform the random policy. On the 2Wheeler tasks, DroQ achieves the highest RunAway-v0 reward (8.96 ± 0.87) and TD3 the highest Spinning-v0 reward (7558.21 ± 28.61), versus random scores of −0.51 and 71.97 respectively. On the RoboArm reaching task, SAC achieves the best RoboArmSim-v0 reward (−3.45 ± 2.66) and a 100% success rate when sim-trained, while on the mixed vision task (RoboArm-mixed-v0), SAC and DroQ both reach 68% success versus TD3's 8%. Real-world training completes in under 120 minutes on a standard laptop (e.g., ~15 min for 2Wheeler, ~70 min for Walker, ~1–2 h for RoboArm).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three off-policy RL algorithms—TD3, SAC, and DroQ—against a random policy baseline across seven real-world and simulated LEGO robot environments. All three algorithms consistently outperform random by large margins (e.g., Walker-v0: best algorithm −52.04 vs. random −191.99; RoboArm-v0: best −20.29 vs. random −149.26). No single algorithm dominates all tasks: TD3 leads on Spinning-v0 (7558.21) and RoboArm-v0 (−20.29), SAC leads on Walker-v0 (−52.04), RoboArmSim-v0 (−3.45), and RoboArm-mixed-v0 (−18.21), while DroQ leads on RunAway-v0 (8.96) and WalkerSim-v0 (−56.62). A notable finding is that on the vision-augmented RoboArm-mixed-v0 task, SAC and DroQ achieve 68% success rates while TD3 collapses to 8%, suggesting TD3's sensitivity to the added image noise. Sim-to-real transfer is demonstrated with sim-trained policies (marked *) achieving competitive or superior real-robot performance—SAC* reaches 100% success on RoboArm-v0—while reducing training time from ~1 hour to 1–2 minutes.

## Relation to Prior Work

BricksRL sits at the intersection of two existing open-source ecosystems: Pybricks for LEGO hub control and TorchRL for RL agent training, bridging them via Bluetooth bidirectional communication. It departs from the dominant ROS-based robotics stack by replacing it with a gym-like interface, and from high-cost research platforms (Franka, ANYmal, Boston Dynamics Spot) by using ~$400 LEGO starter kits as the physical substrate. The RL algorithms employed (TD3, SAC, DroQ, IQL, CQL, BC) are all standard off-policy or offline methods; the paper's contribution is the integration platform and the demonstration that real-world online RL training is feasible on such hardware, rather than any algorithmic novelty. It extends the long history of LEGO-based education (MINDSTORMS, WRO competitions) by introducing state-of-the-art RL as a trainable component rather than rule-based programming.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for education and early-stage research: a $400 robot trained end-to-end in under two hours on a laptop lowers the barrier to real-world RL experimentation by an order of magnitude compared to existing low-cost options that still require 3D printers, workshops, or Raspberry Pi setups. Open questions include whether the 11 Hz communication ceiling (and the counterintuitive finding that 2 Hz can outperform it via a frame-skip effect) limits applicability to faster or more dynamic tasks, and whether the platform scales to multi-agent or vision-heavy settings beyond the single-webcam demo. The paper explicitly leaves hyperparameter tuning, more complex robot morphologies, and transformer-based imitation learning as future directions, suggesting the current results are a proof-of-concept rather than a performance ceiling.
