## Problem

Diffusion models suffer from high cumulative latency due to their inherently sequential multi-step denoising process, where each step depends on the completion of the previous one. Existing distributed acceleration approaches rely on patch parallelism or iterative refining, which either suffer from low GPU utilization, high memory overhead, or require additional model evaluations. The paper addresses the gap of a true model-parallelism strategy that can break the sequential dependency chain without sacrificing generative quality.

## Input / Output

AsyncDiff is a plug-and-play acceleration wrapper around any pre-trained diffusion model (Stable Diffusion 1.5/2.1/XL for text-to-image, AnimateDiff for text-to-video, SVD for image-to-video). It consumes the same inputs as the base model—text prompts and/or reference images—and produces the same outputs (512×512 or 1024×1024 images, 16- or 25-frame videos) but with reduced latency. The method requires no retraining; it only partitions the existing denoising U-Net into N components distributed across N GPUs connected via NVLink, using NCCL broadcast for inter-device communication.

## Method

The core idea is to partition the denoising model ε_θ into N sequential components of roughly equal computational load, each assigned to a separate GPU, then break the inter-component dependency by exploiting the high similarity of hidden states across consecutive diffusion steps. After a short warm-up phase (w ≪ T steps of standard sequential denoising), each component at time step t uses the output of the preceding component at time step t−1 as an approximation of its true input, transforming the pipeline into an asynchronous process where all N components compute in parallel for different future time steps. A stride denoising mechanism further reduces latency by completing multiple denoising steps per parallel batch—skipping unnecessary component computations and halving the number of inter-device broadcasts. The per-step cost drops from the sum of all component latencies to the maximum component latency plus a small communication overhead, yielding speedups nearly proportional to the number of devices (up to 4.0× on four A5000 GPUs for SD 2.1) with only marginal quality degradation.

## Results

On SD 2.1, AsyncDiff achieves a 4.0× speedup (1.35 s vs. 5.51 s) on four NVIDIA A5000 GPUs with only a 0.38 drop in CLIP Score (31.22 vs. 31.60) and a modest FID increase (29.41 vs. 27.89). On SDXL, the method reaches 2.8× speedup (4.91 s vs. 13.81 s) with a CLIP Score of 31.70 (vs. 32.33 original). For video diffusion, AnimateDiff is accelerated by 3.8× (11.5 s vs. 43.5 s) and SVD by 2.7× (68 s vs. 184 s) while preserving CLIP Scores within ~0.7 points of the originals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Faster Diffusion [Li et al., 2023], AsyncDiff clearly wins on generative quality at the same 1.6× speedup: CLIP Score 31.59 vs. 30.84 and FID 27.79 vs. 29.95, while also using less GPU memory (5450 MB vs. 9692 MB). Against Distrifusion [Li et al., 2024], the two methods tie on CLIP Score at 1.6× (both 31.59), but AsyncDiff pulls ahead at higher speedups: at 2.3×, AsyncDiff scores 31.49 vs. 31.43, and at 2.7×, 31.40 vs. 31.31. Crucially, AsyncDiff achieves the 2.7× speedup with only 4 GPUs and 5580 MB memory, whereas Distrifusion requires 8 GPUs and 7280 MB. AsyncDiff also maintains lower LPIPS (0.1940 vs. 0.2934 at 2.7×), indicating better pixel-level consistency with the original output. Overall, AsyncDiff matches or exceeds both baselines on quality while using fewer devices and less memory at equivalent or higher speedup ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the model parallelism paradigm from distributed training (GPipe, PipeDream, Megatron-LM) and repurposes it for diffusion model inference, a setting where prior parallelism efforts focused on patch-level data parallelism (Distrifusion) or iterative Picard-style sampling (ParaDiGMS). The key conceptual departure is breaking the sequential dependency chain of denoising steps by exploiting the high similarity of hidden states across consecutive timesteps — an observation previously leveraged for caching (DeepCache, FrDiFF) but never used to enable true multi-device model parallelism. AsyncDiff thus carves new ground by treating the denoising U-Net as a pipeline whose stages can run asynchronously across GPUs, rather than splitting the input spatially.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: near-linear speedup (up to 4×) on commodity multi-GPU hardware with minimal quality loss, applicable as a plug-and-play wrapper to any pre-trained diffusion model without retraining. This opens a path toward making high-resolution image and video generation more accessible on multi-GPU workstations. Limitations include the dependence on fast inter-device communication (NVLINK/NCCL), the quality gap that widens at higher speedups, and the assumption that hidden-state similarity holds across architectures. Open questions include whether the approach extends to diffusion transformers with different block structures, how it interacts with few-step samplers that already reduce the number of sequential steps, and whether the communication overhead becomes prohibitive beyond 8 devices.
