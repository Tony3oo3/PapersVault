## Problem

Existing brain network analysis methods predominantly aggregate neighborhood (short-range) information via GNN message-passing or graph pooling, neglecting the long-range dependencies between anatomically distant ROIs that are critical for brain-wide functional integration. This limitation prevents an integrated understanding of multilevel communication across the entire brain and degrades performance on neurological disease diagnosis tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240771/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes preprocessed resting-state fMRI data (via DPARSF) formalized as brain graphs with 200 ROIs (Craddock 200 atlas, ABIDE) or 116 ROIs (AAL atlas, ADNI), where node features are Pearson-correlation functional connectivity values and the adjacency matrix is a binary threshold (≥0.3) of the same correlation matrix. Temporal fMRI time-series per ROI are additionally used to compute adaptive factors (pairwise Pearson correlations) that bias the random walk. The output is a binary disease-state label (ASD vs. control or AD vs. control) produced from a graph-level representation obtained via clustering-based pooling followed by an MLP classifier.

## Method

ALTER introduces an Adaptive Long-ranGe Aware (ALGA) strategy that computes adaptive factors from inter-ROI temporal correlations and uses them to bias a K-step random walk (R = (F_G ⊙ A_G) D_G^{-1}), producing long-range embeddings that explicitly encode multi-hop structural dependencies weighted by communication strength. These embeddings are projected to a learnable dimension via a linear layer and concatenated with the original node features to form input tokens for a standard transformer encoder (2 layers, 4 attention heads). The self-attention mechanism then adaptively integrates both the injected long-range embeddings and the short-range neighborhood structure within a unified representation. A clustering-based pooling readout aggregates node representations into a graph-level vector for supervised classification via cross-entropy loss. The key distinction from prior work is that the random walk transition probabilities are modulated by ROI-specific communication strengths rather than being uniform, enabling the model to preferentially traverse high-correlation pathways that reflect genuine brain-wide communication.

## Results

On the ABIDE dataset (ASD vs. controls), ALTER achieves 82.8% AUC and 77.0% accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline BrainNETTF (80.2% AUC, 71.0% ACC) by 2.6 and 6.0 percentage points respectively. On the ADNI dataset (AD vs. controls), ALTER reaches 78.8% AUC and 74.1% accuracy, surpassing BrainNETTF (76.5% AUC, 69.0% ACC) by 2.3 and 5.1 percentage points. Across all four evaluation metrics (AUC, ACC, SEN, SPE) on both datasets, ALTER consistently achieves the best performance among all 10 compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ALTER outperforms all nine baselines on every metric for both datasets. Among generalized graph learning methods, LRGNN is the strongest (71.5% AUC on ADNI) yet still trails ALTER by 7.3 AUC points; SAN, Graphormer, and GraphTrans all fall 7–19 AUC points behind. Among specialized brain network methods, BrainNETTF is the closest competitor (80.2% AUC on ABIDE, 76.5% on ADNI), and ALTER improves upon it by 2.6 and 2.3 AUC points respectively — a gap attributable entirely to the ALGA long-range encoding strategy, since ALTER without ALGA reproduces BrainNETTF's numbers exactly. BrainGNN achieves the second-best accuracy on ABIDE (75.1%) but lags ALTER by 1.9 ACC points and 11.2 AUC points. FBNETGEN, A-GCL, ContrastPool, and BrainNetGNN are all substantially outperformed, with A-GCL and BrainNetGNN showing particularly large standard deviations (up to 42.4 and 37.5), indicating instability. The consistent superiority across both datasets and all four clinical metrics (AUC, ACC, SEN, SPE) supports the claim that explicitly capturing long-range dependencies via biased random walk provides a meaningful advantage over neighborhood-aggregation-only approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

ALTER builds directly on BrainNETTF (Kan et al., NeurIPS 2022) as its base transformer architecture, with the ALGA strategy added as a modular extension. It departs from the dominant GNN-based brain network analysis paradigm (BrainGNN, BrainNetGNN, FBNETGEN) that relies on local neighborhood message-passing, and instead draws on random walk methods from general graph learning (LRGNN, GraphTrans) to explicitly encode long-range structural dependencies. The key conceptual departure is the use of inter-ROI Pearson correlation as adaptive factors to bias the random walk, making the walk non-uniform and reflecting varying communication strengths among brain regions—a property absent in standard random walk formulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Long-range connectivity is well-established in neuroscience as critical for brain-wide functional integration, yet prior computational methods for brain network analysis largely ignored it, limiting their representational capacity. The demonstration that ALGA transfers effectively to other architectures (SAN, Graphormer) suggests the biased random walk strategy is a broadly applicable component rather than a one-off trick. However, the work is limited to fMRI data and two relatively small datasets (especially ADNI with 130 samples), and the authors acknowledge that an optimal balance between short- and long-range dependencies remains unresolved. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to structural connectivity (DTI) data and whether the fixed 16-hop setting is truly optimal or dataset-dependent.
