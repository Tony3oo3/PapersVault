## Problem

As RL agents are deployed in real-world settings, predicting their future actions and events is critical for safety and human-agent interaction, yet no systematic comparison exists across different agent types. This paper asks: how predictable are explicitly planning, implicitly planning, and non-planning RL agents, and which auxiliary information—inner states (plans, hidden activations) or world-model simulations—is most informative and robust for prediction?

## Input / Output

The predictor takes a Sokoban grid-image state, the current discrete action, and auxiliary information that varies by approach: for the inner state approach, this is the agent's internal computation (e.g., the most-visited MCTS rollout for MuZero, all rollouts plus tree representation for Thinker, ConvLSTM hidden states at each internal tick for DRC, or the final convolutional layer for IMPALA); for the simulation-based approach, it is a 5-step deterministic rollout generated by unrolling the agent in a learned world model. The output is either the full sequence of the next 5 discrete actions (action prediction, scored by exact-match accuracy) or a single binary probability that a designated event (agent standing on a marked tile) occurs within 5 steps (event prediction, scored by F1).

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary prediction approaches evaluated under a unified supervised-learning framework. The inner state approach feeds the agent's intermediate computations (plans or hidden activations) as auxiliary tokens alongside the encoded current state into a 3-layer Transformer encoder, while the simulation-based approach instead feeds a 5-step rollout from a learned world model (Thinker's dual-network RNN with an added L2 raw-state loss) as the auxiliary sequence. All state images—current and rollout—are processed by a shared CNN encoder (two strided conv blocks with residual connections, average pooling, output 128×6×6 flattened to 128-d), concatenated with the action to form per-step embeddings, then passed through the Transformer (dim 512) whose first-token output feeds a linear head with softmax (actions) or sigmoid (event) activation. Training uses cross-entropy loss with Adam (lr=1e-4, batch 128) on 1k–50k transitions, and the key empirical finding is that explicit planning agents' plans yield substantially higher prediction accuracy (e.g., MuZero action accuracy jumps from 40% to 87%) and remain more robust under world-model degradation than simulation-based rollouts.

## Results

On Sokoban with 50k training transitions, the inner state approach yields a dramatic improvement for explicit planning agents: MuZero's action prediction accuracy jumps from 40% (state-action baseline) to 87% when given access to its MCTS plans, with Thinker showing similar gains. For implicit planning (DRC) and non-planning (IMPALA) agents, hidden states provide only moderate action prediction improvements and no benefit for event prediction. The simulation-based approach for DRC and IMPALA, using a high-quality world model, surpasses even the explicit planning agents' inner state approach on both action and event prediction, but is less robust under world model degradation (smaller model, stochastic dynamics, or POMDP settings).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between three sources of auxiliary information: (1) state-action only (baseline), (2) inner states (plans or hidden activations), and (3) simulation rollouts from a learned world model. The inner state approach clearly wins for explicit planning agents: MuZero's action accuracy rises from ~40% to ~87% with plan access, a 47-point gain that no other agent type matches. For DRC and IMPALA, hidden states give only a moderate action prediction boost (~15 points) and zero event prediction benefit, because the non-reward-relevant blue tile is not encoded in their representations. The simulation-based approach, when the world model is accurate, outperforms all inner state approaches for DRC and IMPALA on both action and event prediction, effectively matching or exceeding the 87%+ accuracy of explicit planning agents. However, under world model ablation (smaller model, stochastic dynamics, POMDP), the simulation-based approach degrades more sharply on action prediction than the explicit planning inner state approach, which remains more robust because MCTS plans only require accurate value/reward prediction rather than full state prediction. Event prediction under ablation is mixed, with neither approach consistently dominating.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Thinker framework (Chung et al., NeurIPS 2024) for its world model architecture and one of its four agent types, while also drawing on MuZero, DRC, and IMPALA as representative agents for explicit planning, implicit planning, and non-planning categories respectively. It departs from the safe RL literature (shielding, CPO, recovery RL) by focusing purely on prediction rather than action correction, and from opponent modeling in multi-agent settings by studying single-agent predictability. The paper positions itself at the intersection of these threads, connecting to human-agent interaction work on legibility and mutual predictability by providing a technical, agent-type-aware framework for quantifying how predictable different RL architectures are.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that explicit plans are substantially more informative for prediction than hidden activations, and that this advantage is more robust to world model degradation than simulation-based rollouts, gives deployers of RL agents concrete guidance on which auxiliary signals to monitor for safety. However, the evaluation is confined to Sokoban with a 5-step horizon and four algorithms, leaving open whether these findings transfer to high-dimensional, partially observable, or stochastic real-world domains like autonomous driving. The paper also flags the underexplored risk of false alarms in deployment, where a predictor's confident but incorrect forecast could trigger unwarranted interventions. Extending to broader environments, longer horizons, and stochastic/partially observable settings remains a key open question.
