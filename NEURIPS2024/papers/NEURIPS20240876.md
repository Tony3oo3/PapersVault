## Problem

Existing tree-based generative models for tabular data either learn a single tree (requiring large, complex trees to capture intricate densities) or use a set of trees where each generated observation relies on only one tree, failing to exploit the combinatorial power of the ensemble. This paper introduces generative forests where all trees jointly contribute to every generated observation, along with a simple boosting-based training algorithm that provides strong convergence guarantees on a generative loss, improving upon prior theoretical results.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular training sample (an empirical measure R over a mixed-type product domain X = ×X_i with categorical and numerical features) and produces generated observations by stochastically descending all T trees simultaneously and sampling uniformly from the intersection of the resulting leaf supports. For the side tasks, a partial observation with missing values is consumed to produce imputed values (by finding all potentially reachable leaves per tree and sampling from the maximum-density intersection), and a full observation yields a pointwise density estimate computed as the empirical mass in the leaf-support intersection divided by its volume, with complexity O(max_t depth(Υ_t)) for density and O(d·(Σ_t depth(Υ_t))²) for imputation.

## Method

A generative forest is a set of T consistent binary trees whose leaves' support intersections define a partition P(G) of the domain; generating one observation uses all T trees via stochastic star-node descent (STARUPDATE), where each step tosses a Bernoulli coin with probability equal to the empirical mass of the child's support relative to the current candidate set, progressively shrinking the support until all star nodes reach leaves. Training is performed by GF.BOOST, a greedy top-down algorithm that at each iteration selects the heaviest leaf across all trees and splits it using a weak splitter oracle that minimizes a supervised Bayes risk on the binary task (real data R vs. uniform U). The key insight is that this supervised boosting procedure exactly minimizes the likelihood ratio risk (a Bregman-divergence-based generative loss), and under a weak learning assumption WLA(γ,κ) the risk decreases at rate O(κγ²κ²·T·log(1+J/T)), paralleling classical boosting convergence. A single split in a GF can simultaneously refine up to 2·|P_λ(Γ)| partition elements (one per existing sub-partition within the split leaf's support), giving a substantial combinatorial advantage over single-tree induction and enabling even tree stumps to model complex densities.

## Results

On the LIFELIKE data generation task (T=500 trees, J=2000 splits), GF achieves Sinkhorn distances of 0.285–1.064 across 8 domains, beating Adversarial Random Forests (200 trees) on 7/8 domains and Forest Flows (50 trees, up to 6750 splits) on 6/8 domains. Against Vine Copula Autoencoders, GF wins on essentially all metrics across all domains (e.g., Sinkhorn 0.285 vs 0.392 on 'ring', Coverage 0.968 vs 0.364). For missing data imputation, GF matches or beats MICE.RF while using ~100× fewer trees, and for density estimation GF is statistically superior to KDE on more domains than vice versa.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Adversarial Random Forests (ARF, 200 trees), GF wins on Sinkhorn for 7/8 domains, Coverage for 6/8, Density for 7/8, and F1 for 6/8, with the clearest margins on structured domains like 'grid' (Sinkhorn 0.390 vs 0.394) and 'wred' (0.980 vs 1.099). Against CT-GAN (1000 epochs), GF dominates on Coverage and Density across all domains (e.g., Coverage 0.968 vs 0.798 on 'ring') and wins on Sinkhorn for 7/8 domains. Forest Flows (FF, 50 trees with up to 6750 splits) is the closest competitor: FF edges out GF on 'ring' (Sinkhorn 0.276 vs 0.285, F1 0.051 vs 0.070) and 'wred' F1 (0.458 vs 0.459), but GF wins on the remaining 6 domains for Sinkhorn and on most F1 scores. Against all three VCAE variants (Gaussian, Direct, Regular), GF wins on every metric across every domain by large margins (e.g., Coverage 0.968 vs 0.364 on 'ring' for VC-G). For imputation, GF matches or beats MICE.RF (100 trees) while using as few as 20 stumps; for density estimation, GF is statistically better than KDE on more domains than the reverse (Table 3).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generative trees framework of Nock & Guillame-Bert (ICML 2022) from a single tree to a set of trees, and departs from adversarial random forests (Watson et al., AISTATS 2023) by using all trees jointly to generate each observation rather than sampling one tree per sample. The training algorithm unifies the GAN/copycat training of generative trees with the classical boosting framework of Kearns & Mansour (1996), reducing it to a supervised two-class problem (data vs. uniform) that can be implemented with minor modifications to standard CART/C4.5 induction. The convergence analysis builds on the MODABOOST framework (Mansour, Nock, Williamson, ICML 2023) and removes the symmetry constraint on losses that limited prior generative tree theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that a single trained generative forest simultaneously supports data generation, density estimation, and missing data imputation with cheap inference (O(max depth) for density), while the supervised boosting formulation makes implementation accessible via existing tree libraries. The combinatorial expressiveness argument—that n stumps in a GF partition the domain into n^Ω(d) regions versus n+1 for a single tree—provides a principled explanation for why small ensembles of simple trees can match or beat much larger individual models. Open questions include how to automatically select the optimal (T, J) model size per domain, whether pruning can improve generalization, and whether the approach extends beyond tabular data where the bounded-domain and product-measure assumptions are natural.
