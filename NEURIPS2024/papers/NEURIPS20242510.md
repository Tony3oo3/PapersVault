## Problem

Large-scale calcium imaging now produces terabyte-scale datasets with up to a million cell candidates, making manual cell sorting (quality control to remove false positives) infeasible. Existing automated classifiers still require substantial human annotation to reach acceptable accuracy, and no large-scale benchmark existed to validate active-learning or other automated sorting strategies on brain-wide mesoscope data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242510/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

ActSort consumes a Ca²⁺ imaging movie and its cell extraction output (spatial footprints + temporal activity traces), which are jointly compressed into a ~270±90 MB/1,000-cells sorting file that retains only the top-5 Ca²⁺ event snapshots (10 frames each) per cell rather than the full movie. The 76-dimensional feature vector per cell is computed on-demand from this compressed format. Output is a binary label (cell / not cell) and a continuous probability score for every candidate, delivered through a point-and-click GUI that also displays spatial profiles, activity traces, and movie snapshots for human verification.

## Method

ActSort's core contribution is the DCAL (Discriminative-Confidence Active Learning) query algorithm, which adaptively combines two uncertainty scores: a confidence score (CAL) that selects unlabeled samples closest to the current logistic-regression decision boundary, and a discriminative score (DAL) that selects samples most different from the already-labeled pool to ensure diverse coverage. The combination weight is updated online based on the label classifier's accuracy, making DCAL practically parameter-free and converging toward pure CAL as annotations accumulate. Both classifiers are L1-regularized logistic regressions trained in real time on 76 expert-engineered features spanning temporal (SNR, spike statistics), spatial (circularity, eccentricity, area), and spatiotemporal (non-Gaussian noise contamination, neuropil correlation, movie-frame-to-spatial-profile correlation) dimensions. This design reduces required human annotation to 1–3% of candidates (vs. ~10–50% for random sampling) while matching or exceeding human-level balanced accuracy, validated on a first-of-its-kind 160,000-annotation benchmark across five mice and three imaging modalities.

## Results

On the hemisphere dataset (28,010 cell candidates, 112,040 annotations), DCAL achieves human-level balanced accuracy with ~3% annotated samples and converges within <10%, while random sampling requires ~10% for human-level accuracy and ~50% for convergence. In the combined multi-animal setting, DCAL reaches human-level balanced accuracy with <1% annotations and plateaus by ~3%. With pre-labeled samples from a different mouse, DCAL achieves human-level balanced accuracy at ~1% and converges by <6%, whereas random sampling does not reach human-level until ~8% and remains unconverged at 20%. Feature engineering improved false-positive rejection accuracy from 67% (traditional features) to 72% (new 76-feature set) while maintaining 97% true-positive acceptance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCAL consistently outperforms all three active-learning baselines (random, CAL, DAL) across every experimental setting. On the individual hemisphere dataset, DCAL reaches human-level balanced accuracy at ~3% annotations versus ~10% for random and intermediate values for CAL/DAL; the gap widens dramatically in the combined multi-animal setting, where both CAL and DAL 'massively underperformed DCAL, offering little improvement over random sampling' (Fig. 4E–G). In the pre-trained fine-tuning experiment, DCAL converges by <6% while random sampling remains unconverged even at 20% (Fig. 5B). Against the ResNet-50 unsupervised feature baseline [He et al., CVPR 2016], the expert-engineered 76-feature set achieves 97% true-positive rate versus ~90% for ResNet-50 features, while matching true-negative performance (Fig. S4, Appendix A.1). The paper also shows that cell classifiers trained on human annotations are more consistent (ICC = 0.64±0.07) than individual human annotators (ICC = 0.56±0.06), and that majority-vote ground truth (ICC = 0.79±0.05) provides a reliable evaluation standard.

## Relation to Prior Work

ActSort builds on two canonical active learning query strategies—confidence-based active learning (CAL, Culotta & McCallum 2005; Settles & Craven 2008) and discriminative active learning (DAL, Gissin & Shalev-Shwartz 2019)—and introduces DCAL, an adaptive combination that weights both scores based on the label classifier's running accuracy. It departs from prior cell-sorting pipelines (Suite2p, CAIMAN, EXTRACT), which either use fixed thresholding on a handful of quality metrics or train classifiers on randomly sampled annotations, by replacing random sampling with a principled query algorithm and by engineering 76 spatiotemporal features that mimic what human annotators actually inspect. The paper also positions itself within the broader trend of applying active learning to scientific annotation tasks (pathology, hemorrhage detection, melanoma) but is the first to target large-scale mesoscope calcium imaging, where false-positive rates and dataset diversity create distinct challenges.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ActSort addresses a genuine bottleneck: as calcium imaging scales to millions of neurons, manual cell sorting becomes infeasible, yet false positives can silently corrupt downstream behavioral analyses (the paper demonstrates that even rejected cells carry motion-artifact signals correlated with animal speed). The first-of-its-kind 160,000-annotation benchmark and the finding that individual annotators are substantially inconsistent (ICC ≈ 0.56) while classifiers trained on their labels are more consistent (ICC ≈ 0.64) provide a quantitative argument for semi-automated curation. Open questions include whether deep-learning feature extractors can replace the hand-engineered 76 features at scale, how annotator expertise modulates the active-learning loop, and whether the framework extends cleanly to multi-class sorting (e.g., dendrite vs. soma) or emerging voltage-imaging modalities.
