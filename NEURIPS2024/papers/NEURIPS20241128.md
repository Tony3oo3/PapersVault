## Problem

LLMs struggle with real-time new terms (new words, new phrases, old words with new meanings) due to knowledge cutoffs, yet existing benchmarks for real-time information focus on outdated content, limited fields, and rely heavily on human annotation, making annual updates prohibitively costly. No benchmark specifically targets new terms in a way that can be refreshed each year to track both model updates and the emergence of new vocabulary.

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests new terms and their dictionary definitions from Cambridge, Collins, and Oxford update logs (4.2k terms for 2022, 2.9k for 2023), then selects 300 terms per year stratified across three categories. It produces three open-domain NLU tasks per term: COMA (causal-reasoning multiple choice with 4 options), COST (fill-in-the-blank with 4 options drawn from the new term plus related terms), and CSJ (binary commonsense judgment). Evaluation is conducted zero-shot under two conditions—Base (no term meaning given) and Gold (term meaning prompted)—and the Base-to-Gold gap quantifies the performance cost of not knowing the new term.

## Method

The core contribution is a highly automated, LLM-driven construction pipeline that requires no human effort for annual updates. New terms are classified along three axes—frequency (via Google Search API), deducing difficulty (cosine similarity between an LLM's spelling-based guess and the gold definition, computed with Sentence-BERT), and word/phrase status—to select the hardest, rarest terms per category. For question generation, GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613) produces sentences containing the new term, which are then split into questions and correct choices using task-specific fixed phrases (e.g., 'As an effect' for COMA). Incorrect choices are generated indirectly: the pipeline first creates five related terms per new term (synonyms, antonyms, meaning-guessing terms, partial synonyms) and then uses those related terms to produce plausible-but-wrong completions, avoiding the LLM's tendency to generate weakly correlated distractors. A two-stage filtering step (LLM self-consistency check followed by optional human annotation) removes inconsistent questions, yielding 744 (2022) and 715 (2023) clean questions with over 80% agreement between automatic and human judgments, confirming that human filtering can be omitted for future annual updates.

## Results

Across 20+ LLMs, new terms cause a significant average performance drop of −25.63 points (Base vs. Gold setting), confirming that unfamiliar terminology substantially degrades LLM performance. The best-performing model, GPT-4-0613, achieves 66.56% average accuracy under Base on NewTerm 2022 and 76.07% on NewTerm 2023, while the weakest (Llama-2-Chat 7B) scores only 39.29% and 49.90% respectively. Under Gold settings (where term meanings are provided), top models reach 96–99% accuracy, indicating large headroom for prompt-based or retrieval-based improvements. Larger models within each series show +9.39 average improvement under Gold but do not reduce the Base–Gold gap (−25.13 for smaller vs. −26.94 for larger models).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, NewTerm does not propose a method to compare against baselines; instead, it evaluates 20+ LLMs (GPT-3.5/4, Claude 2/3, Llama-2/3) under two settings: Base (zero-shot, no term meaning) and Gold (zero-shot with term definition provided). The central comparison is Base vs. Gold, revealing a consistent −25.63 average accuracy gap attributable solely to unfamiliar terms. Among LLMs, GPT-4-0613 leads under Base (66.56% on 2022, 76.07% on 2023), while Llama-2-Chat 7B trails (39.29%, 49.90%). Knowledge-cutoff updates (e.g., GPT-4-0613 → GPT-4-0125) yield only +0.10 improvement on NewTerm 2023 but +3.57 on NewTerm 2022, showing that updated cutoffs capture some but not all new terms. Across LLM series, the overlap of learned new terms is limited: Llama, Claude, and GPT learn 38, 36, and 67 terms respectively in 2022, with minimal intersection, indicating that no single model family comprehensively absorbs the annual vocabulary update.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of real-time knowledge benchmarks (Kasai et al., Zhong et al., Cohen et al.) by shifting focus from new facts to new terms, and from NLG to NLU tasks. It repurposes established NLU task formats—causal reasoning (Gordon et al., SemEval-2012), fill-in-the-blank (Talmor et al., CommonsenseQA), and yes/no judgment (Clark et al., BoolQ; BIG-bench)—as the evaluation substrate for new-term comprehension. The key methodological departure from prior new-term work (Martinez et al.'s ChatWords, Zheng et al.'s NEO-BENCH) is a fully automated LLM-based construction pipeline that enables low-cost annual updates, whereas prior approaches relied heavily on human annotation or were limited to NLG tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides a sustainable, annually-updatable mechanism to track whether LLMs are actually absorbing new vocabulary as their knowledge cutoffs advance, a question that static benchmarks cannot answer. The finding that knowledge cutoff updates yield limited generalization to more recent terms, and that different model series learn largely disjoint sets of new terms, suggests that no single model update strategy will close the new-term gap. Open questions include whether the pipeline extends to multilingual settings, broader term sources beyond dictionaries, and whether improvement methods (model editing, retrieval, test-time adaptation) can close the 20%+ performance gap the benchmark reveals.
