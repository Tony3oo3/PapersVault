## Problem

Graph Neural Networks rely on high-quality node labels, but real-world graph data frequently contains noisy labels from unreliable sources or adversarial attacks. Although numerous GLN methods have been proposed, the community lacks a unified benchmark: existing works use inconsistent datasets, noise types, rates, data splits, and preprocessing, preventing fair comparison and deeper understanding of how graph structure itself interacts with label noise.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241206/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes standard attributed graphs (node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×d} and adjacency matrix A ∈ R^{N×N}) where labels on both training and validation sets are corrupted by either uniform (symmetric) or pair noise at rates from 10% to 50%, while test labels remain clean. It produces per-node class predictions evaluated via test accuracy, and additionally reports fine-grained metrics (ACLT, AILT, AUCS, AUU, AUIS) that decompose performance by whether unlabeled nodes are correctly supervised, incorrectly supervised, or unsupervised by their neighborhood. All 17 methods share identical GCN backbones, hyperparameters, and 10-run protocols across 8 datasets spanning citation, co-purchase, and social networks.

## Method

NoisyGL is a benchmark library rather than a new model: it standardizes 10 GLN methods (NRGNN, RTGNN, CP, D-GNN, RNCGLN, CLNode, PIGNN, UnionNET, CGNN, CRGNN) and 7 LLN methods (Forward/Backward correction, APL, SCE, Coteaching, JoCoR, S-model) under a unified API with consistent data splits, noise generation via label transition matrices, and GCN-based backbones. Six research questions drive the evaluation: whether LLN transfers to graphs, how much GLN methods improve over baseline, computational efficiency, sensitivity to noise rate, robustness to noise type, and the role of graph structure in amplifying or mitigating noise. Key empirical findings include that pair noise is the most harmful due to its misleading overfitting effect, that sparse graphs are disproportionately vulnerable to noise propagation through message-passing, and that graph-structure-augmentation methods (NRGNN, RTGNN, RNCGLN) most effectively counteract this spread by densifying the effective neighborhood during prediction.

## Results

Under 30% uniform noise, no single GLN method dominates all 8 datasets: NRGNN leads on Cora (74.86% vs. GCN 71.06%) and DBLP (76.21% vs. 69.66%), while CP leads on Citeseer (66.72% vs. 55.05%) and PIGNN on Pubmed (69.02% vs. 66.53%). On the heterophilous Flickr dataset, all GLN methods fail to beat the GCN baseline (49.77%). Pair noise at 50% causes the most severe degradation, nearly halving test accuracy across all methods on DBLP (e.g., GCN drops from 77.03% clean to 39.99%), whereas uniform noise at the same rate is less damaging. RNCGLN is the most computationally expensive method, taking 66.8× longer than GCN on Cora and 2945.8× on DBLP, and runs out of memory on Pubmed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 17 methods (10 GLN + 7 LLN) against a GCN baseline across 8 datasets. Among LLN methods, only APL and SCE provide marginal improvements over GCN (e.g., on Cora 30% uniform: APL 72.37% vs. GCN 71.06%), while Coteaching and JoCoR consistently underperform the baseline on dense graphs (e.g., Coteaching drops to 32.55% on BlogCatalog 30% uniform vs. GCN 69.36%). Among GLN methods, NRGNN is the most consistently strong, outperforming GCN on Cora, Citeseer, and DBLP across most noise levels, but underperforms on A-Photos and BlogCatalog. CP excels specifically on Citeseer (66.72% at 30% uniform vs. GCN 55.05%), while PIGNN leads on Pubmed (69.02% vs. 66.53%). On the heterophilous Flickr dataset, every GLN method fails to beat GCN, revealing a critical gap for heterophily. The paper further shows that pair noise is more harmful than uniform noise at the same rate (e.g., on Cora 50%: GCN 44.15% under pair vs. 54.42% under uniform), and that sparse graphs (Cora, avg. degree 3.9) suffer greater accuracy drops in incorrectly-supervised unlabeled nodes (AUIS 51.55%) compared to dense graphs (A-Photos, avg. degree 31.13, AUIS 87.01%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new method but instead consolidates the fragmented GLN literature into a unified benchmark. Prior work has produced individual GLN methods (NRGNN, RTGNN, CP, D-GNN, RNCGLN, etc.) and LLN methods (Coteaching, JoCoR, SCE, APL, S-model, Forward/Backward correction), each evaluated on different datasets, splits, and noise protocols, making cross-method comparison impossible. NoisyGL departs from this piecemeal evaluation by reimplementing all 17 methods under standardized backbones, consistent data splits, and uniform noise injection, thereby carving out the first systematic comparative study of the field.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that the field's implicit assumption—that LLN techniques transfer to graphs—does not hold, and that no single GLN method generalizes across all datasets or noise types, particularly on heterophilous graphs like Flickr. Open questions include instance-dependent label noise (which depends on node features and graph topology), extension to other graph tasks (link prediction, graph classification), and the need for methods that work on sparse, heterophilous graphs. The finding that graph structure augmentation mitigates noise propagation in sparse graphs points toward a concrete design principle for future GLN methods.
