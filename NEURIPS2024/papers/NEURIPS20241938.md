## Problem

Despite extensive evidence that LLMs encode substantial factual knowledge in their parameters, the fine-grained dynamics of how this knowledge is acquired and subsequently forgotten during pretraining remain largely unexplored. Specifically, it is unclear how each training step contributes to knowledge acquisition, how training conditions (model size, pretraining stage, batch size, data duplication) modulate this process, and what governs the rate at which acquired knowledge is lost.

## Input / Output

The method takes intermediate OLMo checkpoints (1B and 7B parameters, at ~170B, 500B, and 1.5T pretrained tokens) and resumes pretraining on the Dolma v1.5 corpus, injecting 120 GPT-4-generated fictional entity descriptions into batches every 100 steps under three scenarios (duplication, paraphrase, once). Evaluation uses 1,800 cloze probes (5 each for memorization, semantic generalization, and compositional generalization per knowledge passage), measuring the log probability of target spans at every training step. Outputs include step-wise log probability trajectories, effectivity scores (absolute log-prob improvement within a 50-step window post-injection), and retainability curves (fraction of improvement retained relative to the local acquisition maxima), from which power-law decay constants and x-intercepts (learnability thresholds) are extracted.

## Method

The paper introduces a controlled knowledge-injection protocol: fictional yet realistic entity descriptions (ensuring the model has never encountered the facts) are spliced into pretraining batches at fixed intervals, and the model's log probability on corresponding cloze probes is tracked at every step. Three acquisition depths are distinguished—exact memorization, single-sentence semantic generalization, and multi-sentence compositional generalization—each probed with five cloze items. The authors define three formal metrics: local acquisition maxima (the peak log probability within a 50-step window after each knowledge encounter), effectivity (the absolute log-prob gain up to that peak), and retainability (the fraction of total gain still present after additional training steps). By varying model size (1B vs. 7B), pretraining stage (early/mid/late), batch size (2048 vs. 128), and injection scenario (identical duplication vs. paraphrased vs. single exposure), the study reveals that (i) effectivity scales with model size but not with pretraining tokens, (ii) forgetting follows a power-law in training steps, (iii) duplicated data accelerates forgetting of generalization, and (iv) larger batch sizes increase the learnability threshold, collectively explaining long-tail knowledge failures and the benefits of corpus deduplication.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that factual knowledge acquisition in LLM pretraining follows a power-law forgetting curve (R² > 0.80 for memorization and semantic generalization; R² > 0.65 for compositional generalization), with decay constants ranging from 0.14 to 0.26 for OLMo-7B depending on condition. Effectivity (immediate log-probability gain per encounter) does not significantly improve across pretraining stages (170B → 500B → 1.5T tokens) but does increase when scaling from 1B to 7B parameters. The anticipated x-intercept of the retainability curve (the 'learnability threshold') is approximately 11.0–12.1 log(tokens) at batch size 2048, dropping to 9.2–9.9 log(tokens) at batch size 128, meaning smaller batches shrink the set of learnable knowledge by an order of magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical dynamics study rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the 'comparisons' are across training conditions. (1) Duplication vs. paraphrase injection: duplication yields higher immediate effectivity for memorization but faster forgetting for semantic and compositional generalization (decay constant 0.24–0.26 vs. 0.20–0.23), explaining why deduplication improves downstream performance. (2) Model size: OLMo-7B shows consistently higher effectivity than OLMo-1B across all injection scenarios and pretraining stages, while the 1B early checkpoint exhibits markedly unstable dynamics. (3) Pretraining stage: effectivity is statistically indistinguishable between the 170B, 500B, and 1.5T checkpoints (confirmed under constant learning rate), contradicting the intuition that later-stage models acquire knowledge more efficiently. (4) Batch size: reducing from 2048 to 128 increases effectivity slightly but raises decay constants (e.g., 0.25 → 0.31 for duplication/memorization at mid stage) and shrinks the x-intercept from ~11.0 to ~9.5 log(tokens), meaning the model forgets acquired knowledge roughly 10× faster in token-space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on knowledge encoded in LLM parameters (Petroni et al., Roberts et al., Geva et al.) and memorization/forgetting dynamics (Tirumala et al., Lee et al.) by introducing a controlled, step-by-step analysis of factual knowledge acquisition during pretraining. While prior studies have examined what knowledge LLMs store post-hoc or tracked broad memorization trends, this work carves new ground by injecting synthetic factual knowledge at defined intervals and measuring fine-grained log-probability trajectories with novel metrics (effectivity, retainability, local acquisition maxima). It also connects to training dynamics literature (Xia et al., Teehan et al.) but focuses specifically on the acquisition-and-forgetting cycle for individual facts rather than aggregate loss or task performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic account of why LLMs struggle with long-tail knowledge (encounter intervals exceed a learnability threshold), why deduplication helps (slower forgetting of generalization), and why batch size matters (robustness to forgetting), offering actionable guidance for pretraining practitioners. Key limitations include evaluation only on OLMo (1B and 7B), reliance on synthetic FICTIONAL KNOWLEDGE rather than real-world facts, and no generation-based evaluation to directly link log-probability accumulation to decoded outputs. Open questions include whether the power-law forgetting and learnability threshold generalize across architectures, and how the dynamics change at very early training stages where the 1B model showed unstable behavior.
