## Problem

The inverse sensitivity mechanism achieves instance-optimality for differentially private estimation but only under an unbiasedness assumption requiring the most likely output to match the true value. This constraint prevents the method from exploiting asymmetric sensitivities—where changing one individual's data can increase a function far more than decrease it, as with variance, MSE, and cross-entropy loss—and blocks natural handling of unbounded data where local sensitivity is infinite. The paper asks whether deliberately breaking the unbiasedness constraint can yield substantially better private estimates precisely when sensitivities are asymmetric.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of n data points, privacy parameters (ε₁, ε₂), a target aggregate function (variance, cross-entropy loss, MSE, or MAE), and a data-independent granularity parameter β > 1 (fixed at 1.005 in experiments) that structures the search over potential outputs as the exponentially growing sequence t_i = β^i − 1. It produces a single differentially private scalar estimate of the function value. Unlike the inverse sensitivity mechanism, no data bounds are required to maintain accuracy, and the O(n) runtime is guaranteed by capping the sparse vector search at 50,000 queries (sufficient since β^50000 > 10^21).

## Method

The asymmetric sensitivity mechanism replaces the exponential mechanism of the inverse sensitivity approach with the sparse vector technique applied to a 'reflective inverse sensitivity' metric that is negative for outputs below f(x) and positive above it, with a 1/2 offset. The mechanism iteratively queries potential outputs in increasing order and halts at the first output where the noisy reflective inverse sensitivity crosses zero, introducing a slight downward bias but making the output distribution depend primarily on lower output bounds rather than upper ones. This is the key to exploiting asymmetry: when upper sensitivities are much larger or infinite, accuracy degrades gracefully instead of catastrophically, since the sparse vector technique becomes increasingly unlikely to proceed past the threshold crossing. For efficient O(n) implementation, the paper derives approximate upper and lower output bounds for each Hamming distance level (e.g., exact lower bounds for variance via a combinatorial argument, and simple additive upper bounds for bounded domains). The mechanism achieves (ε₁ + 2ε₂)-DP in general and the tighter (ε₁ + ε₂)-DP when the reflective inverse sensitivity is monotonic across neighboring datasets, which holds for all the paper's instantiations.

## Results

The asymmetric sensitivity mechanism (ASM) achieves substantially lower average absolute error than both the inverse sensitivity mechanism (ISM) and smooth sensitivity across all evaluated tasks and datasets, with the gap widening at smaller privacy budgets (lower ε). For variance estimation on five datasets (Diamonds, Abalone, Bike, Adult-age, Adult-hours), ASM consistently shows the tightest confidence intervals. For model evaluation, ASM outperforms baselines on cross-entropy loss (Adult, Diabetes, MNIST, CIFAR-10) and on MSE/MAE for regression (Diamonds, Abalone, Bike). The method also handles unbounded data with negligible accuracy loss compared to the bounded setting, a setting where ISM requires data-independent clipping bounds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: the inverse sensitivity mechanism (Asi & Duchi, 2020b) and smooth sensitivity (Nissim et al., 2007). ASM wins on every comparison across all datasets and privacy parameters for variance estimation, cross-entropy loss, MSE, and MAE. The advantage is most pronounced at low privacy budgets where the asymmetric structure of the sensitivities (e.g., variance can increase without bound but is bounded below by zero) allows ASM to exploit the tighter lower output bounds while the iterative sparse vector technique naturally suppresses the unbounded upper direction. ISM, being unbiased by construction, cannot exploit this asymmetry and must spread probability mass symmetrically, resulting in higher variance. Smooth sensitivity, which adds noise proportional to the (potentially infinite) local sensitivity, performs worst in the unbounded setting. The paper's theoretical guarantee (Lemma 4.1) confirms that as the upper sensitivity grows (increasing asymmetry), ASM's utility bound degrades only logarithmically while ISM's degrades linearly, making the advantage asymptotically unbounded.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the inverse sensitivity mechanism of Asi & Duchi (2020a,b), which selects outputs via the exponential mechanism based on the Hamming distance (inverse sensitivity) from the underlying dataset to each candidate output. The key departure is replacing the exponential mechanism with the sparse vector technique (Dwork et al., 2009), which introduces a controlled bias toward smaller outputs but enables exploiting asymmetric sensitivities—situations where changing one individual's data can increase a function far more than decrease it. This breaks the 'unbiased' assumption (most likely output equals true value) under which the inverse sensitivity mechanism was proven instance-optimal, and the paper positions this as a more natural navigation of the bias-variance tradeoff, connecting to earlier observations that bias was necessary for optimal mean estimation under subgaussian or bounded-moment conditions (Karwa & Vadhan, 2018; Kamath et al., 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that the method handles unbounded data naturally (critical for variance, MSE, and cross-entropy, which have infinite local sensitivity without clipping), while the inverse sensitivity mechanism requires data-independent bounds that introduce bias. The O(n) implementations for common statistics and ML metrics make this immediately applicable to private analytics pipelines. Limitations include the focus on one-dimensional, sample-monotone functions for the exact version (the approximate version extends to general functions), and the introduced bias, while small, means the estimator is no longer centered on the true value. Open questions include extending the asymmetric advantage to multi-dimensional outputs and characterizing precisely when the bias-variance tradeoff favors this mechanism over alternatives like smooth sensitivity or preprocessing-based approaches.
