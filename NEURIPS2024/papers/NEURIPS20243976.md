## Problem

Prior encoding-model studies that used word embeddings or large language models to predict fMRI responses during naturalistic language comprehension consistently revealed strikingly symmetric bilateral activation patterns, at odds with the well-established left hemispheric dominance for language. This paper asks whether the symmetric pattern is an artifact of using small or insufficiently complex models, and whether systematically increasing model complexity recovers the expected left-right asymmetry.

## Input / Output

The method takes fMRI BOLD time series from 49 English speakers listening to the Le Petit Prince audiobook (TR = 2 s, resampled to 4 mm isotropic, 9 runs of ~10 min each) and layer activations from 28 pretrained LLMs (124 M–14.2 B parameters across 8 families) fed with the same text, aligned to word onsets and convolved with the Glover HRF. Outputs are per-voxel brain correlation r-scores (averaged over 9 runs via leave-one-run-out ridge regression), a left-minus-right hemispheric asymmetry measure, and the slope of the scaling law relating r-score to log parameter count. Analyses focus on the 25% most reliable voxels (6,468 voxels, 3,297 left / 3,171 right) selected via a model-free inter-subject correlation procedure.

## Method

For each of 28 pretrained models (GPT-2, OPT, Llama 2, Qwen, Gemma, Stable LM, Mistral, and Mamba; all next-word-prediction base models), the paper extracts activations from every layer, aligns them to word onsets in the audiobook, convolves with the HRF, and fits a linear encoding model (ridge regression with nested cross-validation over 16 log-spaced alpha values) to predict each voxel's BOLD time series using leave-one-run-out prediction across 9 runs. The key manipulation is model complexity: brain correlation follows a scaling law (r = 0.95 with log parameters), and critically, the left-minus-right difference in brain correlation also follows its own scaling law (r = 0.89), meaning larger models progressively recover left lateralization. The asymmetry is not attributable to inter-hemispheric differences in inter-subject correlation (normalizing r-scores by ISC preserves the effect), and training itself—not merely architecture size—drives the emergence of asymmetry, as demonstrated with Pythia-6.9B checkpoints showing the left-right gap shifting from right-biased (untrained) to left-biased (fully trained).

## Results

Across 28 pretrained LLMs (124M–14.2B parameters), brain correlation on the 25% most reliable voxels increases linearly with the logarithm of model size (r = 0.95, p = 7.3×10⁻¹⁵). Critically, the left-minus-right hemispheric difference in brain correlation also follows a scaling law with model size (r = 0.89, p = 1.8×10⁻¹⁰), meaning larger models predict left-hemisphere activity disproportionately better than right-hemisphere activity. Brain correlation also tracks NLP task performance: r = −0.91 (p = 1.1×10⁻¹¹) with Wikitext-2 perplexity and r = 0.95 (p = 4.2×10⁻¹⁵) with Hellaswag accuracy. For the 10 largest models (>3B parameters), the raw parameter count no longer predicts brain score (r = 0.30, p = 0.41), but NLP performance metrics remain significant (r = −0.86 for perplexity, r = 0.83 for Hellaswag).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 28 LLMs against three non-contextual baselines: (1) purely random vectors aligned to word onsets, which yield brain correlations centered around 0; (2) fixed random embeddings (same random vector per word, 300d and 1024d), which produce small positive correlations that increase with dimensionality; and (3) GloVe 300d word embeddings, which outperform both random baselines but are substantially outperformed by all 28 LLMs. The key finding is that the left-right asymmetry is absent in all baselines (which are symmetric by construction) and emerges only as LLM size increases: the smallest models (GPT-2 124M, Mamba 129M) show near-symmetric brain maps, while the largest (Qwen1.5-14B, Llama-2-13B) show clear left-hemisphere dominance. Across model families, newer architectures (Mistral-7B, Qwen1.5) outperform older ones (OPT) at comparable parameter counts, and the non-Transformer Mamba family achieves brain scores at the lower end of the Transformer envelope. The asymmetry is not explained by signal-to-noise differences: normalizing r-scores by inter-subject correlation preserves the left-right scaling effect, and the interhemispheric ISC difference does not covary with the interhemispheric slope difference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the brain encoding model literature (Huth et al. 2016; Toneva and Wehbe 2019; Caucheteux et al. 2021; Schrimpf et al. 2021) by systematically varying the complexity of 28 pretrained LLMs from 8 families (including the non-Transformer Mamba architecture) and asking how model size affects the hemispheric distribution of brain scores. It replicates and generalizes the scaling law between model size and brain fit first reported by Antonello et al. (2024), extending it to multiple languages and model architectures. The key conceptual departure is from prior encoding studies that reported strikingly symmetric bilateral activation maps, which the authors show is an artifact of using small models: left lateralization emerges as a function of model complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding reconciles two previously contradictory bodies of evidence: computational encoding models showing bilateral symmetry and classic neuropsychology establishing left-hemisphere dominance for language in ~90% of individuals. The demonstration that asymmetry emerges during training (Pythia checkpoints) and parallels human developmental lateralization opens a productive line of inquiry into which representations (semantic, pragmatic, discourse-level) drive the leftward shift. Key limitations include reliance on group-average fMRI (individual-level confirmation limited to 5 subjects), the scaling law breaking down above 3B parameters, and the inability to isolate which specific representational improvements in larger models produce the asymmetry.
