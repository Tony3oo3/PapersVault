## Problem

Existing learning-based mesh movement (r-adaptation) methods require re-training from scratch for each new PDE type or boundary geometry, and suffer from mesh tangling (inverted elements) that breaks simulations. Conventional Monge-Ampère PDE-solver-based mesh movement is accurate but computationally expensive and fails on complex, non-convex boundaries. The paper asks whether a single trained network can move meshes universally across PDEs, geometries, and mesh sizes in a zero-shot, non-intrusive manner.

## Input / Output

The network takes as input the original mesh vertex coordinates (serving as positional encoding) and per-vertex monitor function values (e.g., Hessian- or gradient-based norms of the PDE solution), and outputs the adapted mesh vertex coordinates. Training meshes are small (~463–513 vertices on a unit square), yet the model generalizes zero-shot to meshes with 2,000–8,000+ vertices and arbitrary boundary shapes. The monitor function is decoupled from the underlying PDE, so any formula for m(x) can be used at inference without retraining.

## Method

UM2N is a two-stage architecture: a Graph Transformer encoder extracts vertex embeddings from coordinates and monitor values via self-attention over the mesh graph, and a multi-layer GAT decoder produces the displaced vertex coordinates, with the GAT's one-hop attention constraining movement to local neighborhoods to suppress tangling. The key training innovation is a PDE-independent dataset of 600 randomly generated Gaussian-sum fields, so the model learns the MA mapping in the abstract rather than for any specific PDE. The loss combines an element volume loss (averaged |Vol(t_a) − Vol(t_r)| per element, which penalizes negative Jacobian determinants and thus inverted elements) with a bidirectional Chamfer distance loss for vertex-to-vertex alignment. This replaces the coordinate loss used in prior work (M2N), and the ablation shows it is critical for robustness on complex boundaries where coordinate-loss variants fail.

## Results

On the Swirl advection case, UM2N achieves 35.93% PDE error reduction (ER) in 17.66 ms, closely matching the Monge-Ampère (MA) solver's 37.43% ER but at ~318× lower cost. On the flow-past-cylinder Navier-Stokes case (4,993 vertices), UM2N achieves 61.29% ER in drag/lift coefficients in 19.25 ms, while both MA and M2N fail due to mesh tangling within 30 time steps. On the Helmholtz case, UM2N reaches 17.08% ER in 4.86 ms, comparable to MA (17.95% in 3265.23 ms) and slightly better than M2N (16.32% in 4.62 ms). Performance benchmarks on an 11,833-vertex mesh show ~2,347× speedup over MA on an RTX 3090 and ~3,342× on an A100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the conventional Monge-Ampère (MA) solver [36], UM2N achieves near-parity in error reduction on Swirl (35.93% vs 37.43%) and Helmholtz (17.08% vs 17.95%) while reducing inference time by over two orders of magnitude (17.66 ms vs 5615.78 ms; 4.86 ms vs 3265.23 ms). Critically, on the flow-past-cylinder case and the Tohoku tsunami simulation with highly complex coastlines, the MA solver fails entirely due to mesh tangling or non-convergence, whereas UM2N produces valid meshes with 61.29% ER on the cylinder case. Against the learning-based M2N baseline [19], UM2N substantially outperforms on Swirl (35.93% vs 14.15% ER) and Cylinder (61.29% vs Fail), and marginally leads on Helmholtz (17.08% vs 16.32% ER) at comparable inference speed. The ablation study further confirms that the element-volume loss (vs. coordinate loss) is essential for preventing tangling on complex geometries, and that training on PDE-independent monitor functions (vs. PDE-specific solutions) is key to zero-shot generalization across problem types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the M2N line of work (Song et al., NeurIPS 2022), which first proposed GNN-based mesh movement networks trained on PDE-specific data, by replacing the coordinate loss with an element-volume loss, adopting a Graph Transformer encoder plus GAT decoder architecture, and—most critically—training on PDE-independent random Gaussian fields rather than solutions of a specific PDE. The underlying mathematical formulation is inherited from the Monge-Ampère optimal-transport mesh movement framework of McRae et al. (2018), which the paper treats as the 'auxiliary PDE' that the network learns to solve. The key conceptual departure from prior learning-based mesh movement is the decoupling of the monitor function (which encodes PDE-specific information) from the movement operator (which is PDE-agnostic), enabling zero-shot transfer across problem types.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-shot, non-intrusive applicability across PDE types, mesh sizes, and boundary geometries is a significant practical advance: practitioners can drop UM2N into any existing mesh-based solver without retraining, and the 2000–3000× speedup over the conventional MA solver makes adaptive mesh movement feasible at time-step frequencies previously impractical. The paper also demonstrates that the method succeeds where the MA solver fails (complex coastlines, cylinder boundary layers), suggesting it can handle non-convex and non-smooth domains that break the MA convergence theory. Open questions include extension to 3D, theoretical guarantees against mesh tangling, the dependence on a manually chosen monitor function, and whether the GAT's one-hop constraint limits performance for problems requiring large single-step deformations.
