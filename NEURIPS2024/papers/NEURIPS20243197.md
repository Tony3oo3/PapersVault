## Problem

NTK-based NAS methods (TE-NAS, KNAS, Eigen-NAS) assume infinite-width DNNs whose NTK values remain invariant during training, but real finite-width networks exhibit strong non-convexity that causes NTK to drift significantly, leading to unreliable performance predictions. The paper addresses this by proposing a lightweight training-based estimate that captures both the macroscopic non-convex loss landscape and the microscopic convergence speed of candidate architectures, achieving a new state-of-the-art accuracy-cost trade-off on NASBench-201.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a cell-based search space (NASBench-101 with 423K candidates, NASBench-201 with 15.6K candidates, or the MobileNetV3 open space) and a training dataset, but processes them through a heavily reduced architecture (single convolutional stem + two cell layers + 4×4 pooling, reducing parameters by 15–64×) and a reduced dataset (r representative CIFAR-100 labels selected via VGG-16 embeddings, K-Means clustering, and Farthest Point Sampling, with r=10 as default). The output is a single neural network architecture selected via evolutionary search, reported with its test accuracy and total GPU-second cost; the evaluation-free variant (MOTE-NAS-EF) outputs the top-1 MOTE-ranked architecture without any accuracy-based evaluation stage.

## Method

MOTE combines two terms computed during brief training (~7 GPU-seconds per cell): a landscape term that linearly interpolates between initial and trained weights at G=10 intermediate points, computes cross-entropy at each, and sums them to quantify loss-landscape flatness (macro view of non-convexity); and a speed term that sums per-epoch cross-entropy divided by epoch wall-clock time to measure convergence speed under standardized time expenditure (micro view). Both terms are normalized via Box-Cox transformation and summed, with lower values indicating better candidates. Two reduction strategies—reduced architecture and reduced dataset—keep MOTE generation at ~7 GPU-seconds while maintaining ~0.65 Kendall's Tau correlation with full 200-epoch accuracy. MOTE-NAS integrates this estimate into a Regularized Evolution loop: candidates are ranked by MOTE, top 10% undergo mutation (constrained by Euclidean distance in adjacency/operation matrix space), and the top-k survivors are finally evaluated by 12-epoch early-stopping accuracy to select the best architecture.

## Results

On NASBench-201, MOTE-NAS(k=20) achieves 94.32% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-10, 72.81% on CIFAR-100, and 46.34% on ImageNet-16-120, setting new state-of-the-art in the accuracy-cost trade-off among few-cost NAS methods. The evaluation-free variant MOTE-NAS-EF completes a full NAS run in ~0.6K GPU seconds (about 5 minutes) while still outperforming KNAS in accuracy. On ImageNet-1K, MOTE-NAS discovers a MobileNetV3-space architecture reaching 77.1% top-1 accuracy at 473 MFLOPs in only 0.1 GPU days, a 500× speedup over OFA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among NTK-based NAS methods, MOTE-NAS(k=20) outperforms TE-NAS (94.32% vs 93.90% on CIFAR-10), KNAS (94.32% vs 93.38%), and Eigen-NAS (94.32% vs 93.46%) on CIFAR-10, with similar margins on CIFAR-100 (72.81% vs 71.24%, 70.78%, 71.42%) and ImageNet-16 (46.34% vs 42.38%, 44.63%, 45.53%). Against the strongest LGA-based baselines, MOTE-NAS(k=20) edges out REA+LGA on CIFAR-10 (94.32% vs 94.30%) and CIFAR-100 (72.81% vs 72.42%), and surpasses RS+LGA on all three datasets. The evaluation-free MOTE-NAS-EF matches or exceeds KNAS accuracy (93.54% vs 93.38% on CIFAR-10) while being 4.8×–22.2× faster. On ImageNet-1K, MOTE-NAS (77.1%) surpasses ZICO (75.8%) and CARS-D (73.3%) with 4× lower search cost, though it trails OFA (77.7%) by 0.6% at a 500× speed advantage. In Kendall's Tau correlation (Fig. 7), MOTE outperforms SynFlow and TSE by 11%–45% on NASBench-201 and by 96%–104% on NASBench-101, confirming its superiority as a proxy estimate over both zero-cost and training-based alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the NTK-based NAS line (TE-NAS, KNAS, Eigen-NAS, LGA) by rejecting the infinite-width assumption that underpins those methods, instead proposing a training-based multi-objective estimate (MOTE) that combines a loss-landscape term (inspired by Goodfellow et al.'s linear weight interpolation) with a convergence-speed term (adapted from TSE). The search mechanism builds on regularized evolution (Real et al.), with mutation guided by matrix-encoded architecture distances borrowed from predictor-based NAS. Two practical reduction strategies—a stripped-down meta-architecture and a K-Means/FPS-selected subset of CIFAR-100 labels—make the training-based estimate cheap enough (~7 GPU-seconds per candidate) to be competitive with training-free alternatives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MOTE-NAS achieves a new state-of-the-art accuracy-cost trade-off on NASBench-201, and its evaluation-free variant completes a full search in under 8 minutes while still outperforming KNAS, making efficient NAS accessible on modest hardware. The broader implication is that theoretically elegant but assumption-heavy proxies (NTK) can be outperformed by simpler empirical signals (convergence speed, landscape flatness) when evaluated on finite-width networks, suggesting the field should scrutinize the practical transfer of infinite-width theory. Open questions include whether MOTE generalizes to the much larger NASBench-301 space, whether it can be combined with predictor-based or Bayesian-optimization search, and whether the reduced-dataset strategy remains valid for non-CIFAR tasks.
