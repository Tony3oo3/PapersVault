## Problem

Existing video restoration methods face a fundamental trade-off: parallel (sliding-window) approaches process multiple contextual frames simultaneously, incurring high memory and compute costs that scale with context size, while recurrent methods suffer from cumulative errors and cannot be parallelized during training. Additionally, most state-of-the-art methods rely on both past and future frames, precluding use in streaming or online settings where future frames are unavailable. TURTLE addresses this by learning a causal, truncated history model that borrows information from preceding frames without requiring future context or large parallel windows.

## Input / Output

At each timestep, TURTLE consumes a single degraded video frame (e.g., 192×192 patches during training, full resolution at inference) along with a truncated history of τ=5 previously processed frame feature maps stored in a running queue. The encoder processes only the current frame (historyless), while the decoder conditions on the motion-compensated history states. The output is a single restored high-quality frame at the same or higher spatial resolution (scale factor s for super-resolution). Training samples clips of γ=5 frames in parallel, while inference processes frames sequentially with recurrence, maintaining the full trajectory implicitly through the evolving history state.

## Method

TURTLE is a U-Net architecture whose encoder uses historyless feedforward convolutional blocks and whose decoder stacks Causal History Model (CHM) blocks at multiple stages. Each CHM has two components: a State Align Block (φ) that computes patch-level attention between the current frame's query patches and all history key patches, retaining only the top-k (k=5) most similar patches to implicitly compensate for inter-frame motion without explicit optical flow; and a Frame History Router (ψ) that performs cross-channel attention to score and aggregate the motion-compensated history features, accentuating relevant temporal information and suppressing irrelevant channels. The top-k masking (replacing non-selected scores with −∞ before softmax) prevents unrelated patches from introducing spurious correlations. Training uses standard L1 loss on parallel 5-frame clips with cosine-annealed Adam, while inference is fully recurrent, with the history queue evolving frame-by-frame to maintain the entire trajectory without storing all past frames explicitly.

## Results

TURTLE achieves state-of-the-art results across seven video restoration tasks. Headline numbers include 29.26 dB PSNR on nighttime video deraining (+2.53 dB over NightRain), 33.58 dB on real-world video deblurring (+2.0 dB over CDVD-TSP), 25.30 dB on 4× video super-resolution (+1.36 dB over EAVSR+), and 32.01 dB on video raindrop/rain streak removal (+0.99 dB over ViMPNet). On blind video denoising (Set8, σ=50), TURTLE scores 30.29 dB, outperforming BSVD-64 by +0.34 dB, though it trails BSVD-64 by ~0.3 dB on the DAVIS test set. TURTLE also reduces computational cost to 181.06 MACs (G) versus 527–1632 MACs for parallel contextual methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TURTLE outperforms all compared baselines on six of seven tasks. On nighttime video deraining, it beats NightRain [35] by +2.53 dB (29.26 vs. 26.73 PSNR) and MetaRain [47] by +5.77 dB. On real-world deblurring (BSD 3ms-24ms), it surpasses CDVD-TSP [44] and ESTRNN [83] by +2.0 dB (33.58 vs. 31.58/31.39 PSNR). On synthetic deblurring (GoPro), it edges out DSTNet [45] by +0.34 dB (34.50 vs. 34.16) and BasicVSR++ [7] by +0.49 dB. On video super-resolution (MVSR4×), it leads EAVSR+ [67] by a substantial +1.36 dB (25.30 vs. 23.94) and BasicVSR++ [7] by +1.60 dB. On VRDS, it exceeds ViMPNet [71] by +0.99 dB (32.01 vs. 31.02). The one task where TURTLE does not achieve the top score is blind video denoising on DAVIS, where it trails BSVD-64 [49] by ~0.3 dB, though it wins on the generalization set Set8 by +0.31/+0.34 dB at σ=30/50. Computationally, TURTLE (181 MACs) is 3–9× cheaper than parallel methods like VRT [34] (1632 MACs) and RVRT [33] (1182 MACs) while matching or exceeding their quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

TURTLE carves new ground at the intersection of two established video restoration paradigms: parallel sliding-window methods (EDVR, BasicVSR, VRT) that process multiple frames simultaneously, and recurrent methods (IFI-RNN, ESTRNN) that propagate features sequentially. Rather than committing to either, it introduces a Causal History Model (CHM) that stores a truncated history of latent features and uses top-k similarity-based retrieval to implicitly align and aggregate them, achieving the information fusion of parallel methods with the memory efficiency of recurrent ones. The causal (past-only) design departs from the bidirectional context used by nearly all prior SOTA methods, and Appendix D shows that in the idealized no-degradation, perfectly-motion-compensated limit, CHM reduces to a time-variant state space model, connecting it to the Mamba/HiPPO lineage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: TURTLE achieves SOTA on seven video restoration tasks while using roughly 6–9× fewer MACs than comparable parallel methods (181G vs. 1182–1631G), and it can process 1080p video on a single 32GB GPU where prior methods hit OOM. The causal, streaming-friendly design opens the door to online and real-time video restoration where future frames are unavailable. Limitations include difficulty with redundant texture patterns (e.g., zebra stripes) where top-k retrieval misaligns patches, and reduced effectiveness under severe degradation where spurious correlations dominate. Open questions include whether the truncated-history approach scales gracefully to very long videos or to 3D video understanding tasks, and whether the SSM connection in the idealized limit can be extended to handle real-world degradation more directly.
