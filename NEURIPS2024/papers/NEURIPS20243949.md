## Problem

LLM compression techniques (quantization, pruning, sparsification) are validated almost exclusively by benchmark accuracy, with the assumption that preserved accuracy implies negligible quality degradation. This paper identifies a critical gap: even when accuracy is unchanged (≤2% difference), compressed models exhibit large numbers of 'flips'—questions whose answers change from correct to incorrect and vice versa in roughly equal proportion—revealing that the user-visible behavior of the compressed model can be significantly different from the baseline despite identical aggregate scores.

## Input / Output

The evaluation framework takes a baseline 16-bit LLM and its compressed counterpart (quantized, pruned, or sparsified) along with a benchmark dataset containing questions with known correct answers. For each question, it compares the baseline and compressed model outputs to compute flips (the percentage of questions transitioning between correct and incorrect), KL-divergence (mean token-level distributional distance across all answer options), and standard accuracy. For generative tasks like MT-Bench, it additionally produces GPT-4-judged quality scores. All standard benchmark evaluations use greedy decoding for full determinism.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical study across six quantization schemes (BnB W8A8/W4A4, GPTQ W8A16/W4A16, AWQ W4A16, SmoothQuant W8A8), three non-quantization techniques (layer dropping, WANDA pruning, SliceGPT sparsification), four model families (Llama2, Llama3, Yi, Qwen2), and fourteen benchmark tasks. The key analytical contribution is the 'top margin' explanation: quantization introduces roughly uniform noise in output token probabilities, so answers with low top margin (low model confidence) are most likely to flip; since incorrect answers systematically have lower top margins than correct ones, incorrect→correct transitions roughly balance correct→incorrect transitions, preserving aggregate accuracy. The authors validate this by showing that adding Gaussian noise to weights reproduces the flips phenomenon, and demonstrate that flips correlate strongly with KL-divergence (Spearman 0.98 on MMLU) and with MT-Bench free-form generation quality (Spearman -0.75), establishing flips as a practical, interpretable distance metric for evaluating compression fidelity.

## Results

Across all quantization schemes, models, and tasks, accuracy is preserved within 0–2% of the 16-bit baseline, yet the flips metric (percentage of answers changing between correct↔incorrect) reaches up to 13.6% (SQ W8A8 on Llama2-7b MMLU). GPTQ W8A16 is the only scheme that preserves both accuracy and behavior, with flips ≤1% across all models. KL-Divergence and flips are strongly correlated (Spearman ρ = 0.981 on MMLU), and MT-Bench scores correlate negatively with flips (Spearman ρ = −0.75), confirming that high-flip compressed models produce measurably worse free-form generations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares seven compression techniques (BnB W8A8, GPTQ W8A16, SQ W8A8, GPTQ W4A16, AWQ W4A16, BnB W4A4, plus WANDA pruning, layer dropping, and SliceGPT) against the 16-bit baseline on accuracy and the proposed flips metric. GPTQ W8A16 is the clear winner on behavioral fidelity, achieving ≤1% flips across all models and tasks, while all other quantization schemes exhibit 2.5–13.6% flips despite matching accuracy within 1–2%. Among 4-bit schemes, BnB W4A4 consistently produces the most flips (e.g., 10.65% on Llama2-7b MMLU), followed by GPTQ W4A16 and AWQ W4A16; SQ W8A8, despite being 8-bit, produces the highest flips overall (13.62% on Llama2-7b). For non-quantization methods, WANDA pruning and layer dropping show a near-linear increase in flips with compression ratio even when accuracy is unchanged, and SliceGPT produces significant flips even at very low sparsity. Larger models are more robust: Llama2-70b shows roughly 1.4–1.6× fewer flips than Llama2-7b/13b under the same scheme, and harder tasks (MMLU, GSM8k) exhibit more flips than easier ones (PIQA, TriviaQA).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard practice in the LLM compression community of validating techniques solely through aggregate accuracy on benchmark tasks. It draws conceptual inspiration from prior work on compressed BERT models (Xu et al., 2021, who proposed label/probability loyalty) and compressed vision models (Hooker et al., 2020; Joseph et al., 2021, who showed bias increases despite preserved accuracy), but is the first to identify and systematically characterize the 'flips' phenomenon in LLM compression. The paper repurposes the well-known KL-Divergence as a distance metric alongside the newly proposed flips metric, arguing that compression should be evaluated as a fidelity problem (how close is the compressed model to the baseline?) rather than a capability problem (how well does the compressed model perform?).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work challenges a foundational assumption in the LLM compression field: that preserving accuracy on standard benchmarks is sufficient evidence that a compressed model is a valid drop-in replacement. The finding that up to 13.6% of individual answers flip while aggregate accuracy changes by <2%, combined with measurable degradation on free-form generative tasks (MT-Bench), suggests that current compression papers may be overclaiming quality preservation. Open questions include whether flips reliably predict degradation on arbitrary downstream tasks (the authors acknowledge it is 'only a warning'), whether the phenomenon extends to other optimization methods beyond quantization/pruning/sparsification, and whether more principled distance metrics than flips or KL-Divergence can capture user-perceived quality differences. The practical implication is that the compression community should adopt distance metrics alongside accuracy in future evaluations.
