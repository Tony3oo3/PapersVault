## Problem

Next-generation AI models produce outputs (long-form texts, videos, code) too large for human labelers to reliably evaluate, creating a fundamental bottleneck for scalable oversight. This paper formalizes the challenge in a goal-conditioned hierarchical RL setting where human feedback is bounded to trajectories of length at most max(H_h, H_l), and asks how hierarchical structure can be provably leveraged to scale up this limited supervision to train a full hierarchical policy.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a goal-conditioned hierarchical MDP with a known goal function g(s,a) mapping high-level actions to goal states, and receives human feedback constrained to trajectories of length ≤ max(H_h, H_l) — either scalar cardinal rewards for sub-MDP rollouts or pairwise preference comparisons between short trajectories. It outputs a high-level policy π^h and a collection of low-level sub-policies {π_{s,a}^l} whose joint return is within ε of the optimal goal-reaching hierarchical policy. A key structural constraint is that the high-level MDP's transitions and rewards are non-stationary because sub-policies are updated over time, and in the ordinal setting, high-level feedback must account for the current (or idealized) performance of sub-policies.

## Method

For cardinal feedback, Hier-UCB-VI adapts UCB-VI by designing a sub-MDP reward r_{l,h}(s',a') = r(s',a') + κ·1(h=H_l ∧ s'=g(s,a)) that penalizes non-goal-reachability with κ = max(2H_h H_l, C), which simultaneously stabilizes high-level transitions (using the idealized goal-reaching transition in the value backup) and prevents sub-policies from landing at bad states. The main structural result is that HRL regret decomposes additively into multi-task sub-MDP regret, so only low-level feedback is needed and sublinear regret is achievable. For ordinal feedback, the paper first proves a non-identifiability result showing low-level preference comparisons alone cannot rank sub-MDP returns across different goals, necessitating high-level feedback. Hier-REGIME then performs hierarchical experiment design: it first runs REGIME across all sub-MDPs with shared exploration to learn sub-policies, then runs REGIME on the high-level MDP using feature expectations under the learned sub-policies, analyzing two feedback types (current-feedback, which is unbiased but cognitively demanding, and idealized-feedback, which is biased but simpler) and showing the tradeoff between sample complexity and labeler cognitive load.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or datasets. The main quantitative results are regret/sample-complexity guarantees. Under cardinal feedback (Theorem 1, Corollary 3), Hier-UCB-VI achieves Õ(√K) regret, with a concrete bound of Õ(H_l^{3/2} √(max_{s,a}|S_{s,a}^l||A|) · √(|C(S,A^h)|·H_h·K) + H_h·H_l·√(|C(S,A^h)|·H_h·K)). Under ordinal feedback (Corollary 1), the sample complexity rates are O(N_l^{-1/4} + N_h^{-1/2}) for idealized high-level feedback, O(N_l^{-1/2} + N_h^{-1/2}) for current high-level feedback, and O(N_l^{-1/2}) when only low-level feedback suffices.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a theoretical paper, this work does not run empirical comparisons against baselines. Instead, it positions itself theoretically against two closest prior works: Robert et al. [22] studies goal-conditioned HRL and provides a sample-complexity lower bound but presents an algorithm (SHQL) without theoretical guarantees, whereas this paper provides a provably no-regret algorithm (Hier-UCB-VI) and shows HRL regret decomposes additively into multi-task sub-MDP regret. Wen et al. [25] studies HRL under the options framework with a Bayesian model-based approach requiring a prior over MDPs; this paper instead uses adaptive UCB exploration without prior knowledge and shows that a suitable sub-MDP reward (penalizing non-goal-reachability) induces a small effective set of exit states, making learning efficient even when the total number of possible exit states is large. Within the ordinal feedback setting, the paper compares two feedback types: current-feedback (labeler accounts for current sub-policy performance) achieves the better rate O(N_l^{-1/2} + N_h^{-1/2}) but requires higher cognitive load, while idealized-feedback (labeler assumes perfect sub-policy execution) is simpler but incurs bias that degrades the rate to O(N_l^{-1/4} + N_h^{-1/2}).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of goal-conditioned HRL (Robert et al. 2023; Wen et al. 2020) and scalable oversight / RLHF (Leike et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2021; Zhan et al. 2023), formalizing a problem that prior work in both threads had only addressed empirically or informally. It adapts UCB-VI (Azar et al. 2017) into a hierarchical variant (Hier-UCB-VI) and builds the preference-based algorithm (Hier-REGIME) directly on top of REGIME's experiment-design and MLE machinery. The key departure from prior HRL work is the bounded-human-feedback constraint and the resulting sub-MDP reward design that penalizes non-goal-reachability, which prior HRL analyses did not need to consider.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first provable guarantee that hierarchical structure can be exploited to scale up bounded human supervision, with the clean structural result that HRL regret decomposes additively into multi-task sub-MDP regrets (confirming a speculation in Leike et al. 2018). The non-identifiability result under ordinal feedback—that low-level preferences alone cannot rank across sub-MDPs and high-level feedback is fundamentally necessary—is a useful negative result that constrains future scalable-oversight designs. Open questions include how to learn good goal functions from limited data, how to practically implement current-feedback via AI assistance, and whether the linear-reward and tabular assumptions extend to the high-dimensional, non-linear settings relevant to LLMs.
