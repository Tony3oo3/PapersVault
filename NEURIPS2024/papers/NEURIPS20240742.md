## Problem

Dense Associative Memories (DenseAMs) achieve super-linear storage capacity but are typically formulated in a 'memory representation' where each stored pattern occupies its own row in the weight matrix, so adding new memories necessarily grows the parameter space. The paper asks whether the superior storage properties of DenseAMs can be combined with a 'distributed representation'—analogous to the classical Hopfield formulation—where the parameter space remains fixed and new memories are incorporated by redistributing information across existing weights, without adding new parameters.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query pattern (an occluded RGB image rasterized to a D-dimensional vector, or a D-dimensional binary vector normalized to {0, 1/√D}) and a single fixed-size distributed memory tensor T ∈ ℝ^Y that encodes all K stored memories via random feature projections. It produces the retrieved fixed-point pattern after L steps of energy gradient descent, along with intermediate energy values and gradients. Unlike the standard MrDAM whose memory matrix has shape (K, D) and grows with K, DrDAM's tensor T has shape Y regardless of K, enabling parameter compression (e.g., storing 20 images of 12288 pixels in a tensor of size 2×10^5, a ~20% reduction).

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the separation-similarity composition F(S[ξ^μ, x]) in the DenseAM energy with an inner product ⟨φ(ξ^μ), φ(x)⟩ using an explicit random feature map φ: ℝ^D → ℝ^Y (trigonometric SinCos features for the RBF kernel). This allows the sum over K memories to be precomputed into a single vector T = Σ_μ φ(ξ^μ), so the energy becomes Ê(x) = −Q(⟨φ(g(x)), T⟩) and the gradient depends only on T and φ, not on individual memories. Adding a new memory ξ simply updates T ← T + φ(ξ) in O(DY) time without changing the tensor size. The paper provides a tight theoretical bound (Theorem 2, Corollary 1) on the L-step divergence between MrDAM and DrDAM dynamics, showing it scales as O(√(D/Y)) under appropriate step-size choices, and depends on query proximity to stored patterns, inverse temperature β, and pattern dimension D. Empirically, DrDAM matches MrDAM's fixed points on Tiny ImageNet and CIFAR-10 image completion tasks when Y is sufficiently large relative to D and β is moderate.

## Results

DrDAM achieves a ~20% parameter reduction over MrDAM (Y=2×10⁵ vs. K·D=245,760 for 20 Tiny ImageNet images of 12,288 pixels each) while successfully retrieving all 20 memories with 40% occlusion. The theoretical divergence bound (Corollary 1) scales as O(√(D/Y)) with an appropriate step size, and is empirically validated: at β=10, D=100, and queries near stored patterns, energy MAE is near machine precision, while at β=50 the energy error grows ~10× (vs. β=30) and ~100× (vs. β=10). At high D (≥1000) and far queries, approximation quality degrades substantially, with β=50 errors approaching 1000× those of β=10. DrDAM recovers the same fixed points as MrDAM on Tiny ImageNet image completion (Fig. 1) and CIFAR-10 retrieval (Fig. 4B) under favorable conditions (low β, large Y, queries near stored patterns).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is MrDAM (the standard memory-representation DenseAM of Krotov & Hopfield, 2016), which stores each pattern in a separate row of the weight matrix. DrDAM matches MrDAM's fixed-point retrievals and energy values closely when queries are near stored patterns, β is moderate (≤30), and Y is sufficiently large (≥10⁵ for D~10³); in this regime the Hamming retrieval error is near zero and energy/gradient MAE is orders of magnitude below the random-guess baseline. However, DrDAM loses to MrDAM in approximation fidelity at high β (≥50) and high D (≥1000), where the random-feature kernel approximation introduces errors that accumulate over recurrent update steps, causing retrieval to degrade toward random. The key structural advantage of DrDAM over MrDAM is that adding a new memory requires only O(DY) time and O(D+Y) memory (Proposition 5) without growing the parameter tensor, whereas MrDAM's memory matrix grows linearly in K. In the parameter-compression experiment (Fig. 2), DrDAM with Y=2×10⁵ stores 20×12,288=245,760 parameters' worth of information in a 200,000-dimensional tensor, achieving ~20% compression while maintaining retrieval quality at 40% occlusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two well-developed lines of research: Dense Associative Memories (DenseAMs/Modern Hopfield Networks, Krotov & Hopfield 2016; Demircigil et al. 2017) and random feature approximations of kernel machines (Rahimi & Recht 2007; Choromanski et al. 2020). While prior work has applied the kernel trick to single-step attention or retrieval in associative memory (Iatropoulos et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2024) and random features to Hopfield network construction (Negri et al. 2023), this paper is the first to study the full recurrent energy-descent dynamics of a featurized DenseAM, providing rigorous bounds on how the random-feature approximation propagates through multiple gradient steps to affect fixed-point retrieval. The key conceptual departure is from the standard 'memory representation' (MrDAM) where each pattern occupies a separate row in the weight matrix, to a 'distributed representation' (DrDAM) where all memories are consolidated into a single fixed-size tensor T.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fixed-parameter-size property of DrDAM is practically significant: new memories can be added by updating existing weights rather than growing the network, enabling a form of parameter compression (demonstrated at ~20% reduction on Tiny ImageNet). However, the paper's own theoretical and empirical results reveal a fundamental tension: the approximation quality degrades sharply at high inverse temperature β and high dimensionality D, precisely the regime where DenseAMs achieve their exponential storage capacity. This limits the practical applicability of DrDAM to moderate-β settings. Open questions include whether hierarchical or multi-layer architectures can mitigate the β/D breakdown, whether learned (rather than random) feature maps could close the gap, and how the distributed representation interacts with the compression properties the paper explicitly leaves unexplored.
