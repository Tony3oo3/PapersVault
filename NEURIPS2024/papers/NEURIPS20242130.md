## Problem

While flatness of the loss surface and neural collapse have each been identified as useful pre-training metrics for transfer learning, the mechanism connecting them to the geometric complexity of learned representations remained unexplored. This paper addresses the gap by showing that the geometric complexity (GC) of a pre-trained embedding network directly controls its neural collapse, thereby providing a unified theoretical framework linking learning-path flatness, function-space complexity, and embedding-space clustering as a chain of implicit regularizers.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100/CIFAR-FS, 28×28 for MNIST/Fashion-MNIST, 84×84 for mini-ImageNet) and produces both class labels and a penultimate-layer embedding vector. Critically, all geometric complexity and neural collapse measurements are taken at the embedding/feature-map layer f (mapping R^d → R^p) rather than the full logit network, which distinguishes this work from prior GC analyses that focused on the softmax output. The embedding dimension p varies by architecture (e.g., 1024 for VGG-13), and the GC is computed as the expected Frobenius norm squared of the input-to-embedding Jacobian.

## Method

The paper proves that under a Poincaré inequality on the input distribution, the embedding geometric complexity GC(f, Q) upper-bounds the neural collapse NC(f, Q) via a 'geometric collapse' quantity that decouples intra-class variance (captured by GC) from inter-class mean separation (captured by inverse squared class-mean distances). It further shows the empirical GC is a robust, low-variance estimator that can be accurately approximated by sampling a small number of batch examples, Jacobian entries, or embedding dimensions, making it computationally tractable. A new generalization bound for the nearest-mean classifier is derived in terms of the embedding GC, and a transfer-learning bound (Proposition 5.1) shows that lower source-distribution GC promotes lower NC on unseen target classes, provided a granularity compatibility condition between source and target labels holds. Empirically, the authors verify these predictions by sweeping learning rate, batch size, and L2 regularization on VGG-13/11/16 and ResNet-18/50 across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR-FS, and mini-ImageNet, demonstrating that mechanisms reducing embedding GC consistently produce more neural collapse and higher few-shot transfer accuracy.

## Results

The paper establishes that neural collapse is bounded by geometric complexity: NC(f,Q) ≤ c·GC(f,Q)/(k−1) · Σ_{i≠j} 1/d²ᵢⱼ (Proposition 4.1), where c is the Poincaré constant. Empirically, the GC is shown to be a robust and computationally tractable metric, remaining stable when estimated from as few as a handful of samples, Jacobian entries, or output dimensions (Figure 2). The derived generalization bound (Proposition 4.3) is verified to be non-vacuous on VGG-13/CIFAR-10 with an estimated Poincaré constant c ≈ 1000 (Figure 3). In the transfer learning setting, lower source GC consistently produces lower target NC and higher 5-shot transfer accuracy on CIFAR-FS (ResNet-18) and mini-ImageNet (VGG-16) (Figures 4, 10), with the relationship holding across VGG-11/13/16, ResNet-18/50 architectures and CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, MNIST, and Fashion-MNIST datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new architecture or training method to benchmark against competing approaches; instead, it compares different implicit and explicit regularization mechanisms to verify a unified theoretical framework. Three implicit strategies—increased learning rate, decreased batch size, and increased L2 regularization—are shown to all produce the same qualitative chain: lower embedding GC → lower geometric collapse → lower NC → higher test accuracy (Figures 1, 6, 7, 8, 9). To rule out confounding from batch size and learning rate, the authors also apply explicit GC regularization directly (rates 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁴ at the logit layer), confirming the same GC→NC relationship in isolation (Figure 11). In the transfer learning experiments (Figure 4), the comparison is between pre-trained models with different source GC levels: models trained with higher learning rates or stronger L2 regularization (yielding lower source GC) achieve measurably lower target NC and higher 5-shot ridge-regression accuracy on unseen CIFAR-FS classes. The paper positions its contribution as a unifying theoretical lens rather than a new method, so no head-to-head accuracy comparison against prior transfer-learning baselines is reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads in implicit regularization research: loss-surface flatness (Barrett & Dherin, 2021), geometric complexity of learned functions (Dherin et al., 2021, 2022), and neural collapse in transfer learning (Galanti et al., 2022). It extends the geometric complexity measure from the logit layer (Munn et al., 2023) to the embedding/feature-map sub-network, proving that embedding GC bounds neural collapse via a Poincaré inequality. The resulting 'geometric collapse' quantity decouples intra-class variance (captured by GC) from inter-class separation (captured by mean distances), providing a new interpretable proxy metric that sits between the two existing concepts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners a computationally tractable progress metric (embedding GC, estimable from small batches and sampled Jacobians) that predicts both in-domain generalization and few-shot transfer performance, potentially guiding pre-training hyperparameter choices. A key open question is whether the granularity compatibility condition between source and target classes (target labels expressible as mixtures of source labels) holds broadly enough in practice, and whether the Poincaré inequality assumption is satisfied for real-world data distributions. The paper explicitly notes that extending neural collapse to language models is an open problem due to the embedding dimension being smaller than vocabulary size, limiting the framework's applicability to LLMs.
