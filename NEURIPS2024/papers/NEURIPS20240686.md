## Problem

Large-scale simulation models of complex socio-technical systems (e.g., agent-based epidemic models) are too computationally expensive for rapid policy experimentation, motivating the use of simpler surrogate models. However, existing surrogates are trained purely on observational data and offer no guarantee that they will behave consistently with the simulator under policy interventions, potentially misdirecting decision-makers toward suboptimal policies. This paper addresses the gap by formalizing the simulator–surrogate relationship through causal abstraction and providing a principled framework for learning surrogates that are interventionally consistent.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a black-box simulator (runnable to generate trajectories under specified interventions), a pre-specified aggregation map τ that reduces microstates to macroscopic counts (e.g., number of susceptible/infected/recovered agents per time step), and an interventional distribution η encoding which policy interventions matter to the decision-maker. It produces jointly trained surrogate parameters ψ (defining the structural equations of a probabilistic SCM with tractable multinomial emission distributions) and intervention-map parameters φ (a neural network mapping base-model intervention parameters to surrogate-equivalent parameters), enabling rapid forward simulation of aggregate population trajectories under any intervention in the learned intervention set.

## Method

The core contribution is a framework that treats both the complex simulator and its surrogate as structural causal models and defines an abstraction error as the expected KL divergence between the pushforward of the simulator's post-intervention distribution under τ and the surrogate's post-intervention distribution, averaged over an interventional distribution η. Surrogate parameters ψ and intervention-map parameters φ are jointly optimized by minimizing this abstraction error via Monte Carlo gradient estimation, where each gradient step samples an intervention from η, runs the simulator, applies τ, and backpropagates the negative log-likelihood of the resulting aggregate trajectory through the surrogate's tractable density. Three surrogate families are instantiated: a latent ODE (SIRS ODE feeding multinomial class probabilities), a latent ODE-RNN (SIRS ODE output passed through a GRU before indexing multinomial logits), and a latent RNN (GRU over intervention parameters directly indexing multinomial logits). The key insight is that training on data drawn from the relevant interventional distributions—rather than purely observational data—induces the surrogate to preserve the simulator's causal response to interventions, as confirmed both theoretically (a 0-approximate transformation recovers exact commutativity) and empirically (interventionally trained surrogates dramatically outperform observationally trained ones on interventional test sets, while observationally trained surrogates can even invert the ranking of policy interventions).

## Results

On the interventional test set I′, interventionally trained surrogates achieve dramatically lower error than observationally trained ones: the best surrogate (LODE-RNN, interventional) reaches AMSE of 3.35×10⁻¹ and ANLL of 1.99×10³, versus AMSE of 18.5×10⁻¹ and ANLL of 8.40×10³ for the same architecture trained observationally — a roughly 5.5× reduction in AMSE. On the observational test set O′, the gap narrows (LODE-RNN: 3.59 vs 2.52 AMSE), confirming that interventional training costs only a minor drop in observational fidelity. The LODE-RNN hybrid (SIRS ODE + GRU) consistently outperforms the pure LODE and LRNN families on both test sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between two training paradigms applied to the same surrogate architectures: interventional training (minimising KL divergence over interventional distributions) versus standard observational training. On the interventional test set I′, interventionally trained surrogates outperform their observationally trained counterparts by a wide margin — e.g., LODE-RNN drops from AMSE 18.5 to 3.35 (×5.5 improvement) and ANLL from 8.40 to 1.99 (×4.2 improvement). On the observational test set O′, the advantage reverses slightly (LODE-RNN: 2.52 vs 3.59 AMSE), but the degradation from interventional training is modest. Qualitatively, the observationally trained surrogates can misrank interventions entirely (e.g., predicting no-lockdown is best in some runs), while interventionally trained surrogates never make this error. Among the three surrogate families, the LODE-RNN hybrid (mechanistic SIRS ODE + GRU) achieves the best interventional and observational consistency, outperforming both the pure LODE (which lacks learnable parameters) and the LRNN (which lacks the mechanistic prior).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the causal abstraction framework of Rubenstein et al. (2017) and Beckers et al. (2020) to the problem of surrogate modeling for complex simulators, a connection not previously drawn. It departs from Beckers et al.'s worst-case (maximum) abstraction error by replacing it with an expectation over a policy-relevant interventional distribution η, and from Zennaro et al. (2023a) by targeting expensive black-box simulators rather than explicitly specified SCMs. On the surrogate modeling side, it extends the standard practice of training surrogates on observational data (Lamperti et al. 2018, Platt 2022) by jointly learning the surrogate's structural equations and an intervention map ω via KL-divergence minimisation, using differentiable surrogate families (latent ODEs, GRU-RNNs) with tractable multinomial emission distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are high: the paper demonstrates that observationally-trained surrogates can predict lockdowns *increase* infections and rank no-lockdown as the best policy, potentially misdirecting decision-makers during time-pressured crises. Key limitations include the reliance on tractable densities (restricting surrogate families), the expectation-over-interventions formulation that can mask poor performance on individual interventions, and the absence of any exploitation of the simulator's causal graph structure. Open questions include sample complexity of abstraction learning, extension to divergences that relax the tractable-density requirement (e.g., MMD), and whether do-calculus on large DAGs could reduce the number of base-model simulations needed.
