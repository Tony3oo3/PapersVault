## Problem

Stackelberg game algorithms deployed in security domains (airport screening, anti-poaching, cyber-crime) ignore payoff-relevant side information such as weather, traffic patterns, or network congestion that can shift both players' optimal strategies. This paper formalizes the online setting where a leader faces a sequence of contexts and follower types, and asks whether no-regret learning is possible. It proves a sharp impossibility result in the fully adversarial setting (via reduction from online linear thresholding) and then provides no-regret algorithms under two natural relaxations where one sequence is stochastic.

## Input / Output

At each round t, the leader observes a context vector z_t ∈ Z ⊆ R^d before committing to a mixed strategy x_t ∈ Δ(A) over A leader actions. The follower (one of K known types) best-responds to both x_t and z_t. In full feedback the leader learns the follower's type after the round; in bandit feedback the leader only observes the follower's realized action a_f ∈ A_f, never the type. The leader's utility u(z, x, a_f) is known and bounded in [0,1], and the follower's utility functions for all K types are known but the active type is hidden during play.

## Method

The key structural insight is that contextual best-response regions are convex polytopes, so it is nearly without loss of generality to restrict the leader to a finite set of δ-approximate extreme points E_z per context. For stochastic followers with adversarial contexts, a greedy algorithm (Algorithm 1) estimates the distribution over follower best-responses via empirical frequencies and plays the utility-maximizing strategy, yielding O(min{K, A_f} √T log T) regret. For stochastic contexts with adversarial followers, the leader plays Hedge over a finite policy class parameterized by weight vectors ω ∈ Δ^K (Algorithm 2), obtaining O(√(KT log T) + K) regret. Under bandit feedback, both settings leverage barycentric spanners to construct bounded or low-variance loss estimators for all leader mixed strategies from only K exploration samples, achieving Õ(T^{2/3}) regret via explore-then-exploit (stochastic followers) or block-wise Hedge with per-block estimation (stochastic contexts).

## Results

The paper establishes that no-regret learning is impossible in the fully adversarial setting (both contexts and follower types adversarial), with an Ω(T) lower bound via reduction from online linear thresholding. Under the two natural relaxations, the leader achieves Õ(√T) regret with full feedback: O(min{K, A_f}√(T log T)) for stochastic followers with adversarial contexts, and O(√(KT log T) + K) for stochastic contexts with adversarial followers. Under bandit feedback, the regret degrades to Õ(T^{2/3}): O(K^{2/3} A_f^{2/3} T^{2/3} log^{1/3} T) and O(K A_f^{1/3} T^{2/3} log^{1/3} T) respectively, using barycentric spanners for loss estimation.

**Table 1: Summary of regret bounds across adversary types and feedback settings.**

| Setting | Full Feedback | Bandit Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| Fully Adversarial | Ω(T) | Ω(T) |
| Stochastic Followers, Adversarial Contexts | O(min{K, A_f} √(T log T)) | O(K^{2/3} A_f^{2/3} T^{2/3} log^{1/3} T) |
| Stochastic Contexts, Adversarial Followers | O(√(KT log T) + K) | O(K A_f^{1/3} T^{2/3} log^{1/3} T) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The only empirical comparison is against the non-contextual algorithm of Balcan et al. [5] (EC 2015), which does not leverage side information. In synthetic experiments (K=5, A=A_f=3, d=3, linear utilities sampled u.a.r. from [-1,1]^{3×3×3}), both Algorithm 1 (stochastic followers) and Algorithm 2 (stochastic contexts) significantly outperform the Balcan et al. baseline across all three experimental configurations (non-stochastic contexts, stochastic contexts, and fully stochastic). The paper reports this as a qualitative finding from cumulative average reward curves over five runs (Figure 2), without providing specific numerical gaps. The comparison is most meaningful in the non-stochastic context setting (Figure 2a), where the baseline cannot adapt to changing contexts while the proposed algorithms can. The paper notes it would be interesting to find instances where each algorithm performs poorly when its respective assumption is violated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online learning framework for non-contextual Stackelberg games established by Balcan et al. (EC 2015) to the setting where both players observe a context before playing. The key algorithmic tools carry over: the discretization argument via approximate extreme points of best-response regions generalizes directly, and the Hedge-based approach for adversarial follower types is preserved. For the bandit feedback extension, the paper borrows the barycentric spanner technique from Awerbuch and Kleinberg (JCSS 2008) to construct bounded, low-variance loss estimators over the exponentially large set of extreme points. The paper also positions itself against contextual bandit literature, noting that off-the-shelf adversarial contextual bandit algorithms require transductive or small-separator assumptions that are not needed here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result for fully adversarial contextual Stackelberg games is a clean negative result that delineates the boundary of learnability: adding side information fundamentally changes what is achievable compared to the non-contextual setting. The two partially-stochastic relaxations are practically motivated (e.g., in security games, follower types may be drawn from a fixed population while contexts vary adversarially, or vice versa). Open questions include whether intermediate adversary models (e.g., finite Littlestone dimension for the context-to-type mapping) admit no-regret learning, and whether the O(T^{2/3}) bandit feedback rate can be improved to O(√T) via a reduction to adversarial contextual linear bandits, for which no known algorithm currently exists.
