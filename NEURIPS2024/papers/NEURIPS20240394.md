## Problem

Machine unlearning research lacks understanding of what makes a given unlearning request easy or hard, and how different state-of-the-art algorithms behave under varying difficulty conditions. Prior evaluations used random or adversarial forget sets, masking previously-unknown failure modes and algorithm-specific strengths. This paper identifies two interpretable factors—embedding-space entanglement between forget and retain sets, and the memorization level of forget examples—that systematically affect unlearning difficulty and algorithm performance.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB image classification models (ResNet-18/50, VGG-16) trained on CIFAR-10 (32×32), CIFAR-100 (32×32), or Tiny-ImageNet (64×64), with a forget set of size 3000. Beyond the standard unlearning inputs (original model weights, forget set S, retain set R), RUM additionally requires per-example memorization scores or the cheaper C-proxy (average softmax confidence over training epochs) to partition S into K=3 homogeneous subsets. The output is a single unlearned model produced by sequentially applying potentially different unlearning algorithms to each subset, where the retain set at step i includes R plus all not-yet-unlearned subsets.

## Method

RUM (Refined-Unlearning Meta-algorithm) consists of two steps: (1) Refinement, which partitions the forget set into K homogeneous subsets based on memorization scores (or the compute-efficient C-proxy, a confidence-based metric with Spearman correlation of −0.80 to −0.91 with memorization at <0.02% additional cost); and (2) Meta-unlearning, which sequentially applies the best-suited unlearning algorithm to each subset in a chosen order (e.g., low→medium→high memorization), where the retain set at each step includes the original retain set plus all remaining not-yet-unlearned subsets. The key insight is that different algorithms excel on different memorization levels—doing nothing suffices for low-mem, Fine-tune works best for medium-mem, and SalUn or NegGrad+ for high-mem—so per-subset algorithm selection yields the strongest results (ToW 0.965 on CIFAR-10 vs. 0.849 for vanilla Fine-tune). The paper also introduces the Entanglement Score (ES) to quantify how intermixed forget and retain sets are in the model's embedding space, showing that relabelling-based methods degrade disproportionately under high entanglement.

## Results

On CIFAR-10/ResNet-18, full RUM (refinement + per-subset algorithm selection: do-nothing → Fine-tune → SalUn) achieves a ToW score of 0.965±0.014 and MIA gap of 0.034, substantially outperforming the best single-algorithm vanilla baseline (Fine-tune at 0.849 ToW). On CIFAR-100/ResNet-50, full RUM (NegGrad+ RUM^F) reaches 0.921±0.034 ToW and 0.059 MIA gap. The refinement step alone (RUM^F) consistently improves every algorithm over both vanilla and random-shuffle sequential application, e.g., boosting SalUn from 0.731 to 0.887 ToW on CIFAR-10 and L1-sparse from 0.794 to 0.900.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RUM^F (refinement only, same algorithm applied sequentially to memorization-homogenized subsets) improves every compared baseline over its vanilla counterpart: Fine-tune [36] gains +0.088 ToW on CIFAR-10 (0.849→0.937) and +0.050 on CIFAR-100; L1-sparse [26] gains +0.106 on CIFAR-10 (0.794→0.900) and +0.059 on CIFAR-100; NegGrad+ [25] gains +0.077 on CIFAR-10 (0.802→0.879) and +0.060 on CIFAR-100; SalUn [10] gains +0.156 on CIFAR-10 (0.731→0.887) and +0.069 on CIFAR-100. Notably, random-shuffle sequential application (a control) actually degrades performance relative to vanilla for most algorithms, confirming that the gain comes from homogenization rather than simply splitting the forget set. Full RUM with per-subset algorithm selection (do-nothing → Fine-tune → SalUn on CIFAR-10; NegGrad+ RUM^F on CIFAR-100) achieves the best overall ToW of 0.965 and 0.921 respectively, with MIA gaps of 0.034 and 0.059, approaching the retrain-from-scratch ideal of 1.000/0.000. NegGrad [15] and Influence Unlearning [21] are evaluated in the difficulty-analysis tables (Table 6) but not in the main RUM comparison; both show the same trend of degrading with higher entanglement and higher memorization, consistent with the other baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the machine unlearning literature (Golatkar et al., Kurmanji et al., Liu et al., Fan et al., Izzo et al.) and the memorization/atypical-examples literature (Feldman 2020, Toneva et al. 2018, Carlini et al. 2019) to identify two interpretable factors—embedding-space entanglement and memorization level—that govern unlearning difficulty. It departs from the closest prior work (Fan et al. 2024, which constructs adversarial worst-case forget sets via bi-level optimization) by instead isolating interpretable, measurable characteristics and studying how they differentially affect existing algorithms. The RUM meta-algorithm is novel in combining a refinement step (partitioning by memorization) with per-subset algorithm selection, repurposing the observation that different algorithms excel on different forget set profiles into a practical pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with actionable guidance: which unlearning algorithm to use depends on the memorization profile of the forget set, and for low-memorization subsets, doing nothing may be optimal. The C-proxy (Spearman correlation −0.80 to −0.91 with memorization at <0.02% extra compute) makes the refinement step practically deployable. Open questions include the interaction between entanglement and memorization (shown to be distinct but not yet jointly optimized), privacy implications of sequential unlearning (the 'privacy onion effect'), and extension to LLMs where memorization is defined differently. The scope is limited to image classification with CNNs, and the sequential-only meta-algorithm leaves parallel or adaptive compositions for future work.
