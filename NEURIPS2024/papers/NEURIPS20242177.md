## Problem

Deep GNNs suffer from over-smoothing, where node representations become indistinguishable as layers increase. Existing residual methods (ResGCN, APPNP, GCNII, DenseGCN, JKNet) alleviate this by combining multi-order neighborhood subgraph aggregations, but they use fixed, node-agnostic coefficients and still lose information in high-order subgraphs. The paper identifies these two limitations—lack of node adaptivity and severe high-order information loss—as the key barriers preventing residual methods from scaling to very deep networks.

## Input / Output

The PSNR module takes the graph adjacency matrix, the initial node feature matrix H, and the current layer index k as inputs to a lightweight graph encoder (e.g., a single GraphSAGE layer). It outputs a per-node, per-layer Gaussian posterior (mean μ and variance σ²) from which a scalar residual coefficient η ∈ (0,1) is sampled via the reparameterization trick. The final output at each layer is a node representation matrix H_k that blends the initial features H_1 with the backbone GNN's current-layer output H'_k using these node-specific coefficients. The same encoder is shared across all layers, with a learnable positional embedding of the layer index to differentiate the posterior distributions.

## Method

PSNR formulates node-level residual coefficients as latent variables and estimates their posterior distribution P(η_k | A, H_k, k) using a graph encoder that processes the residual signal (H_1 − H'_k) plus a layer positional embedding. The reparameterization trick (η = μ + ζ·σ, ζ ~ N(0,1)) makes the sampling differentiable, enabling end-to-end training. The residual update is H_k = H_1 + φ(diag(η_{k−1}))(H_1 − H'_k), where φ is a sigmoid constraining coefficients to (0,1), making the aggregation fine-grained and node-adaptive. Unlike DropEdge or GRAND, PSNR introduces stochasticity during both training and testing, which the authors show theoretically (Proposition 1) slows the convergence of cumulative product terms to a rank-one matrix compared to the fixed N^k in vanilla GCN. The module is model-agnostic, adds O(n²d) time and O(n) space overhead, and is validated on ten datasets including large-scale Ogbn-arxiv.

## Results

On the fully observed node classification benchmark (Table 3), PSNR-GCN achieves the best average rank (1.00) across six datasets, with headline accuracies of 81.01% on Cora, 91.51% on Amazon-Photo, 71.51% on Chameleon, and 54.95% on Squirrel. PSNR-GAT similarly leads with 60.85% on Squirrel (+12.92% over vanilla GAT) and 72.24% on Chameleon. In the missing-feature setting (Table 4), PSNR-GCN boosts Pubmed accuracy from 36.4% (vanilla GCN) to 77.0% (+40.6%), and PSNR-GAT from 38.5% to 77.3% (+38.8%). On the large Ogbn-arxiv graph, PSNR-GCN reaches 67.81% accuracy with only 2539 MB memory, outperforming GCN (64.37%) and ResGCN (66.32%) while using far less memory than DenseGCN (3131 MB) and JKNet (2921 MB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PSNR-GCN outperforms all GCN-based baselines on 5 out of 6 datasets, with the largest margins over DenseGCN (e.g., +6.07% on Cora, +5.38% on Squirrel) and GCNII (e.g., +3.61% on Cora, +8.82% on Squirrel). Against ResGCN, PSNR-GCN leads on every dataset, most notably on Squirrel (+2.52%) and Chameleon (+0.81%). PSNR-GAT similarly dominates all GAT variants, achieving +4.87% over Res-GAT on Squirrel and +2.35% over Init-Res-GAT on Chameleon. In the missing-feature setting, PSNR surpasses all normalization-based methods (BatchNorm, PairNorm, DGN, DeCorr) and drop-based methods (DropEdge, DropMessage) on all three citation datasets, with the most dramatic gain being +40.6% over vanilla GCN on Pubmed. On large graphs, PSNR-GCN achieves the best accuracy on all three datasets (Coauthor-Physics, Flickr, Ogbn-arxiv) while consuming less memory than JKNet and DenseGCN, though it uses slightly more memory than vanilla GCN and ResGCN.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the residual-connection family of over-smoothing mitigations (ResGCN, APPNP, GCNII, DenseGCN, JKNet) but departs from them by replacing fixed or globally-shared residual coefficients with node-level coefficients sampled from a learned posterior distribution. The conceptual lineage draws from variational inference (reparameterization trick) and from the spectral convergence analysis of GAT (Wu et al., 2023), repurposing the insight that row-stochastic matrices with heterogeneous entries converge slower than the uniform GCN operator. The neighborhood-subgraph-overlap reinterpretation of over-smoothing provides a unifying lens under which all prior residual methods are recast as different strategies for combining multi-order subgraph aggregations, exposing their shared limitation of lacking node adaptivity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PSNR offers a lightweight, model-agnostic plug-in module that consistently improves deep GNN performance across backbones and datasets, with particularly large gains in the missing-feature setting where deep propagation is essential. The node-adaptive residual mechanism addresses a genuine gap: prior methods assume uniform importance of neighborhood orders across all nodes, which is clearly suboptimal for heterogeneous graphs. However, the paper acknowledges that training truly deep GNNs (beyond 64 layers) remains challenging, and the randomness introduced during inference (unlike standard dropout-style regularization) is an unconventional design choice whose long-term stability and interaction with other regularization techniques remain unexplored. Open questions include whether the posterior-sampling framework extends naturally to dynamic or temporal graphs, and whether the layer-embedding mechanism generalizes to architectures without a fixed depth.
