## Problem

Diffusion models require large training sets, but real-world target domains often have only limited samples due to collection costs or risks. Existing transfer approaches either finetune the pre-trained model (risking overfitting and memorization) or add heuristic regularization, and in both cases the pre-trained model serves only as an initialization point rather than a reusable prior. The paper asks whether the pre-trained source-domain diffusion model can be leveraged as a plug-and-play component without any parameter modification.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen pre-trained diffusion model on the source domain, a small set of labeled target-domain samples (n ≪ m), and optionally a label y for conditional generation. It produces synthetic samples matching the target marginal q_X or paired (x, y) samples matching the joint q_XY. In the ECG experiments, inputs are 12-lead recordings at 100 Hz (10-second clips from PTB-XL as source, 6–60-second clips from ICBEB2018 as target), and the guidance network is a lightweight 4-layer MLP (~2.8M parameters) compared to the full 50.2M-parameter diffusion backbone.

## Method

The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) shows that the optimal target-domain score decomposes as the source-domain score plus a guidance term ∇_{x_t} log E_{p(x_0|x_t)}[q(x_0)/p(x_0)], where q/p is the density ratio between target and source distributions. Practically, the density ratio is estimated by training a binary domain classifier via logistic loss, and the expectation is learned by a guidance network h_ψ trained to regress the classifier-derived ratio over source-domain samples. The pre-trained diffusion model is never finetuned; generation simply adds ∇log h_ψ(x_t, t) to the frozen source score at each denoising step. Two additional regularizations—cycle regularization (exploiting the equivalent form E_{q(x_0|x_t)}[q_t/p_t] to use limited target data) and consistency regularization (enforcing that the combined score matches target-domain denoising score matching)—improve calibration without altering the optimal solution. A conditional extension (Theorem 3.3) replaces the marginal density ratio with the joint ratio q(x_0,y)/p(x_0,y), enabling paired (x, y) generation and downstream classification.

## Results

On the ECG downstream task (Table 3), TGDP achieves the best AUC of 0.953, F_β=2 of 0.773, and G_β=2 of 0.534, outperforming all baselines. On generative quality (Table 2), TGDP attains the lowest FID (10.46) and highest diversity (0.53) while training only 2.8M parameters in 30 minutes, compared to 50.2M parameters for the full-model baselines. In the Gaussian mixture simulation (Table 1), TGDP achieves the highest average likelihood under the target distribution (0.417 at n=10, 0.627 at n=100, 0.673 at n=1000), substantially exceeding both Vanilla Diffusion and Finetune Diffusion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TGDP is compared against four baselines: (1) Vanilla Classifier (train from scratch on limited target data), (2) Finetune Classifier (finetune a source-domain classifier on target data), (3) Vanilla Diffusion (train a diffusion model directly on limited target data), and (4) Finetune Generator (finetune the pre-trained source-domain diffusion model on target data). TGDP wins on all three downstream metrics (AUC, F_β, G_β), achieving 0.953 AUC vs. 0.941 for both Finetune Classifier and Finetune Generator, and 0.932 for Vanilla Diffusion. On generative quality, TGDP achieves the best FID (10.46 vs. 11.01 for Vanilla Diffusion and 12.26 for Finetune Generator) and highest diversity (0.53 vs. 0.37 and 0.47), while requiring only 2.8M trainable parameters and 30 minutes of training versus 50.2M parameters and 40–60 minutes for the full-model baselines. In the Gaussian mixture simulation, TGDP's advantage is most pronounced at the smallest sample size (n=10), where it achieves 0.417 average likelihood versus 0.290 for Finetune and 0.145 for Vanilla Diffusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

TGDP departs from the dominant finetuning paradigm for diffusion model transfer (adapters [25,44], regularization-augmented finetuning [52]) by reframing the problem: rather than adjusting pretrained weights, it decomposes the target-domain score into the frozen source-domain score plus a guidance term derived from a density ratio estimator (Theorem 3.1). This connects to the importance-weighted denoising score matching framework [40] and the SDE-based diffusion formulation [36], but the key novelty is showing that the guidance is exactly the log-gradient of an expectation of the density ratio, making it optimal rather than heuristic. Remark 3.4 explicitly distinguishes this from vanilla classifier guidance [8,3,6], which does not recover the true target score.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, parameter-efficient alternative to finetuning (2.8M vs. 50.2M trainable parameters on ECG) with a clean theoretical guarantee that the resulting score matches the oracle target-domain score. However, validation is limited to 2D Gaussian mixtures and a single biomedical ECG benchmark; no image or text-to-image generation experiments are provided, which limits confidence in scalability. Open questions include whether the density ratio estimator remains accurate under large domain shifts, how the method extends to heterogeneous label spaces (sketched in Appendix B.2 but not empirically tested), and whether the approach transfers to high-dimensional vision-language tasks the authors acknowledge as future work.
