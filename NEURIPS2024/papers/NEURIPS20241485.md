## Problem

Existing theoretical bounds for programmatic weak supervision fail to account for two empirically critical phenomena: pseudolabel correction (the student outperforms the noisy teacher) and coverage expansion (the student generalizes to data points where the teacher abstains). Prior work either treats weak labels as exogenous noise or focuses on combining labeling functions, neither of which captures the setting where a strong pretrained model is trained on the deterministic outputs of a weaker model.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes as input a pseudolabeled dataset partitioned into correctly-labeled (S_i^good), incorrectly-labeled (S_i^bad), and uncovered (T_i) subsets, a student hypothesis class F, and a symmetric neighborhood function N over the input space. It produces upper bounds on the true classification error err(f, y|X) in terms of the weak-label error err(f, y~|S), expansion parameters (c, q), and robustness parameters. In the empirical validation, the input is IMDb movie reviews with rule-based pseudolabels (based on 'incredible'/'horrible' counts), the student is a linear probe on SentenceBERT embeddings, and the neighborhood oracle uses a pretrained paraphrase model to generate neighbors for expansion estimation.

## Method

The core contribution is an error bound based on expansion properties of the data distribution: if 'bad' points (incorrect or missing pseudolabels) have many 'good' neighbors, and the student is robust on those neighborhoods, then a mistake on a bad point forces mistakes on good points, linking the training objective (weak-label error) to the desired objective (true error). The key technical innovation is replacing the standard expansion assumption with robust expansion (from the graph conductance literature), which allows the student to be merely 'robust on average' rather than adversarially robust at every point—matching empirical observations that adversarial training is not required for weak-to-strong generalization. The paper further provides a statistical procedure for checking expansion from finite data using a neighborhood oracle, with a generalization bound controlled by the VC dimension of the hypothesis class. Proposition 4.1 shows the expansion conditions inherently rule out hypothesis classes rich enough to exactly fit the weak teacher, capturing the tradeoff between model capacity and generalization.

## Results

On the IMDb sentiment classification task using a linear probe on SentenceBERT embeddings, the paper validates its theoretical bounds empirically. For label 0, the measured expansion is 0.848, the teacher error α_i is 0.11, and the student's true error err(f,y|S_0) is 0.04—below the teacher error, confirming pseudolabel correction. The theoretical bound (0.05) is also below α_i, correctly predicting this correction. For label 1, the expansion is lower (0.497), the bound (0.37) exceeds α_i (0.33), and indeed no pseudolabel correction occurs (student error 0.35 > teacher error 0.33). For coverage expansion, the worst-case student error on uncovered points T_1 is 0.29, well below the bound of 0.33, with measured expansion c=0.75 to S_i^good and c=0.55 to S_i^bad.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so comparisons are against existing theoretical bounds rather than empirical baselines. The paper shows that the bound from Fu et al. [21] (Theorem 3) gives err(f̃,y) ≤ P(S)·4α(1−α) + P(T), which cannot capture pseudolabel correction (4α(1−α) > α for α < 3/4) and charges every uncovered point as an error, failing to account for coverage expansion. The bound from Wei et al. [69] (Theorem 4.3) requires coverage P(x∈S) ≥ 2/3 to apply, ruling out the low-coverage regime where weak-to-strong generalization is empirically observed. The paper's Theorem 4.2 exactly recovers the co-training bounds of Blum and Mitchell [6] as a special case (c=1, q=0, P(R̄(f)|T_i)=0) while removing the multi-view and conditional independence assumptions. Unlike all prior expansion-based results [3, 69, 12], the paper's bounds hold for average-case-robust classifiers rather than requiring adversarial robustness, and it provides the first statistical procedure for checking expansion from finite data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and generalizes expansion-based analysis from co-training (Blum & Mitchell, Balcan et al.), self-training (Wei et al.), and domain adaptation (Cai et al.) into a single framework for programmatic weak supervision, while departing from prior work in two key ways: (1) it only requires average-case robustness rather than adversarial robustness, by connecting to the robust expansion literature (Kannan et al., Kwok et al.), and (2) it makes the student hypothesis class a first-class object in the bounds via data-dependent expansion parameters, rather than treating weak supervision as a noisy-label problem. It provides the first theoretical grounding for the empirical weak-to-strong generalization phenomenon documented by Burns et al. and the broader programmatic weak supervision literature (Ratner et al., Fu et al.), which had no error bounds accounting for pseudolabel correction or coverage expansion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first set of error bounds for programmatic weak supervision that provably allow the student to outperform the teacher on both covered and uncovered data, giving a principled explanation for why weak-to-strong generalization occurs: the strong model cannot fit the weak teacher's mistakes without incurring additional error on correctly-labeled neighbors. The finite-sample procedure for checking expansion (though heuristic) is a step toward making these assumptions empirically verifiable. Key limitations include the heuristic nature of the worst-expanding-set search, the narrow experimental scope (single dataset, single representation), and the absence of new training algorithms. Open questions include whether expansion can be checked efficiently, whether contrastive pretraining objectives can be informed by the pseudolabeler structure, and whether the framework extends to reward modeling and other non-classification settings.
