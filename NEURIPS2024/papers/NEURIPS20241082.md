## Problem

Existing customized video generation methods face a fundamental conflict: fine-tuning a T2V model on a few subject images degrades its inherent motion generation capability, producing static 'moving images,' while methods that learn motion from video data require separate fine-tuning for each motion type. The paper argues that the base T2V model already contains diverse motion priors, and the real challenge is preserving video generation quality during subject learning while enabling flexible, training-free motion control at inference.

## Input / Output

For fine-tuning, the method requires only 3–5 images of the target subject plus 500 randomly sampled preservation videos from Panda-70M, completing in ~10 minutes on a single A100 80G GPU. At inference, the user provides a text prompt, a per-frame bounding box sequence specifying the subject's desired position (top-left and bottom-right x-y coordinates), and a 2D camera movement ratio vector [c_x, c_y]. The output is a short video (576×320×24 frames for Zeroscope, 512×320×16 for LaVie) generated via 50-step DDIM denoising with classifier-free guidance scale 7.5.

## Method

During training, MotionBooth fine-tunes a T2V diffusion model with three complementary losses: a subject region loss that computes the diffusion reconstruction loss only within the SAM-segmented subject mask to prevent background overfitting, a video preservation loss that jointly trains on common captioned videos to maintain the model's video generation capability, and a Subject Token Cross-Attention (STCA) loss that applies binary cross-entropy to bind the special [V] token's cross-attention map to the subject's spatial position. At inference, subject motion is controlled training-free by editing cross-attention maps: the [V] and [class name] token attention values are amplified within the user-specified bounding boxes (scaled inversely to box area) and set to −∞ outside, with amplification applied only in early denoising steps (t ≥ τ) while suppression persists throughout. Camera motion is controlled by a latent shift module that progressively translates the noised latent over a timestamp window [σ₁, σ₂], filling the exposed gap by sampling tokens horizontally and vertically from the original latent (excluding the subject region) to preserve semantic coherence along the shift axis.

## Results

On the primary motion-aware customized video generation task (Table 1), MotionBooth achieves the best scores across nearly all metrics on both Zeroscope (R-CLIP 0.667, R-DINO 0.306, T-Cons. 0.958, Flow error 0.252) and LaVie (R-CLIP 0.712, R-DINO 0.472, T-Cons. 0.962, Flow error 0.332) base models. For camera movement control (Table 2), the training-free latent shift module achieves FVD of 905.40 (Zeroscope) and 723.26 (LaVie), substantially outperforming training-based methods like CameraCtrl (FVD 1468.53) and MotionCtrl (FVD 1109.45), while also reducing flow error from 0.807 to 0.190 (Zeroscope). The method requires only ~10 minutes of fine-tuning on a single A100 GPU and adds only 0.3 seconds to inference for subject motion control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MotionBooth outperforms all three customized video generation baselines (DreamBooth, CustomVideo, DreamVideo) on both Zeroscope and LaVie, with the largest margins on R-DINO (subject fidelity in specified positions) and Flow error (motion accuracy). DreamVideo [57] comes closest on Flow error (0.349 vs 0.252 on Zeroscope) due to its video-based motion learning, but still trails by 0.097 and significantly underperforms on R-DINO (0.238 vs 0.306). CustomVideo [55] shows the weakest subject positioning (R-DINO 0.267/0.189), attributed to its parameter-efficient approach of only tuning text embeddings and cross-attention layers. For camera control, MotionBooth's training-free latent shift module beats CameraCtrl [17] by 0.617 in flow error and 0.015 in CLIP-T (Zeroscope), and MotionCtrl [56] by 0.682 in flow error, while also achieving the lowest FVD (905.40 vs 1468.53 and 1109.45). The only near-tie is CLIP-T on LaVie where CustomVideo scores 0.248 vs MotionBooth's 0.247, a negligible 0.001 difference. In the extended comparison (Table 4b), MotionBooth also surpasses TrailBlazer [34] and Directed Diffusion [35] on R-CLIP and R-DINO for subject motion control, while Motion-Zero [4] collapses entirely under the customized setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

MotionBooth extends DreamBooth's fine-tuning paradigm from image to video generation, but addresses the critical failure mode where image-only fine-tuning destroys the base T2V model's motion prior. For subject motion control, it adapts cross-attention map manipulation techniques from Direct-A-Video and Dense Text-to-Image Generation, adding a novel STCA loss to explicitly bind subject tokens to spatial positions during training. For camera control, it departs fundamentally from training-based approaches (CameraCtrl, AnimateDiff, MotionCtrl) by proposing a training-free latent shift module that directly translates the noised latent, requiring no additional parameters or large-scale motion datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MotionBooth is the first framework to unify customized subject learning, subject motion control, and camera motion control in a single pipeline, making it practically useful for content creators who need precise control over both what appears and how it moves. The training-free inference for both motion types (adding only ~0.3s for subject control and ~5s for camera control) makes the approach broadly applicable to any T2V model without retraining. Key limitations include struggles with multi-object scenes and implausible subject motions, and the approach has only been validated on relatively short clips (16-24 frames) with two base models. Open questions include whether the latent shift generalizes to longer videos, more complex camera trajectories, and whether the STCA loss scales to multiple simultaneous subjects.
