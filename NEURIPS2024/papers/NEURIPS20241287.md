## Problem

Most mechanistic interpretability research identifies circuits at a single snapshot (end of pre-training), yet deployed LLMs undergo continuous training and finetuning. Prior work on mechanisms over time has been limited to encoder-only or toy models, leaving open whether circuit analyses generalize across training duration and model scale in the decoder-only LLMs that are actually deployed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241287/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 154 intermediate checkpoints per Pythia model (70M–2.8B parameters, spanning 300B tokens) and evaluates them on four tasks using paired clean and counterfactual/corrupted inputs. Task metrics differ by task: IOI and Gendered-Pronoun use logit difference (single clean vs. single corrupted answer), while Greater-Than and SVA use probability difference over multiple valid/invalid completions. Outputs include per-checkpoint circuit subgraphs (attention heads and MLPs as nodes), component-type functional scores, path-patching-based algorithmic stability ratios, and exponentially-weighted Jaccard similarity of circuit node sets over training.

## Method

Circuits are found at every checkpoint using EAP-IG (edge attribution patching with integrated gradients), which scores all edges in a fixed number of forward/backward passes independent of model size; the minimal circuit is selected via binary search for the smallest edge set achieving ≥80% of full-model task performance. Component emergence is tracked by scoring each in-circuit head against task-specific functional metrics (copy score for name-movers, CSPA for copy suppression, prefix-matching for induction, succession score for successor heads) and summing across heads per checkpoint. Algorithmic stability of the IOI circuit is verified via iterative path patching: the three-step algorithm (induction/duplicate-token heads → S-inhibition heads → name-mover/copy-suppression heads) is quantified at each checkpoint by measuring the fraction of downstream effect attributable to each step, confirming the algorithm persists even as individual heads swap in and out. Circuit-level stability is measured with an exponentially-weighted (α=0.5) Jaccard similarity between each checkpoint's circuit node set and all prior checkpoints, while circuit size is tracked as node count across training.

## Results

Across Pythia models (70M–2.8B parameters) over 300B training tokens, task abilities and their supporting functional components (induction heads, name-mover heads, copy suppression heads, successor heads) emerge at remarkably similar token counts regardless of model scale—induction heads appear after ~2×10⁹ tokens, name-mover heads between 2–8×10⁹ tokens. The IOI circuit's algorithmic structure remains stable post-formation: path-patching metrics for copy suppression and name-mover heads stay above 70% of total effect, and S-inhibition head contributions remain above 50%, with no dramatic shifts corresponding to individual head turnover. Circuit size positively correlates with model scale (Pearson r = 0.9 for Greater-Than, 0.72 for IOI and SVA, 0.6 for Gendered-Pronoun), and larger models exhibit higher EWMA-Jaccard similarity of circuit node sets over training, indicating greater constituent stability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an observational empirical study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no direct baseline methods being benchmarked against. Instead, the paper validates and extends prior circuit analyses: the IOI circuit algorithm identified at the end of training in Pythia-160m matches the structure originally characterized by Wang et al. [71] in GPT-2 small, and induction head emergence timing (~2B tokens) replicates Olsson et al. [54]. The key cross-scale comparison shows that circuit algorithms generalize from 160M to 2.8B parameters with high fidelity (all algorithm metrics remain above 50%), though small differences exist (e.g., duplicate-token heads appear in larger models' IOI circuits but not in Pythia-160m's). Cross-training comparisons reveal that while individual attention heads swap in and out of circuits (e.g., name-mover head (4,6) loses its function at 3×10¹⁰ tokens in Pythia-160m), the overall algorithm and task performance remain stable, suggesting that circuit analyses at a single checkpoint generalize to earlier and later training phases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the circuit-analysis paradigm from a single training snapshot to a longitudinal view across 300B tokens and model scales from 70M to 2.8B parameters, using EAP-IG for circuit discovery and path patching for algorithmic verification. It builds directly on Wang et al.'s IOI circuit and Olsson et al.'s induction-head emergence work, but departs from prior 'over time' mechanistic studies (which used encoder-only or toy models trained on ≤50M tokens) by studying decoder-only LLMs at realistic training duration and scale. The paper also connects to the broader behavioral-interpretability literature on learning curves across scales, but provides the first mechanistic (circuit-level) evidence that task algorithms are stable across both training and scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: interpretability researchers can gain confidence that circuit analyses performed on small models at the end of pre-training will remain informative for larger models and for models undergoing continued training or fine-tuning, reducing the computational cost of mechanistic studies. Key limitations include the restriction to four simple tasks solvable by small models, a single model family (Pythia), and component-level analysis limited to attention heads and MLPs rather than SAE latents. Open questions include whether algorithmic stability holds for more complex tasks with greater algorithmic diversity, across different architectures, and whether the observed component-level 'load-balancing' mechanism is a general property of transformer training.
