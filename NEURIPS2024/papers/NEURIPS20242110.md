## Problem

In the Gold-Angluin model of language learning, an adversary enumerates strings from an unknown language K drawn from a countable collection of candidates, and it is provably impossible to identify K in the limit for general collections. The paper asks whether the weaker task of *generating* unseen strings from K (rather than naming K) is also impossible, and shows it is not: generation in the limit is always achievable, revealing a fundamental asymmetry between identification and generation.

## Input / Output

At each step t the algorithm receives a finite set S_t of strings enumerated adversarially from the hidden target language K, and has access only to membership queries of the form 'w ∈ L_i?' for each candidate language L_i in the countable collection C. It must output a single string a_t that belongs to K − S_t; in the prompted variant it additionally receives a robust prompt p_t and must output a_t with p_t as a prefix. The adversary may repeat strings and choose enumeration order arbitrarily, and the algorithm is never told whether its output is correct.

## Method

The core mechanism is the notion of a *critical language*: at step t, L_n is critical if it is consistent with S_t and is a subset of every earlier-indexed consistent language. The true language L_z eventually becomes critical and remains so, and all critical languages with index ≥ z are subsets of L_z, making them safe sources for generation. The algorithm avoids explicit subset queries by working with finite prefixes L_i[m] and incrementing a counter m until it finds a string u_m in the highest-indexed (t,m)-critical language; termination is guaranteed because only finitely many 'disruptive' iterations (where the critical language changes) can occur. For finite collections, a simpler closure-based argument applies: after seeing t(C) distinct strings, the intersection of all consistent languages must be infinite, so the algorithm can enumerate that intersection directly.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical results. Theorem (2.1) proves that for any countable collection of candidate languages C, there exists an algorithm that generates from the true language K in the limit — i.e., after some finite step t*, every output string belongs to K minus the set of strings seen so far. Theorem (2.2) strengthens this for finite collections: there exists a uniform bound t(C) such that after seeing t(C) distinct strings from K, the algorithm can produce an infinite sequence of distinct unseen strings from K. Theorem (7.1) extends the result to prompted generation, showing that with robust prompts (prompts that can be completed in every candidate language), the algorithm still achieves generation in the limit. These are existence results with no quantitative performance metrics, as the paper is entirely theoretical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other generation methods experimentally, as it is a purely theoretical work. The central contrast is with the Gold-Angluin identification-in-the-limit framework: Gold (1967) and Angluin (1980) proved that language identification from positive examples is impossible in general (even for regular languages), while this paper shows that language generation from the same adversarial positive examples is always possible for any countable collection of candidate languages. This contrast is the paper's core contribution — it demonstrates that generation is a fundamentally more tractable task than identification under identical information-theoretic constraints. The paper also contrasts its approach with distributional/probabilistic models of LLMs, arguing that worst-case generation guarantees hold without any distributional assumptions, though it acknowledges that practical LLMs do exploit empirical regularities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is built directly on the Gold-Angluin model of language identification in the limit (Gold 1967; Angluin 1980), which established that identifying an unknown language from positive examples is impossible in general, even for regular languages. The key departure is a reformulation of the task: rather than asking the algorithm to name the correct language from a countable candidate list, the paper asks it to produce new strings from the true language, and proves this generation task is solvable for every countable collection. The paper also draws on Angluin's characterization of identifiable families to establish a stronger uniform-bound result for finite collections, and connects its validity/breadth tension to the LLM literature on hallucination and mode collapse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result — that generation in the limit is always possible while identification is not — provides a clean formal argument that the tractability of LLM text generation does not fundamentally require distributional assumptions, which reframes how one thinks about why LLMs work. The paper's analysis of the algorithm's behavior (early over-broad generation analogous to hallucination, later over-narrow generation analogous to mode collapse) offers a worst-case theoretical lens on the validity-breadth tradeoff that is central to practical LLM deployment. Open questions include whether the validity-breadth tension is inherent to any generation-in-the-limit solution, how the results extend to richer prompt structures beyond robust prompts, and whether the formalism can be made to say anything quantitative about the rate at which the algorithm's provisional languages converge.
