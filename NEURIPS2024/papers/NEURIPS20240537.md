## Problem

Binary choices in preference-based bandits provide limited information about preference strength, particularly for strong preferences where choices become nearly deterministic and carry little signal. Existing response-time models from psychology (e.g., hierarchical Bayesian DDM fitting) are computationally intractable for real-time interactive systems and do not aggregate data across multiple option pairs to exploit linear utility structure. This paper addresses the gap by deriving a computationally efficient estimator that combines choices and response times within a linear bandit framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240537/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes, for each pairwise query x = z₁ − z₂, a binary choice c_x ∈ {+1, −1} and a scalar response time t_RT,x (from which the decision time t_x is recovered by subtracting a known non-decision time t_nondec), along with arm feature vectors z ∈ R^d. It outputs an estimated preference vector θ̂ (specifically θ*/a for the proposed estimator, or 2aθ* for the choice-only baseline) and, under the GSE algorithm, a single best-arm recommendation ẑ. A key practical detail is that the estimator is robust to unknown t_nondec: using raw response times instead of decision times yields nearly identical performance.

## Method

The core insight is the identity E[c_x]/E[t_x] = u_x/a derived from the difference-based EZ-diffusion model (dEZDM), which transforms utility estimation from a logistic regression problem into an ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression that aggregates data across all sampled queries. The choice-decision-time estimator θ̂_CH,DT computes empirical means of choices and decision times per query, takes their ratio, and applies OLS to recover θ*/a. Theoretically, the choice-only estimator's information weight a²V[c_x] decays to zero as preferences strengthen, while the decision-time weight E[t_x] remains informative, so response times complement choices precisely where choices are most uninformative. This estimator is integrated into the Generalized Successive Elimination (GSE) algorithm for fixed-budget best-arm identification, where random response times require a stochastic sampling procedure instead of deterministic query pre-allocation. Empirically, the proposed estimator consistently outperforms both the choice-only logistic MLE and prior response-time estimators from Wagenmakers et al. and Xiang Chiong et al. across three real-world datasets.

## Results

The paper evaluates six GSE variations on three real-world datasets (Food-risk, Snack from Clithero 2018, Snack from Krajbich et al. 2010) using best-arm identification error probability P[ẑ ≠ z*] as the metric, reported via violin plots across two budget levels. The proposed method (λ_trans, θ̂_CH,DT) consistently achieves the lowest median error probability across all three datasets, outperforming the choice-only baseline (λ_trans, θ̂_CH) and the weak-preference design (λ_weak, θ̂_CH). Synthetic experiments (Figure 3) further show that the choice-only estimator's error probability degrades as preference strength increases (larger c_Z), while the choice-decision-time estimator maintains robust performance, confirming that response times complement choices for strong-preference queries. The method also performs similarly when the non-decision time is unknown (θ̂_CH,RT), indicating robustness to this assumption.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed (λ_trans, θ̂_CH,DT) consistently outperforms the choice-only GSE (λ_trans, θ̂_CH) from Azizi et al. [3] and Jun et al. [31] across all three real-world datasets, demonstrating that incorporating decision times reduces identification error. It also outperforms the weak-preference design (λ_weak, θ̂_CH) from Jun et al. [31], which theoretically targets informative queries but fails in practice because θ* is unknown and weak-preference queries consume more time budget. Compared to the logit-based estimator from Wagenmakers et al. [67] (θ̂_CH,logit) and the combined choice-response-time estimator from Xiang Chiong et al. [73] (θ̂_CH,DT,logit), the proposed OLS-based estimator (eq. 3) performs more consistently across datasets, suggesting that the simple ratio E[c_x]/E[t_x] is a more effective aggregation than the logit-transformed or square-root-based alternatives. The ablation comparing θ̂_CH,DT with θ̂_CH,RT (using raw response times instead of decision times) shows minimal performance difference, indicating the method is robust to the assumption of known non-decision time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: the drift-diffusion model (DDM) tradition in cognitive psychology, which models both choices and response times but typically estimates parameters for individual option pairs using computationally intensive methods (hierarchical Bayesian inference, MLE), and the preference-based linear bandit literature, which scales to large option spaces via linear utility structure but relies solely on binary choices. The key methodological departure is exploiting the dEZDM identity E[c_x]/E[t_x] = u_x/a to transform utility estimation from a logistic regression problem (choice-only) into a simple OLS linear regression problem that aggregates data across all sampled queries. The paper is explicitly positioned as the first to integrate response times into bandit or RL settings, building on the GSE algorithm of Azizi et al. [3] and the transductive/weak-preference designs of Jun et al. [31].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that response times are unobtrusive and cost nothing to collect, yet they provide complementary information about preference strength that binary choices lack—especially when preferences are strong and choices become nearly deterministic. This could meaningfully accelerate preference learning in recommender systems, assistive robots, and LLM fine-tuning where interaction budgets are limited. Key limitations include reliance on reliable response time data (challenging in crowdsourcing), the assumption of known non-decision time (though empirically shown to be robust), and the restriction to the dEZDM with linear utilities. Open questions include whether the insight generalizes to other bounded accumulation models (race models, attentional DDM), whether non-decision time can be estimated jointly, and whether the approach extends to multi-attribute or multi-alternative settings.
