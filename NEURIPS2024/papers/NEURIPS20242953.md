## Problem

Designing cell-type-specific promoters for closely related cell types (e.g., three hematopoietic leukemia lines) is difficult because they share similar regulatory environments, and existing MBO-based promoter design methods rely on large, expensive PE datasets and do not account for adversarial designs or principled candidate selection. This paper introduces a data-efficient MBO framework that addresses these gaps through conservative regularization, transfer learning, and a diversity-aware selection scheme.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242953/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes one-hot encoded 250 bp DNA sequences (length constrained by pooled oligonucleotide synthesis) along with 17,104 experimentally measured promoter-driven expression (PE) values across three leukemia cell lines (Jurkat, K562, THP1), and large MPRA datasets (Sharpr-MPRA, SuRE MPRA) for pretraining. It produces 4,000 candidate promoter sequences per target cell type, each scored by a pessimistic ensemble estimate of differential expression (DE), defined as the mean PE difference between the target cell and the other two cell types. A final greedy selection step picks a diverse subset balancing predicted DE and sequence diversity (Hamming distance + 6-mer frequency composition).

## Method

The core architecture is MTLucifer (convolutional layers followed by transformer layers with RoPE embeddings) with multi-task learning heads predicting PE in all three cell types simultaneously. The key training innovation is a modified conservative objective model (COM) loss: during fine-tuning, a regularizer penalizes the model's predicted DE on adversarially generated sequences (produced by 100 steps of gradient ascent in the softmax-parameterized one-hot simplex) relative to the training set, applied independently per cell type. Five design models are trained with different conservatism coefficients α ∈ {0, 0.0003, 0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03}, each used to generate ~17K candidate sequences via gradient ascent from every starting sequence. A 36-model ensemble (varying MLP depth, width, and activation in the fine-tuning head) provides uncertainty estimates, and a greedy selection algorithm picks the final 4,000 sequences by maximizing the sum of pessimistic DE (mean minus std of ensemble predictions) plus a diversity term combining normalized Hamming distance and 6-mer frequency Euclidean distance.

## Results

The proposed COM-based MBO workflow designs 4,000 cell-type-specific promoters (250 bp) per cell line for Jurkat, K562, and THP1 leukemia cells. For K562, the best designed promoter achieves 75.85% higher differential expression (DE = 5.17) than the best sequence in the fine-tuning dataset (DE = 2.94). Overall, designed sequences improve upon their starting sequences in 72.12% of cases for Jurkat and 80.48% for K562, but fail to improve most starting sequences for THP1 due to limited high-DE training data. Designed sequences are highly diverse, with mean base pair entropy of 1.87–1.96 (max 2) and mean pairwise Hamming distance of 176–184 bp (max 250).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the motif tiling baseline (inspired by Nissim et al. [5]), the proposed COM-based workflow wins decisively: motif tiling 'almost always leads to worse sequences' than the starting sequences it modifies, while the MBO approach improves 72–80% of starting sequences for Jurkat and K562. Against DENs (Linder et al. [7]) used as an alternative optimizer, gradient ascent produces sequences with higher experimentally measured DE (Figure 3, Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test), and is also easier to implement and tune. On diversity, the two methods are comparable for Jurkat and K562 (entropy ~1.87–1.89), but DENs produce significantly less diverse designs for THP1 (entropy 1.00 vs. 1.96; Hamming distance 108 vs. 184), likely because the sparse high-DE training data for THP1 makes it difficult for a generative model to discover the design model's modes. The paper does not report a single unified DE performance table across all three baselines; the key quantitative comparison is the 75.85% DE improvement over the best fine-tuning dataset sequence for K562, which neither baseline achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Conservative Objective Models (COMs) framework of Trabucco et al. [13] from generic offline MBO to the multi-task promoter design setting, modifying the conservative regularizer to penalize overestimated differential expression (DE) rather than raw expression. It builds on the transfer learning pipeline of Reddy et al. [12] (pretrain MTLucifer on large MPRA datasets, then fine-tune on small target-cell PE data) as the backbone for constructing differentiable design models. The key departure from prior MBO-based promoter design work (Gosai et al. [11], Linder et al. [7]) is the explicit focus on closely related cell types in a data-constrained regime, combined with a principled sequence selection step that balances diversity, optimality, and ensemble-based uncertainty—practical considerations that previous methods overlooked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that COM-based offline MBO can discover cell-type-specific promoters for closely related hematopoietic cell lines using only ~17K training measurements, a setting where prior methods relying on large MPRA datasets or markedly different cell types have not been tested. The 75.85% DE improvement over the best training sequence in K562 and the consistent outperformance of gradient ascent over generative baselines (DENs) provide practical evidence for iterative design-validation loops. However, the approach failed for THP1 due to poor training data quality, highlighting that model accuracy remains the bottleneck and that multi-round optimization (which the framework is designed to support) has not yet been demonstrated. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes beyond hematopoietic cells, how many rounds of iteration are needed to converge, and whether conservative regularization's potential to suppress diversity becomes problematic with limited-diversity training sets.
