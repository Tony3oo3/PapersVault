## Problem

The fundamental limit of how sparsely a deep network can be pruned without performance degradation has remained uncharacterized. Prior work (Paul et al., 2022) provided only a lower bound via Gordon's Escape Theorem that poorly matched experiments and offered no upper bound, leaving the sharp phase transition of pruning ratio unknown.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242897/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained network's weight vector w*, the Hessian matrix of the empirical loss at w* (estimated via an improved Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature algorithm that explicitly accounts for zero-valued and vanishingly small eigenvalues), and a loss tolerance ε computed as the standard deviation of batch losses on training data. It outputs a sharp pruning ratio threshold ρ (where the lower and upper bounds nearly coincide, with gaps under 0.3% in experiments) and a pruned sparse network produced by l1-regularized one-shot magnitude pruning (LOMP). The Hessian is typically non-positive definite in practice, requiring a convexification step before Gaussian width computation.

## Method

The paper formulates pruning feasibility as a set intersection problem between the loss sublevel set (a high-dimensional ellipsoid defined by the Hessian) and a k-sparse subspace, then applies the Approximate Kinematics Formula from convex geometry to obtain both necessary and sufficient conditions for intersection, yielding sharp lower and upper bounds on the pruning ratio. The Gaussian width of the projected ellipsoid is computed via concentration of measure, with the key formula w² ≈ Σᵢ rᵢ²/(‖w*−wᵏ‖² + rᵢ²), where rᵢ = √(2ε/λᵢ). Two key factors emerge: smaller weight magnitude of pruned parameters and flatter loss landscape (smaller Tr(H)) both permit higher sparsity. For the achievable scheme, the authors train with l1 regularization (convex relaxation of l0) followed by a single magnitude-based pruning step, which drives the upper bound to nearly coincide with the lower bound because l1 training produces a 'zero-dominating' weight distribution. An improved SLQ spectrum estimation algorithm handles the large-scale, non-positive Hessian by sampling row l1-norms to count missing near-zero eigenvalues and adding them as 1e-30 to the estimated spectrum.

## Results

The theoretical pruning ratio limit (derived via the Approximate Kinematics Formula) matches experimental values with differences (Δ) of only 0.01%–0.54% across eight tasks (Table 2), confirming the sharpness of the characterization. The proposed LOMP (l₁-regularized one-shot magnitude pruning) achieves the best test accuracy at the theoretical pruning limit across all models: e.g., 55.42% on ResNet18/TinyImageNet vs. 45.43% (Rare Gems) and 45.12% (IMP), and 97.61% on MLPNet/MNIST at 90% sparsity vs. 95.55% (CHITA) and 94.30% (EWR). The gap between the theoretical upper and lower bounds of the pruning ratio is ≤0.27% in all experiments (Table 1), validating that the limit is essentially tight.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LOMP consistently outperforms all three pruning baselines (Rare Gems, IMP, and Smart-Ratio random pruning) across every dataset–model combination in Table 10, with the largest margins on TinyImageNet (e.g., +10.0% over RG and +10.3% over SR on ResNet18). Against optimization-based pruning methods (Table 11), LOMP achieves 97.43–97.61% accuracy on MLPNet/MNIST at 50–90% sparsity, surpassing CHITA (95.55–95.97%) and EWR (94.30–95.24%) by 2–3 points, and maintaining an advantage even at 95–98% sparsity where other methods degrade sharply. The paper also validates its theoretical pruning limit against the actual performance cliff: the predicted threshold (Table 2) agrees with the empirically observed accuracy drop within 0.01–0.54 percentage points, a tighter match than the lower-bound-only prediction in [25] which the authors note can have 'quite big gap.' Notably, LOMP's one-shot nature (no iterative retraining) still beats the multi-iteration IMP and the initialization-based Rare Gems, supporting the paper's claim that magnitude pruning guided by the theoretical limit is near-optimal in the pruning-after-training regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the convex geometry framework of Amelunxen et al. [1] (Approximate Kinematics Formula) and the degrees-of-freedom analysis of Larsen et al. [19], applying them to the pruning problem for the first time in a complete form. The most direct predecessor is Paul et al. [25], which used Gordon's Escape Theorem to derive only a lower bound on pruning ratio with significant inaccuracy due to spectrum estimation errors; this paper replaces that tool with the Approximate Kinematics Formula, which yields both necessary and sufficient conditions, and improves the SLQ spectrum estimation [39] to handle the many zero-valued eigenvalues typical of trained Hessian matrices. The key conceptual departure is framing pruning feasibility as a set-intersection problem between a loss sublevel set and a sparse subspace, enabling a sharp phase-transition characterization rather than a one-sided bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a tight, two-sided fundamental limit on how sparsely a trained network can be pruned, identifying weight magnitude and loss-landscape sharpness as the two governing factors. The practical consequence is that a simple l1-regularized one-shot magnitude pruning (LOMP) provably achieves this limit and empirically outperforms more complex iterative methods (Rare Gems, IMP) across eight tasks, suggesting that much of the complexity in existing pruning algorithms may be unnecessary. Key open questions include whether the limit extends to multi-shot/iterative pruning regimes, whether l1-regularized training is truly optimal for minimizing the sub-network size, and whether the framework applies to structured (filter-level) pruning rather than unstructured weight pruning.
