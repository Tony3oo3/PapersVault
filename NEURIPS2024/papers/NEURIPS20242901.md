## Problem

Existing data curation pipelines for vision-language models disproportionately favor English image-text pairs, discarding non-English samples that could enrich training data with culturally salient concepts and diverse visual representations. This paper asks whether deliberately including more (translated) multilingual data in the training set can improve performance on standard English-centric vision tasks, rather than only benefiting non-English benchmarks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242901/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The training pipeline consumes 128M raw image-text pairs from DataComp's Common Crawl pool, where captions are in 200+ languages. All captions are translated to English via NLLB before filtering, so the model is trained exclusively on English text paired with images of diverse cultural origins. At inference, the trained CLIP model (ViT-B/32 image encoder) takes an RGB image and a text prompt, producing zero-shot classification scores or image-text similarity scores for retrieval.

## Method

The core procedure translates all 128M raw captions to English using the NLLB-600M model, then re-filters the pool using cosine similarity between image embeddings and translated-text embeddings from the Data Filtering Network (DFN). This re-filtering is critical: it changes the data ranking so that (translated) non-English samples, previously dominated out by English-biased filtering, now constitute the majority of the selected subset. The paper trains CLIP with ViT-B/32 under DataComp's fixed compute budget (128M steps) and shows that combining both raw-caption-filtered and translated-caption-filtered subsets (union or concatenation) yields the best performance, outperforming English-only or raw-caption-only baselines by 2–4% on ImageNet and 1.5–2.1 points across 38 DataComp tasks. Ablations confirm the gains are not merely from language unification (replacing raw captions with translations without re-filtering hurts performance) and are robust to the choice of filtering network (DFN vs. OpenAI CLIP).

## Results

On the DataComp benchmark (128M training steps), training on filtered translated multilingual captions achieves 32.9% ImageNet accuracy, 27.5% ImageNet distribution-shift accuracy, 29.6% retrieval score, 70.9% GeoDE worst-region accuracy, and 35.9% average across 38 tasks, outperforming the standard filtered raw-caption baseline (31.6%, 26.0%, 28.2%, 68.8%, 35.0% respectively). Combining both raw and translated captions (51.2M samples) yields the best overall performance: 33.6% ImageNet, 28.0% ImageNet shifts, 30.1% retrieval, 72.5% GeoDE, and 36.1% average. When training for 10× longer (1.28B steps), the gap between the combined approach and raw-only widens to +4.2% on ImageNet and +2.1 percentage points on average across 38 tasks. On GeoDE specifically, translated multilingual captions improve accuracy across all six regions, with the largest gain in Africa (+5.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five data-curation strategies, all using the same DFN filtering network and CLIP ViT-B/32 architecture. The proposed approach (filtered translated multilingual captions) beats the standard filtered raw-caption baseline by +1.3% on ImageNet, +1.5% on ImageNet shifts, +1.4% on retrieval, and +2.1% on GeoDE worst-region accuracy at the same 25.6M dataset size. It also substantially outperforms the English-only baseline (16.0M) by +4.6% on ImageNet and +3.2% on average across 38 tasks, demonstrating that restricting to English data is strictly worse. The best-performing configuration—combining both raw and translated captions (51.2M samples)—surpasses the raw-only baseline by +2.0% on ImageNet and +1.1 percentage points on average across 38 tasks, and this advantage grows to +4.2% and +2.1pp respectively when training is extended to 1.28B steps. Notably, simply replacing raw captions with their translations without re-filtering (the 'replaced' baseline) actually degrades performance, confirming that the benefit comes from re-ranking the data pool after translation rather than from the language medium alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CLIP contrastive pre-training paradigm and the DataComp benchmark's data curation framework, but departs from prior work that treats multilingual data as relevant only for non-English tasks. While LAION-Translated and CLIP-filtering bias studies (Hong et al., DataComp) have documented that English data dominates filtered corpora, no prior work systematically tested whether re-including non-English samples (via translation and re-filtering) improves performance on English-centric benchmarks. The paper also extends Ye et al.'s finding that multilingual captions increase semantic coverage from synthetic captions on 3.6K images to raw web-crawled data at 128M scale, and uses a more advanced translation model (NLLB) on less-filtered raw data compared to LAION-Translated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that translated multilingual data improves English-centric vision tasks (ImageNet, retrieval, distribution shifts) challenges the prevailing assumption that English-dominated curation is optimal, suggesting data diversity should be a deliberate design axis rather than a byproduct. Open questions include whether benefits persist with stronger filtering methods (HypeSampler, T-MARS), whether translation artifacts cap the achievable gains, and whether text-encoder adaptation alone suffices for multilingual benchmarks given the enriched image diversity. The practical implication is straightforward: existing pipelines that discard non-English samples are leaving performance on the table even for English tasks.
