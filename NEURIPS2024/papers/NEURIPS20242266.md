## Problem

Existing RL-via-Sequence-Modeling (RvS) algorithms like Decision Transformer and Trajectory Transformer suffer from inference-time suboptimality: even when the learned sequence model accurately encodes transition dynamics and reward structure, it fails to sample high-return actions during evaluation. The paper identifies the root cause as a tractability gap—sampling from the expected-value-conditioned distribution p(a_t | s_t, E[V_t] ≥ v) is intractable for autoregressive models, which can only compute next-token probabilities and cannot marginalize over intermediate states or condition on global return constraints. This paper demonstrates that the bottleneck is not model expressiveness but the inability to exactly and efficiently answer the probabilistic queries required at inference time.

## Input / Output

At each decision step, Trifle receives the current state s_t and the past trajectory τ_{<t} (a sequence of state-action-reward-RTG tuples) and outputs a single action a_t to execute in the environment. The offline trajectory dataset is discretized into 100 quantile bins per continuous variable (following TT), and the method jointly maintains two models over this sequential data: an autoregressive Transformer for the prior policy and a Probabilistic Circuit for exact inference. In the multi-step variant, the output extends to an action sequence a_{t:t'} whose expected return is computed by exactly marginalizing over all intermediate states s_{t+1:t'} via the PC.

## Method

Trifle augments any base RvS policy (TT, DT, or TT+Q) with a Probabilistic Circuit (PC) trained on the same offline data using the Hidden Chow-Liu Tree structure and latent variable distillation initialization. The key mechanism is a per-dimension correction term in the autoregressive sampling distribution: p̃(a_t^i | s_t, a_t^{<i}; v) ∝ p_GPT(a_t^i | s_t, a_t^{<i}) · p_TPM(V_t ≥ v | s_t, a_t^{≤i}), where the PC computes the conditional probability of exceeding a return threshold given only partially specified actions—something the autoregressive model alone cannot do. This is followed by beam search over H steps, where candidate action sequences are scored by their exactly computed expected return (via PC marginalization over intermediate states in the multi-step case) and the top-N sequences are retained. The paper also proves that exact sampling from p(a_t | s_t, E[V_t] ≥ v) is NP-hard even under a Naive Bayes assumption, justifying the approximate correction-term approach. For safe RL, the same PC machinery conditions on action-space constraints (e.g., torque limits) during sampling, enabling simultaneous satisfaction of return and safety objectives.

## Results

On the 9 Gym-MuJoCo (D4RL) benchmarks, Trifle achieves the highest average normalized score of 83.1, outperforming all baselines including DD (81.8), IQL (77.0), CQL (77.6), and TD3+BC (75.3). Trifle attains state-of-the-art scores on 7 out of 9 environments, with notable gains such as 113.0 on Hopper Med-Expert (vs. 111.8 for DD) and 97.8 on Hopper Med-Replay (vs. 100.0 for DD, but the closest Trifle variant). In stochastic environments (Taxi, FrozenLake), multi-step Trifle (m-Trifle) achieves an episode return of −57 on Taxi, substantially outperforming TT (−182), DT (−388), and DoC (−146). On action-constrained MuJoCo variants, Trifle outperforms TT by 4–10 points across all three environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Trifle (built on TT) consistently outperforms its base TT policy across all 9 Gym-MuJoCo environments, with average gains of 4.2 points (83.1 vs. 78.9), confirming that the TPM correction term improves inference-time optimality regardless of the base model. Against Decision Transformer (DT), Trifle achieves substantially higher scores in most settings (e.g., 97.8 vs. 82.7 on Hopper Med-Replay), though DT-Trifle is not reported for Hopper environments. Compared to Decision Diffuser (DD), Trifle wins on 5 of 9 benchmarks but loses on Hopper Medium (67.1 vs. 79.3) and Hopper Med-Replay (97.8 vs. 100.0), where DD's diffusion-based generation provides an advantage. Against offline TD methods (IQL, CQL, TD3+BC), Trifle achieves the best average score (83.1 vs. 77.0, 77.6, 75.3 respectively) and wins on 6 of 9 individual benchmarks. In stochastic environments, m-Trifle dramatically outperforms TT (−57 vs. −182 on Taxi), DT (−57 vs. −388), and DoC (−57 vs. −146), with the gap widening as dataset quality degrades (FrozenLake ε=0.7: m-Trifle 0.37 vs. TT 0.12, DT 0.10). On action-constrained MuJoCo variants, Trifle outperforms TT by 4.1 (HalfCheetah), 9.6 (Hopper), and 1.5 (Walker2d) points, demonstrating the benefit of exact conditional computation under constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: RL-via-Sequence-Modeling (RvS) methods like Decision Transformer and Trajectory Transformer, which frame offline RL as conditional sequence generation, and Tractable Probabilistic Models (TPMs), particularly Probabilistic Circuits, which support exact and efficient computation of marginal and conditional probabilities. Prior RvS work has focused almost exclusively on improving training-time expressiveness (better sequence models, diffusion-based generation, Q-value corrections), while this paper identifies and addresses a complementary axis: inference-time tractability. It is the first work to deploy PCs (specifically HCLT-structured PCs initialized via latent variable distillation) as the inference engine within an RvS pipeline, using them to compute per-dimension correction terms that bias autoregressive action sampling toward high expected returns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central diagnostic — that RvS methods can accurately *recognize* high-return actions yet fail to *sample* them due to intractable conditional generation — reframes a persistent source of underperformance in offline RL and motivates a new design axis (inference-awareness) alongside the well-studied expressiveness axis. The practical limitations are acknowledged: Trifle depends on the TPM being well-calibrated, and current PC implementations are slower than neural-network inference, which constrains deployment to moderate-dimensional action spaces. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional continuous control (e.g., humanoids), whether more expressive or better-trained PCs can close the remaining gap to oracle policies, and whether the tractability benefits extend to online or partially-observed settings.
