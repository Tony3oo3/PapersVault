## Problem

Existing LLM watermarks are robust to removal attacks but this very robustness enables a new class of spoofing attacks: attackers can subtly alter the semantics of watermarked text (e.g., flipping neutral content to harmful) while the watermark signal persists, causing wrongful attribution to the model owner. No prior scheme simultaneously achieves robustness, unforgeability, tamper-evidence, and transparent detection, as these properties are in fundamental tension—robustness requires insensitivity to perturbations while anti-spoofing requires sensitivity to them.

## Input / Output

During generation, the method takes a text prompt, a secret key sk (held by the model owner), and a pre-generated random key sequence ξ of dimension K (vocabulary size). The first m tokens of the output form a 'message' that is hashed and signed with sk; the remaining tokens carry the resulting signature bits. During detection, only the public key pk, the hash function h, and ξ are needed—no access to sk or the generating model is required. The detector outputs one of five provenance cases: (1) valid signature, no suspicion; (2) valid signature but local alignment anomaly indicating signature-preservation attack; (3) valid signature, normal alignment (genuine but safety-mechanism failure); (4) invalid signature but small global alignment p-value (tampered model output); (5) invalid signature and large p-value (foreign text).

## Method

Bileve embeds a bi-level signature via a novel rank-based sampling strategy. At the coarse-grained level, a random key sequence ξ is embedded by ranking candidate tokens using a Weighted Rank Addition (WRA) score, WRA_k = R(P_k) + γ·R(ξ_k), which blends the model's probability ranking with the watermark key's ranking, preserving generation quality while injecting a detectable statistical signal. At the fine-grained level, the first m tokens form a message whose hash is signed with sk to produce a bit string; each subsequent token is selected as the first candidate (in WRA order) whose hash matches the corresponding signature bit, making the signature content-dependent and unforgeable. A shift-generate mechanism cycles through n pre-generated ξ sequences to ensure diversity across generations. Detection combines public-key signature verification (integrity check) with a statistical alignment test (coarse-grained provenance), and the consistency or inconsistency between these two levels yields the five-case provenance classification, distinguishing genuine output, tampered output, signature-preservation attacks, and foreign text.

## Results

On both OpenGen and LFQA with OPT-1.3B, Bileve achieves perfect detection (TPR=1.000, FPR=0.000, F1=1.000) under no editing, matching SLS and slightly outperforming Unigram (FPR=0.010, F1=0.995). Under 10% token editing, Bileve maintains F1=0.999 and FPR=0.000 on both datasets, while SLS completely fails (TPR=0.000, F1 inapplicable) and Unigram degrades to F1=0.991 (OpenGen) and 0.994 (LFQA). Against the semantic manipulation attack, Bileve produces a tampering p-value of 0.01 confirming the text originated from the target LLM but was altered, whereas Unigram's z-score remains above the detection threshold (10.5 vs. threshold 6.0), making the spoofing undetectable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Bileve is compared against two baselines: Unigram (Zhao et al., ICLR 2024), a robust statistical watermark, and SLS (Fairoze et al., 2023), a cryptographic single-level signature scheme. Under no editing, Bileve ties SLS with perfect F1=1.000 and FPR=0.000, while both outperform Unigram (FPR=0.010). The critical differentiator emerges under 10% token editing: Bileve retains F1=0.999 with zero false positives, whereas SLS collapses entirely (TPR drops to 0.000, making F1 inapplicable) due to its fragility to any perturbation, and Unigram degrades modestly to F1=0.991–0.994. Against the semantic manipulation spoofing attack, Unigram is the most vulnerable—its z-score remains above the detection threshold (10.5 vs. 6.0) after token replacements shift content from neutral to offensive, meaning the detector cannot distinguish genuine output from tampered content. SLS detects the tampering via signature verification failure but cannot confirm the text originated from the target LLM. Bileve uniquely provides both: the failed signature check flags tampering, while the coarse-grained statistical test (p-value=0.01) confirms provenance, enabling it to differentiate all five detection scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

Bileve synthesizes two previously separate lines of LLM watermarking research: the statistical-signal approach (Kuditipudi et al. [13], Zhao et al. [23]) that provides robustness to perturbations, and the cryptographic-signature approach (Fairoze et al. [6]) that provides asymmetric, unforgeable detection. The key departure is identifying a fundamental tension between these goals—robustness to removal attacks conflicts with sensitivity to tampering—and resolving it through a bi-level design where a coarse-grained rank-based statistical signal handles robustness while a fine-grained digital signature handles integrity. The paper also introduces a new spoofing attack (semantic manipulation) that exploits watermark robustness itself, extending the threat model beyond prior attacks that relied on key symmetry [6,15] or watermark learnability [8,10].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5-case provenance classification (genuine, signature-preserved tampering, safety-mechanism failure, tampered model output, non-model output) is a practically important advance over binary watermark detection, offering model owners concrete evidence to distinguish jailbreaking from spoofing. However, the experimental scope is limited to OPT-1.3B and LLaMA-7B on two datasets, and the semantic manipulation attack is demonstrated primarily as a qualitative proof-of-concept rather than a thorough quantitative evaluation. Open questions include how the scheme scales to larger models, the practical overhead of the rank-based sampling with shift-generate in production settings, and whether the perplexity penalty (23% higher than SLS, higher than Unigram) becomes unacceptable for quality-sensitive applications.
