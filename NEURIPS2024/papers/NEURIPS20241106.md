## Problem

Existing drag-based editing methods treat the task as a purely positional 'how to drag' problem, constraining feature correlation between source and target points while ignoring the inherently ill-posed nature of the task—where a single pair of drag points can correspond to multiple valid semantic intentions (e.g., 'elongate the neck' vs. 'raise the head' vs. 'bring objects closer'). Additionally, these methods prioritize editing accuracy at the expense of overall image quality, producing artifacts and distributional mismatches with the input.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a user-drawn binary mask specifying the editable region, and a pair of drag points (a handle point and a target point) as input. It produces one or more edited images along with the inferred source and target prompts that define the semantic editing direction. The pipeline is training-free with respect to the diffusion backbone (Stable Diffusion v2.1-base, 50 DDIM steps, classifier-free guidance scale 5); the only trained component is a quality discriminator fine-tuned on 20,000 images (10K high-fidelity, 10K low-fidelity) generated via DragonDiffusion with aesthetic-score filtering.

## Method

LucidDrag decomposes drag editing into a 'what-then-how' paradigm. The Intention Reasoner first uses an LVLM (Osprey) to localize the drag points and produce a textual description of the object of interest, then an LLM (GPT-3.5) to reason N candidate semantic intentions, each yielding a source prompt, target prompt, and confidence score; the top-n intentions are sampled for diverse outputs. The Collaborative Guidance Sampling stage then performs DDIM inversion with the source prompt (recording intermediate noise and self-attention keys/values in a memory bank) and generates the edited image with the target prompt, injecting three simultaneous guidance signals: (1) semantic guidance from the asymmetric source/target prompt pair, which establishes a directional editing bias; (2) quality guidance from a score-based classifier using a discriminator built on the U-Net's down and middle blocks, whose gradient is added to the noise prediction via Eq. 5; and (3) editing guidance following DragonDiffusion's feature-correspondence energy over the drag and preservation masks. The three energy terms are combined additively and applied during the last n₁ denoising steps (quality guidance only in the last n₂ < n₁ steps), while the memory bank enforces content consistency in unrelated regions.

## Results

On the DragBench benchmark for content dragging, LucidDrag achieves a Mean Distance of 20.46±0.77 (lower is better), substantially outperforming the next-best method DiffEditor (26.01±0.81) by over 5 pixels. For image quality, LucidDrag attains a GScore of 7.37, surpassing FreeDrag (7.15), DragDiffusion (6.94), DiffEditor (6.42), and DragonDiffusion (6.26). On the object moving task, LucidDrag also leads with a CLIP-score of 0.260 and an LMM-score of 7.25, compared to DragonDiffusion (0.255 / 6.25) and DiffEditor (0.257 / 6.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LucidDrag outperforms all four baselines on both dragging accuracy and image quality. In terms of Mean Distance (dragging precision), LucidDrag (20.46) beats DiffEditor (26.01) by 5.55 pixels, DragonDiffusion (29.16) by 8.7 pixels, FreeDrag (30.40) by 9.94 pixels, and DragDiffusion (32.60) by 12.14 pixels. On GScore (image fidelity), LucidDrag (7.37) leads FreeDrag (7.15) by 0.22, DragDiffusion (6.94) by 0.43, DiffEditor (6.42) by 0.95, and DragonDiffusion (6.26) by 1.11. The gap is most pronounced against DragonDiffusion and DiffEditor, which, while achieving reasonable point tracking, introduce noticeable artifacts that degrade image quality. On the secondary object moving task, LucidDrag also wins on both CLIP-score (0.260 vs. 0.257 for DiffEditor) and LMM-score (7.25 vs. 6.5 for DiffEditor), with the LMM gap being particularly large, indicating substantially better overall perceptual quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DragonDiffusion's feature-correspondence editing guidance and MasaCtrl's self-attention key/value replacement for content consistency, but departs from the entire drag-based editing line (DragDiffusion, FreeDrag, DragonDiffusion, DiffEditor) by reframing the problem from a deterministic 'how to drag' to a 'what-then-how' paradigm. The key conceptual departure is treating drag points as an ill-posed signal that admits multiple valid semantic interpretations, and resolving this ambiguity with an LVLM (Osprey) + LLM (GPT-3.5) intention reasoner that produces asymmetric source/target prompts. This positions the work at the intersection of the visual-programming trend (using LLMs to plan image generation) and score-based classifier guidance, which prior drag-editing methods had not combined.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine practical limitation: users who drag a point often have a semantic intent (elongate, rotate, move) that pure positional tracking cannot capture, and the resulting ambiguity leads to suboptimal or single deterministic outputs. The training-free nature of the main pipeline (only a small discriminator requires training) makes the approach easy to adopt on top of existing Stable Diffusion deployments. Open questions include whether the LLM-based intention reasoning generalizes to more complex multi-object scenes, whether the quality discriminator trained on DragonDiffusion outputs transfers to other base models, and whether the reliance on GPT-3.5 as an external API is a practical bottleneck for real-time interactive editing.
