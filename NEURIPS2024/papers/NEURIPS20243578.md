## Problem

Standard model explanation methods like Partial Dependence Plots and SHAP implicitly assume independence between predictors when varying one feature, which can produce qualitatively misleading explanations when predictors are causally related (e.g., showing a decrease where the true causal effect is an increase). No prior method used an explicit causal model to determine how other features should co-vary when one is changed, leaving a gap between the causal questions practitioners naturally ask and what existing plots can answer.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a black-box predictive model (queried but not inspected), an Explanatory Causal Model (ECM) specifying a DAG and structural equations over the predictor variables, an explanatory dataset (which may be the training data or generated by the ECM), and a single feature of interest. It produces a plot with the feature on the horizontal axis and model predictions on the vertical axis, displaying both individual counterfactual curves per observation and their empirical average (the Causal Effect Function). The ECM need not include the outcome variable, and only the subgraph relevant to the feature of interest and its causal descendants is required, allowing partial causal knowledge to suffice.

## Method

CDPs replace the independence assumption in PDPs with an explicit Structural Causal Model: for each grid point of the target feature, the method applies a do-intervention in the ECM, simulates counterfactual values for all features via abduction-action-prediction, feeds these to the black-box model, and plots the resulting predictions. Different intervention specifications yield named CDPs—Total Dependence Plots (TDP) for the full causal effect, Natural Direct Dependence Plots (NDDP) for the direct pathway, and Natural Indirect Dependence Plots (NIDP) for mediated pathways—each corresponding to a distinct causal question. Theorem 2.10 proves that PDP+ICE is exactly equivalent to NDDP, providing the first universally valid causal interpretation of PDPs. The framework is modular: ECMs can be hand-specified from domain knowledge, learned via causal structure discovery algorithms like PC, or varied to produce uncertainty bands showing sensitivity to structural assumptions.

## Results

This paper introduces Causal Dependence Plots (CDPs) as a visualization framework rather than a method with standard quantitative benchmarks. The key empirical finding is qualitative: Total Dependence Plots (TDPs) reveal qualitatively different—and sometimes opposite—trends compared to PDPs, ALE, and SHAP. In the Sachs et al. protein-signaling dataset, the TDP for PKA's effect on predicted p44/42 shows an increasing relationship, while the NDDP (equivalent to PDP+ICE), ALE, and SHAP all show a decrease. In the salary motivating example, the TDP shows a strong increasing dependence of predicted salary on parental income, whereas PDP/ALE/SHAP show only a weakly increasing relationship. Theorem 2.10 formally establishes that PDP + ICE = NDDP, providing the first universally valid causal interpretation of Partial Dependence Plots.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CDPs (specifically TDPs) against four standard model-agnostic explanation methods: PDP [Friedman, 2001], ICE [Goldstein et al., 2015], ALE [Apley & Zhu, 2020], and SHAP [Lundberg & Lee, 2017]. The comparison is qualitative rather than metric-based, as all methods produce plots rather than scalar scores. The central finding is that TDPs can show qualitatively opposite trends: in the Sachs dataset, TDP shows an increase in predicted p44/42 with PKA while PDP, ALE, and SHAP all show a decrease; in the salary example, TDP shows a strong increasing relationship while PDP/ALE/SHAP show a weakly increasing one. The paper proves (Theorem 2.10) that PDP and ICE are mathematically identical to the Natural Direct Dependence Plot (NDDP), a special case of CDPs, establishing that all four baselines capture only the direct effect while TDPs capture the total causal effect. No baseline 'wins' or 'loses' in a traditional sense; rather, the paper demonstrates that the baselines answer a different (narrower) causal question than TDPs, and that the choice of question materially changes the visual conclusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Partial Dependence Plot (PDP) and ICE curve framework, formally establishing (Theorem 2.10) that PDP+ICE is equivalent to a Natural Direct Dependence Plot under a causal model. It departs from all prior model-agnostic explanation methods (PDP, ICE, ALE, SHAP) by replacing their implicit independence assumption with an explicit Explanatory Causal Model (ECM) that determines how other predictors co-vary when one is intervened upon. The theoretical foundation draws on structural causal models and do-calculus, and the framework generalizes classical mediation analysis quantities (total, natural direct, natural indirect effects) into a unified plotting language. Prior work by Zhao and Hastie and Cox Jr. had noted connections between PDPs and causal effects under specific conditions, but this paper provides the first universally valid causal interpretation and the first method that uses a causal model to govern feature variation during explanation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Total Dependence Plots and PDPs can show qualitatively opposite trends (e.g., increase vs. decrease in the Sachs protein-signaling dataset) is practically consequential: widely-used PDPs may give the wrong directional answer about how a model's prediction responds to an intervention, with implications for scientific hypothesis generation, fairness auditing, and policy decisions. The main limitation is that CDPs require specifying an ECM, which can be a strong assumption; the paper addresses this partially through sensitivity analysis over candidate ECMs and modular integration with causal structure learning (PC algorithm), but uncertainty quantification for CDPs remains an open problem. Future work could extend CDPs to non-tabular data via causal representation learning and develop conformal or Bayesian methods for uncertainty bands on the effect functions.
