## Problem

Existing fairness definitions for image restoration (RDP, PR, CPR) treat a reconstruction as binary correct or incorrect based solely on set membership, ignoring the distribution of images within each group and the severity of errors. This makes them blind to algorithms that, for example, always output a single fixed image for one group while producing diverse outputs for another, yet still satisfy fairness criteria. The paper asks whether a fairness notion can capture how well an algorithm preserves the full distribution of each group's images rather than just a membership indicator.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a degraded image y (noisy, downsampled, or blurred) and a sensitive attribute label a (e.g., age × ethnicity combination) as input, and produces a reconstructed image x̂ along with a per-group Group Perceptual Index (GPI) score computed as a statistical distance (KID, FID, or Wasserstein) between the group's ground-truth distribution p_{X|A}(·|a) and its reconstruction distribution p_{X̂|A}(·|a). In experiments, 512×512 face images from CelebA-HQ are translated into four synthetic fairness groups (old/young × Asian/non-Asian) via Stable Diffusion XL, then degraded with average-pooling downsampling (×4 to ×32) and/or additive Gaussian noise (σ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.25}). GPI features are extracted from the last pooling layer of a FairFace age-and-ethnicity classifier rather than generic image features.

## Method

The paper defines the Group Perceptual Index (GPI) as a distributional divergence (e.g., total variation, Wasserstein, KID) between each group's ground-truth image distribution and its reconstruction distribution, and defines Perceptual Fairness (PF) as the parity of GPIs across all groups. Theoretically, it proves that perfect PF_{TV} implies common lower bounds on Group Precision and Group Recall (generalizing RDP), that perfect GPI for all groups simultaneously is infeasible when degraded measurements of different groups overlap more than their ground truths, and that PF and the overall Perceptual Index are often in tension—improving one group's GPI under perfect PI can only improve others'. Empirically, the paper evaluates 11 state-of-the-art face super-resolution methods (diffusion-based, GAN-based, and transformer-based) across four demographic groups, showing that PF detects an ethnicity bias in the old&Asian group that RDP completely misses, and that adversarial perturbations targeting one group's facial features are caught by GPI but remain invisible to RDP.

## Results

The paper evaluates 11 state-of-the-art face super-resolution algorithms across scale factors s∈{4,8,16,32} and noise levels σ_N∈{0,0.1,0.25} using four fairness groups (young/old × Asian/non-Asian). The headline finding is that the young&non-Asian group consistently receives the best treatment (lowest GPI_KID, highest GP) across all algorithms, while the old&Asian group is systematically disadvantaged relative to old&non-Asian in GPI_KID despite achieving nearly identical GP scores (i.e., RDP declares them equally treated). This discrepancy between RDP and PF holds uniformly across all 11 evaluated methods and all degradation settings, and is further confirmed by an adversarial attack experiment where PF detects injected age bias that RDP completely misses.

**Summary of key fairness findings across all 11 evaluated algorithms (s=32, σ_N=0; representative of all settings).**

| Fairness Group | GP (RDP metric, higher=better) | GPI_KID (PF metric, lower=better) | RDP Verdict | PF Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Young & non-Asian | Highest (~0.9+) | Lowest | Best treated | Best treated |
| Young & Asian | High | Low | Good | Good |
| Old & non-Asian | ~Same as old&Asian | **Lower** than old&Asian | Equal to old&Asian | **Better** than old&Asian |
| Old & Asian | ~Same as old&non-Asian | **Higher** than old&non-Asian | Equal to old&non-Asian | **Worse** than old&non-Asian |

*Key observation: RDP (via GP) reports old&Asian ≈ old&non-Asian, while PF (via GPI_KID) reveals a clear disadvantage for old&Asian. This pattern is consistent across DDNM+, DDRM, DPS, PiGDM, GFPGAN, VQFR, GPEN, DiffBIR, CodeFormer, RestoreFormer++, and RestoreFormer.*

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 11 evaluated restoration algorithms (DDNM+, DDRM, DPS, PiGDM, GFPGAN, VQFR, GPEN, DiffBIR, CodeFormer, RestoreFormer++, RestoreFormer) exhibit the same qualitative pattern: RDP (measured via GP/classification hit rate) reports roughly equal treatment for old&Asian and old&non-Asian groups, while PF (measured via GPI_KID) consistently reveals a disadvantage for old&Asian. The diffusion-based methods (DDNM+, DDRM, DPS, PiGDM) are evaluated at all scale factors (×4 to ×32), while GAN/transformer-based face restoration methods (GFPGAN, VQFR, GPEN, DiffBIR, CodeFormer, RestoreFormer, RestoreFormer++) are only evaluated at ×4 and ×8 since they produce completely wrong outputs at higher scale factors. The young&non-Asian group receives the best treatment in both GP and GPI_KID across all methods, consistent with known training data biases toward young, white demographics in datasets like FFHQ. In the adversarial attack experiment (Section 4.3), RestoreFormer++ roughly satisfies RDP for Asian vs. non-Asian groups, yet PF successfully detects injected age bias (wrinkles) via elevated GPI_KID for the attacked group, while GP remains unchanged. The paper also shows that GPSNR and GLPIPS fail to detect the same biases that GPI_KID reveals, further motivating the proposed metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Perceptual Index (PI) framework of Blau & Michaeli (CVPR 2018) from a global distributional metric to a group-level one, defining the Group Perceptual Index (GPI) as the statistical distance between a group's ground truth distribution and its reconstruction distribution. It departs from the fairness definitions of Jalal et al. (ICML 2021) — RDP, PR, CPR — which treat reconstructions as binary (in or out of a group's support), by instead measuring how well the full distribution of each group is preserved. Theorem 1 formally establishes PF as a generalization of RDP, showing that perfect PF under total variation distance guarantees common lower bounds on both Group Precision and Group Recall.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental and practically important tension: algorithms achieving perfect overall Perceptual Index (PI) often cannot simultaneously achieve perfect Perceptual Fairness (PF), as shown in Theorem 3, and the counter-intuitive Theorem 4 shows that for perfect-PI algorithms, improving one group's GPI necessarily improves others'. Empirically, PF detects ethnicity bias in face super-resolution that RDP completely misses, and adversarial attacks can inject bias undetectable by RDP but caught by PF. Open questions include the nature of optimal estimators for PF, the severity of the PI–PF tradeoff, and whether PF can achieve obliviousness (fairness under arbitrary group partitioning), which it currently cannot guarantee.
