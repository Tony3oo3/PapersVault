## Problem

Existing emotion recognition datasets focus on single individuals or facial close-ups, ignoring the contextual and social cues that psychology shows are essential for understanding emotional states. FindingEmo is the first dataset to target higher-order social cognition by annotating the overall emotional content of entire scenes depicting multiple people in naturalistic social settings, rather than isolating one person's face.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240161/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of RGB images (scraped from the internet, resized to 800×600 for CNNs or 798×602 for DINOv2) each depicting at least two people in a social context; images are distributed as URLs rather than files due to copyright constraints. The model produces three types of output: a discrete emotion label from Plutchik's Wheel (8 leaves or 24 fine-grained emotions), a continuous Valence score on an integer scale [−3, +3], and a continuous Arousal (labeled 'Intensity') score on an integer scale [0, 6]. Annotations describe the emotional gist of the whole scene, not any single individual.

## Method

The dataset was built in two phases: a custom DuckDuckGo scraper generated random queries combining emotion, people-group, and social-setting keywords to collect over 1 million images, filtered by a trained keep/reject CNN; then 655 Prolific annotators labeled 25,869 public images (single annotation) and 1,525 private images (multi-annotation) across Valence, Arousal, Emo8/Emo24, and auxiliary dimensions. For baselines, the authors apply transfer learning by replacing only the final layer of ImageNet- or Places365-pretrained CNNs (AlexNet, VGG16, ResNet18/50/101, DenseNet161) and ViTs (CLIP ViT-B/32, DINOv2 ViT-B/14), training with a novel UnbalancedCrossEntropyLoss that penalizes misclassifications proportionally to the emotional distance between classes on Plutchik's wheel. Beyond baselines, late-fusion multi-stream models concatenate predictions or penultimate-layer features from Emo8 classifiers, EmoNet, YOLOv3+FER2013 face detectors, and Places365 models through an additional linear layer. Key findings: VGG16 is the strongest CNN, ViTs do not clearly outperform CNNs for Emo8 classification but are superior for Valence/Arousal regression, and facial emotion features yield the largest late-fusion gains.

## Results

On the FindingEmo test set, CLIP (ViT-B/32) achieves the best Emo8 classification performance with a Weighted F1 of 0.431 and Average Precision of 0.340 (using UnbalancedCrossEntropyLoss), substantially outperforming the best CNN (ResNet50/ResNet101 at W.F1 0.335, AP 0.251). For regression, CLIP also leads with Spearman R of 0.344 (MAE 1.260) on Arousal and 0.685 (MAE 1.015) on Valence, compared to the best CNN (ResNet101 at S.R 0.254/0.487). Among CNNs, VGG16 is the clear winner for classification (W.F1 0.311), while ResNet50/101 edge it out on regression. The authors note these are hard problems, with even modern ViT models struggling, and that Arousal prediction is consistently harder than Valence prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP (ViT-B/32) dominates all CNN baselines across all three tasks: it achieves W.F1 0.431 vs. 0.335 for the best CNN (ResNet50/101) on Emo8 classification, and S.R 0.685 vs. 0.487 on Valence regression. Among CNNs, VGG16 is the strongest classifier (W.F1 0.311 with CE, 0.310 with UCE), followed by ResNet50/101 (0.335 with UCE), while DenseNet161 underperforms despite greater depth. ImageNet-pretrained models outperform their Places365-pretrained counterparts in 9 out of 13 cases, suggesting object features are more relevant than scene features for emotion recognition. The novel UnbalancedCrossEntropyLoss improves over standard CrossEntropyLoss in 9 of 13 classification cases, with the largest gain for CLIP (0.346→0.431 W.F1). In the late-fusion experiments (Section 4), adding facial emotion features via YOLOv3+FER2013 (OIToFER) yields the largest gains over baseline—particularly for VGG16—while no combination improves upon CLIP or DINOv2, and the best augmented VGG16 results merely approach (not exceed) CLIP/DINOv2 performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in automated emotion recognition, which has historically focused on single-individual facial expressions (JAFFE, CK+, FER2013) or, more recently, single-person context-aware annotations (EMOTIC, HECO, CAER-S). FindingEmo carves new ground by annotating the emotional content of the entire scene as a holistic unit, explicitly targeting 'higher-order social cognition' rather than any one person's affect. On the method side, it repurposes standard transfer-learning pipelines (ImageNet/Places365-pretrained CNNs, CLIP, DINOv2) and introduces a novel UnbalancedCrossEntropyLoss that penalizes misclassifications proportionally to the distance between emotion leaves on Plutchik's Wheel, alongside late-fusion multi-stream experiments combining facial emotion, scene, and emotion-schema features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset provides the first large-scale benchmark for whole-scene emotion recognition, a task that prior datasets have not addressed, and the baselines demonstrate that even state-of-the-art vision transformers (CLIP, DINOv2) struggle, with best Emo8 weighted F1 around 0.43–0.45 and Arousal/Valence Spearman R around 0.34–0.69. This suggests that current architectures are insufficient for the multi-person, context-dependent reasoning the task demands, pointing toward the need for novel ML approaches. Key limitations include single-annotation public images (reliability concerns), inherited internet biases with no representation analysis, and the inability to distribute actual images (URLs only), which may hinder reproducibility. The cross-cultural analysis, while preliminary, raises open questions about whether Arousal annotations are more culturally variable than Valence or emotion labels.
