## Problem

Neural contextual bandit algorithms, while powerful for complex reward functions, are vulnerable to adversarial reward corruptions that can severely degrade performance. Existing robust bandit methods (linear, kernelized) require restrictive assumptions on the reward mapping, and existing neural bandit works rely on arm separateness assumptions that an adversary can deliberately violate (e.g., by injecting duplicate arm contexts). This paper addresses both gaps: robustness to unknown adversarial corruptions and removal of the arm separateness requirement.

## Input / Output

Each round, the learner receives K candidate arms as d-dimensional context vectors (normalized to unit length) and a pool of previously observed context-reward pairs P_{t-1}. The method outputs a single selected arm and, for each candidate, an arm-specific reward estimate combined with a UCB confidence bonus derived from an arm-specific gradient covariance matrix. The reward mapping h(·) is treated as an arbitrary unknown function (no linearity or RKHS membership assumed), and the corruption level C is not required to be known in advance.

## Method

R-NeuralUCB maintains separate network parameters θ_{i,t-1} for each candidate arm x_{i,t}, trained via a context-aware weighted gradient descent procedure. The key mechanism is an arm weight w_{i,t}^{(τ)} (Eq. 4) that scales each training sample's contribution based on the ratio of the candidate arm's uncertainty (weighted gradient norm) to the training sample's uncertainty, capped at 1 and scaled by a tunable α. For high-uncertainty arms, small weights shrink the data-fitting term in the loss (Eq. 5), causing the L2 regularization to pull parameters toward random initialization θ₀—a conservative strategy that limits the damage from potentially corrupted rewards. For low-uncertainty arms, large weights allow the model to fully exploit the training data. Arm selection uses an arm-specific UCB score (Eq. 6) built on an arm-specific gradient covariance matrix Σ_{i,t-1}. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 5.6) achieves a regret bound of Õ(d̃√T + Cβ⁻¹d̃) without the arm separateness assumption, by constructing the confidence ellipsoid on a non-duplicate arm set and decomposing single-round regret into prediction error, model discrepancy, and arm-selection discrepancy components.

## Results

The paper evaluates cumulative pseudo-regret (in terms of corruption-free rewards) over T rounds on three real datasets under adversarial reward corruptions. On MNIST with corruption level C=2000, R-NeuralUCB achieves a regret of approximately 1989±67 (best hyperparameters: λ=0.001, ν=0.1), outperforming its base algorithm NeuralUCB-WGD (2103±119). Under increased corruption (C=4000 on MNIST, 50% corruption probability on MovieLens/Amazon), the performance gap between R-NeuralUCB and conventional neural baselines (Neural-UCB, Neural-TS) widens, while linear baselines (Lin-UCB, CW-OFUL) are consistently outperformed by all neural methods due to the representation power of neural networks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

R-NeuralUCB is compared against four baselines: two linear methods (Lin-UCB and CW-OFUL) and two conventional neural methods (Neural-UCB and Neural-TS). On all three datasets (MovieLens, Amazon, MNIST), both linear baselines are consistently outperformed by all neural methods, as expected given the non-linear reward mappings. Among neural methods, R-NeuralUCB outperforms both Neural-UCB and Neural-TS under adversarial corruptions, with the advantage growing as corruption intensity increases (e.g., from 20% to 50% corruption probability on MovieLens/Amazon, and from C=2000 to C=4000 on MNIST). R-NeuralUCB also outperforms its own base algorithm NeuralUCB-WGD across all settings, confirming the benefit of incorporating candidate-arm uncertainty information alongside training-sample uncertainty. On the Amazon dataset, the performance gap among algorithms narrows under 50% corruption (regret reaching ~8000), reflecting the increased difficulty of that setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: neural contextual bandits (Neural-UCB [86], Neural-TS [84]) that use over-parameterized networks with NTK-based exploration, and corruption-robust bandits (CW-OFUL [42], RGB-PE [16], Fast-slow GP-UCB [15]) that handle adversarial reward perturbations under linear or kernelized assumptions. R-NeuralUCB departs from both by (i) adopting the weighted GD training strategy from CW-OFUL but extending it to the neural setting with arm-specific parameter sets, and (ii) removing the arm separateness assumption (positive-definite NTK Gram matrix or distinct arm contexts) that is standard in neural bandit theory but fails when an adversary can inject duplicate arms. The base algorithm NeuralUCB-WGD (Appendix E) serves as an intermediate step that applies sample-weighted GD to a single shared network, while R-NeuralUCB further refines this by customizing both the loss function and the UCB confidence ellipsoid per candidate arm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first regret analysis for neural bandits under adversarial reward corruptions, and it does so without requiring the corruption level C to be known in advance or the arm separateness assumption to hold—both of which are practically important given that real adversaries can be unknown and can deliberately create duplicate contexts. The corruption-dependent regret term improves from Õ(d̃^{3/2}) (matching the best kernelized results) to Õ(d̃), and is free of the NTK norm S, which is a meaningful theoretical tightening. A key open question the authors flag is the absence of a matching lower bound for neural bandits with corruption, which they argue would require new tools tied to NTK properties and could constitute a separate research direction. Practically, the arm-specific parameter customization increases per-round computational cost (K separate GD runs), though the warm-start strategy mitigates this; scalability to very large K or deep networks remains untested.
