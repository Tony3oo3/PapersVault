## Problem

Even the strongest proprietary LLMs fail to meaningfully improve their responses over sequential attempts at a hard problem, and prompting-based self-correction methods (e.g., Self-Refine) often degrade performance rather than improve it. The paper asks whether it is possible to fine-tune an LLM so that it genuinely learns a test-time self-improvement strategy—detecting and correcting its own mistakes across multiple turns—rather than merely being shown what the correct answer is.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241753/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each turn, the model receives as input the original prompt, its own full history of prior responses, and an auxiliary instruction (e.g., 'this response is not correct, please introspect and correct your answer' or external compiler feedback). It outputs a single revised text response conditioned on that multi-turn context. At inference, RISE supports two modes: 'with oracle' (early termination upon verified correctness) and 'without oracle' (fixed-length rollout followed by majority voting across all turn outputs), with a sliding window of size k to avoid distribution shift when inference turns exceed training iterations.

## Method

RISE converts a single-turn prompt-response dataset into a multi-turn MDP where the state is the prompt plus history of prior attempts, the action is the model's response, and the reward is a sparse correctness indicator. In each training iteration, the current model is unrolled on-policy to generate multi-turn rollouts; improved responses are then constructed either by best-of-N resampling from the model itself (self-distillation) or by querying a stronger teacher model (distillation). The learner is fine-tuned on these rollouts using an advantage-weighted regression objective that assigns exponential weights to responses based on centered reward values, enabling learning from both successful and unsuccessful attempts. The key design choice, validated by ablations, is the DAgger-style on-policy data collection: the model must see corrections to its own mistakes rather than expert demonstrations, and the multi-turn interaction history (including intermediate errors) is essential for inducing the self-improvement capability.

## Results

On GSM8K, RISE (Llama2-7B + 2 iterations) achieves 55.0% m1@t5 (a +17.7% improvement over its 37.3% m1@t1), while RISE on Mistral-7B reaches 59.2% m1@t5 (+23.9%). On MATH, RISE (Mistral-7B + 1 iteration) improves from 6.7% to 18.4% m1@t5 (+11.1%). With oracle early termination, RISE attains 68.4% p1@t5 on GSM8K (Llama2) and 68.6% (Mistral-7B), substantially exceeding the parallel-sampling baseline (m5@t1) by 17–24 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RISE substantially outperforms Self-Refine [33], which degrades performance across all models without oracle feedback (e.g., GPT-3.5 drops from 66.4% to 61.0% on GSM8K m1@t3; Mistral-7B drops from 33.7% to 21.2%), whereas RISE on Mistral-7B improves by +23.9% over five turns. Against GLoRE [19], which trains a separate refinement model and achieves only +3.4% on GSM8K (m1@t3), RISE gains +17.7% (Llama2) or +23.9% (Mistral-7B) with a single end-to-end model. RISE on Mistral-7B (59.2% m1@t5) surpasses the 7B state-of-the-art Eurus-7B-SFT [63] (47.9% m1@t5) on GSM8K, despite Eurus being fine-tuned on a much larger math-specific corpus. Compared to V-STaR [21], which requires 64 samples per prompt for a +35.2% gain, RISE achieves +44.5% with only 5 sequential samples, and additionally solves problems that remain unsolved even at pass@B for B ≫ 5 in the first turn.

## Relation to Prior Work

RISE repurposes the DAgger framework for on-policy expert querying (Ross et al., 2011) and the reward-weighted regression objective (Peters & Schaal, 2007; Peng et al., 2019) to the novel setting of converting single-turn LLM fine-tuning into a multi-turn MDP. It departs from prompting-based self-correction methods (Self-Refine, GLoRE) by showing that the algorithmic structure of iterative on-policy data collection with a weighted training objective is what induces self-improvement, not merely access to expert supervision. The paper also connects to the diffusion-models-vs-VAEs analogy, arguing that sequential conditional distributions over intermediate responses yield a more flexible marginal than a single-shot prediction, providing a capacity-based explanation for why multi-turn training helps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RISE demonstrates that test-time self-improvement over sequential turns is a learnable capability, directly contradicting the hypothesis from Huang et al. (2023) that even strong proprietary LLMs cannot self-correct reasoning. The finding that standard SFT on identical oracle data fails to induce this capability while RISE's MDP formulation succeeds highlights that the training protocol, not just the data, is the critical ingredient. Open questions include scaling to more iterations (only 2 tested), developing a fully online RL variant, integrating RISE data into general instruction-tuning pipelines, and extending beyond math to broader reasoning and agentic tasks. The weak-to-strong result (Llama2-generated RISE data improving Mistral more than Mistral's own data) suggests surprising transferability properties worth further investigation.
