## Problem

Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) relies on utility functions to aggregate multiple objectives, yet the field has never formally characterised (1) for which utility functions an optimal policy is guaranteed to exist, or (2) which user preference relations can actually be expressed as utility functions. The standard solution concept—the undominated set—implicitly assumes both that every utility function admits an optimal policy and that every preference is representable, but the paper demonstrates with explicit counterexamples that neither assumption holds, even for finite MOMDPs.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input a finite MOMDP (state set, action set, transition function, and a vector of n reward functions) together with a utility function u: R^n → R, and produces purely theoretical characterisations rather than algorithmic outputs. Specifically, it yields sufficient conditions on u (continuity for per-state optimality; decomposability into an affine function composed with a strictly monotonically increasing function for global optimality) and necessary-and-sufficient conditions on a preference relation (completeness, transitivity, and existence of at least one maximal element per state) for a quasi-representative utility function to exist. No training, sampling, or numerical computation is involved; all results are theorems, lemmas, and finite counterexamples.

## Method

The paper introduces two levels of utility optimality—per-state ⟨u,s⟩-optimal and global u-optimal—and proves that continuity of u is sufficient for per-state optimal policy existence by applying the Extreme Value Theorem to the convex coverage set polytope at each state (Theorem 1). For global optimality, it shows that any utility function decomposable as u(x) = h(g(x)) with g affine and h strictly monotonically increasing admits a deterministic stationary u-optimal policy (Theorem 2), extending the known result for linear utilities to a broader non-linear family. On the preference side, the paper formalises preference relations over value functions in MOMDPs, distinguishes representative from quasi-representative utility functions (the latter preserving only maximal elements), and proves that any complete, transitive preference relation with at least one maximal element per state admits a quasi-representative utility function via a constructive assignment (Theorem 3). Three counterexamples (Chebyshev, a strictly monotone C¹ function, and a discontinuous strictly monotone function) establish that monotonicity, strict monotonicity, and continuous differentiability are each individually insufficient to guarantee optimal policy existence.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The main results are three theorems: (1) Theorem 1 proves that continuity of a utility function is sufficient to guarantee a stationary ⟨u,s⟩-optimal policy exists for every state of a finite MOMDP; (2) Theorem 2 proves that utility functions decomposable as h(g(x)) with g affine and h strictly monotonically increasing guarantee a deterministic and stationary u-optimal policy for the entire MOMDP; (3) Theorem 3 proves that any complete, transitive preference relation with at least one maximal element per state admits a quasi-representative utility function. Three counterexamples (Examples 3–5) demonstrate that monotonicity, strict monotonicity, and continuous differentiability are individually insufficient to guarantee optimal policy existence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper contains no empirical baselines or quantitative comparisons, as it is entirely theoretical. In terms of positioning against prior theoretical work, the paper's Theorem 2 extends Skalse and Abate [23], who showed that a u-optimal policy exists if and only if the objective can be represented by a linear utility function; Theorem 2 identifies a strictly larger family (affine composed with strictly monotonically increasing functions) for which optimal policies are guaranteed. The paper's state-level preference definition (Definition 9) provides more granularity than the objective definition in [23] and [26], enabling the identification of contradictory policies that those frameworks cannot detect (illustrated in Examples 4 and 5). The quasi-representative utility function (Definition 12) is a new concept not present in prior MORL literature, bridging the gap between non-representable preference orders (e.g., lexicographic) and computable utility-based solution concepts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard MORL framework (Roijers et al. 2013; Hayes et al. 2022; Roijers & Whiteson 2017) but departs from it by formally questioning two assumptions the community has operated under: that an optimal policy exists for every utility function, and that all user preferences are expressible as utility functions. It extends Skalse & Abate (2023), who showed that for 'objectives' (preorders over policies) a u-optimal policy exists iff the objective is linearly representable, by identifying a strictly broader family (affine composed with strictly monotonically increasing functions) that also guarantees optimal policies. The paper also introduces the concept of quasi-representative utility functions, which preserve maximal elements of preference relations that lack full utility representations (e.g., lexicographic order), a notion absent from prior MORL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes that the undominated set and convex hull — the standard solution concepts in MORL — rest on assumptions that are provably false, with concrete finite counterexamples showing no optimal policy exists even for strictly monotonically increasing, continuously differentiable utility functions. This has direct practical consequences: MORL algorithms that compute the undominated set may return policies that are not actually optimal for the user's true utility function. Open questions include generalising the results to multi-agent multi-objective settings and developing algorithms that exploit the characterised families (continuous utility functions for per-state optimality; affine∘SMI for global optimality) to verify or construct utility-optimal policies.
