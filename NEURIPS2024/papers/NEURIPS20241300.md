## Problem

Multi-parameter persistent homology lacks a succinct, computable representation analogous to the barcode in the one-parameter setting, making it difficult to integrate into machine learning pipelines. Existing vectorization methods for two-parameter persistence rely on weaker invariants (rank-invariant, signed barcodes) that capture only partial topological information and are often computationally expensive. This paper asks whether a complete, interpretable, and efficiently computable summary of two-parameter persistence can be constructed that directly feeds into graph neural networks without an explicit vectorization step.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a bifiltered simplicial complex (e.g., a Delaunay bifiltration from point clouds scored by local density, or a Heat Kernel Signature–Ricci Curvature bifiltration from graphs) and produces a layered graph in R³: vertices are bars [b,d) tagged with a slice index i, and edges connect bars between consecutive slices according to the inclusion-induced homology map. The graphcode is not a topological invariant—it depends on the chosen barcode bases—but for fixed bases it permits complete reconstruction of the persistence module. The layered graph is then passed to a GNN that outputs a class label, with vertex attributes including birth, death, slice index, and optionally additive/multiplicative persistence values.

## Method

Graphcodes are constructed by taking one-parameter slices of a bifiltration (fixing one parameter), computing a barcode basis for each slice, and defining a bipartite graph between consecutive slices whose edges encode how each basis cycle in slice i decomposes into the basis of slice i+1. The key algorithmic insight is that all vertices and edges can be obtained from a single out-of-order matrix reduction of the boundary matrix—sorting (p+1)-simplices by their horizontal filtration value but adding them in vertical order—yielding O(n³) worst-case time with near-linear practical performance. For learning, the graphcode is fed into a pipeline of graph attention (GAT) layers followed by per-slice local max-pooling, concatenation across slices, and a standard feed-forward classifier; the per-slice pooling is critical because it preserves the positional information of each layer. The connecting edges between slices allow the GNN to propagate importance across adjacent layers, which the experiments show improves accuracy over the edge-free variant (which reduces to a multi-slice PersLay). The paper further demonstrates that random basis changes during training (providing 20 different graphcode realizations per instance) can further boost performance beyond the standard sparse reduction.

## Results

On the synthetic shape dataset (5 classes of point clouds with varying numbers of annuli), graphcodes (GC) achieve 86.9±1.4% test accuracy, outperforming the next-best method GRIL (74.9±2.7%) by over 12 percentage points, while also being the fastest to compute (95 s vs. 333,187 s for GRIL and 3,519 s for MP-L). On the orbit classification benchmark, GC reaches 92.3±0.3% on Orbit100k, surpassing PersLay (89.2±0.3%) and all kernel-based baselines. On the random point-process dataset, GC achieves 83.4±2.5% versus 70.7±4.9% for MP-HSM-C. On the TUDatasets graph classification benchmark, GC is competitive (e.g., 73.6% on PROTEINS, 86.4% on MUTAG) but does not consistently outperform all vectorization-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the shape dataset, graphcodes decisively outperform all 11 baselines: GC (86.9%) beats GRIL (74.9%), MP-I (64.1%), MP-HSM-C (57.0%), P-I (43.6%), and MP-L (37.2%), with the edge-connected graphcode improving over the edge-free variant GC-NE (82.8%) by ~4 points. On the random point-process dataset, GC (83.4%) again leads all methods, with a 12.7-point margin over MP-HSM-C (70.7%), though the edge contribution is smaller here (GC-NE reaches 83.1%). On the orbit benchmarks, GC surpasses PersLay [11] by 1.1 points on Orbit5k (88.5% vs. 87.7%) and 3.1 points on Orbit100k (92.3% vs. 89.2%), and outperforms all kernel methods (PSS-K, PWG-K, SW-K, PF-K). On the TUDatasets graph classification task, GC is competitive but not dominant: it trails MP-HSM-C on PROTEINS (73.6% vs. 74.6%) and DHFR (76.4% vs. 81.9%), while matching or exceeding other baselines on COX2 and MUTAG. The authors note that graphcodes capture more topological information at the cost of invariance, which may explain the mixed results on datasets with weaker topological signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

Graphcodes generalize PersLay [11], a deep-set architecture for one-parameter persistence diagrams, to the two-parameter setting by constructing a layered bipartite graph from consecutive one-parameter slices of a bifiltration. Unlike prior multi-parameter vectorization methods (persistence images, landscapes, kernels, GRIL, signed measure convolutions) that rely on weaker invariants like the rank-invariant or signed barcode, graphcodes encode a complete description of the two-parameter persistence module. The construction is conceptually related to persistent vineyards [17], which also produce layered graphs of persistence diagrams, but operates on bifiltered complexes that grow along both axes rather than a fixed complex with changing simplex order. The practical pipeline depends on recent Delaunay bifiltration algorithms [2] to generate the required bifiltered complexes from point clouds efficiently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Graphcodes are, to the authors' knowledge, the first method that feeds a complete (not partial) representation of two-parameter persistent homology directly into a machine learning pipeline without an explicit vectorization step, and they do so with near-linear practical computation time—orders of magnitude faster than competing multi-parameter descriptors. The key limitation is basis-dependence: graphcodes are not topological invariants, and the standard reduction algorithm produces sparse but non-unique bases. An open direction identified by the authors is combining sparse graphcodes with random basis changes during training, which empirically improved accuracy in their experiments, and decomposing two-parameter modules into indecomposable summands to reduce edge count. The mixed results on graph datasets (competitive but not dominant) versus strong results on inherently topological point-cloud tasks suggest the method's advantage is most pronounced when topological signal is the primary discriminative feature.
