## Problem

DBSCAN's core step of computing ε-neighborhoods for every point requires O(n²) time and, when a large ε is needed for parameter exploration, O(n²) memory, making it infeasible on million-point high-dimensional datasets. Existing scalable variants (sampling-based, grid-based) either require strong distributional assumptions, offer no theoretical clustering guarantees, or still suffer from quadratic worst-case complexity. The paper addresses both the computational bottleneck and the lack of a scalable tool for selecting the critical ε parameter.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of n high-dimensional points (normalized to the unit sphere for cosine distance, or embedded via random kernel features for L1, L2, χ², and JS distances), a distance threshold ε, a density threshold minPts, and D random projection vectors (typically D=1024). It outputs cluster labels for all points plus, via sOPTICS, a reachability-plot dendrogram that visualizes clustering structure across a range of ε values. The approximate neighborhood of each point is bounded to O(k·minPts) candidates rather than the full O(n) set, keeping memory linear in n.

## Method

sDBSCAN exploits the extreme order statistics property of Gaussian random projections (CEOs): for a point q, the top-k random vectors closest to q preserve the inner-product ordering of all other points with q asymptotically. The algorithm precomputes, for each point, its top-k closest/furthest projection vectors, and for each vector, the top-m closest/furthest points (m=O(minPts)). To identify core points, it computes distances only between q and the 2km candidate points associated with its extreme vectors, reducing per-point cost from O(n) to O(k·minPts). Theoretical guarantees (Theorem 1) show that under a mild min-cut condition on the DBSCAN graph, sDBSCAN recovers the exact clustering with high probability. The approach extends to L1, L2, χ², and JS distances via random Fourier or sampling-based kernel feature embeddings, and uses Fast Hadamard Transform (Structured Spinners) to reduce projection cost to O(D log D) per point. sOPTICS reuses the same indexing to compute approximate reachability distances, producing dendrograms in seconds-to-minutes that guide ε selection.

## Results

On MNIST (n=70,000, d=784) with cosine distance, sDBSCAN matches scikit-learn DBSCAN's AMI of 43% while running in 8.8s versus 86s (~10× faster). On the million-point Pamap2 dataset (n=1.77M), sDBSCAN achieves 46% NMI on L1 in 0.3 minutes, compared to 50% for exact DBSCAN in 29.3 minutes. On Mnist8m (n=8.1M), sDBSCAN-1NN reaches 38% NMI on χ² and JS distances in 15 minutes on a single 32-core machine, approaching the 41% NMI of kernel k-means that requires 15 minutes on a 32-node supercomputer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

sDBSCAN ties scikit-learn DBSCAN on clustering accuracy (43% AMI on MNIST cosine) while running ~10× faster (8.8s vs 86s), and on Pamap2 achieves 46% NMI on L1 versus 50% for exact DBSCAN but in 0.3 min vs 29.3 min. Against DBSCAN++, sDBSCAN matches uDBSCAN++ on MNIST (43% AMI) and is faster, while kDBSCAN++ collapses to 7% AMI; on Pamap2 cosine, uDBSCAN++ edges out sDBSCAN (46% vs 34% NMI) but sDBSCAN is ~15× faster. sDBSCAN consistently outperforms sngDBSCAN by 5–10% NMI across all datasets and distances (e.g., 32% vs 26% on Mnist8m cosine, 38% vs 28% on χ²). pDBSCAN matches DBSCAN accuracy but is infeasible at scale (1.85 hours on MNIST, cannot finish on million-point sets). On Mnist8m, sDBSCAN-1NN (38% NMI on χ²/JS) approaches kernel k-means (41% NMI) but runs on a single machine versus a 32-node supercomputer. sDBSCAN also significantly outperforms k-means++ (46% vs 36% NMI on Pamap2 cosine) while supporting arbitrary distance measures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DBSCAN and OPTICS by replacing their O(n²) exact neighborhood queries with a random-projection-based index grounded in the extreme order statistics theory of CEOs (Pham, KDD 2021). It departs from prior sampling-based DBSCAN variants (DBSCAN++, sngDBSCAN) that require strong distributional assumptions for their guarantees, instead leveraging the asymptotic independence of top-k extreme projections to obtain guarantees under only a mild cluster-connectivity condition. The extension to L1, L2, χ², and JS distances repurposes standard random Fourier and additive kernel feature mappings (Rahimi & Recht 2007; Vedaldi & Zisserman 2012) as a preprocessing step before the same projection-based neighborhood search.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

sDBSCAN makes density-based clustering practical on million-point datasets (minutes on a single 32-core machine vs. hours or infeasibility for scikit-learn), and sOPTICS provides the first scalable visual tool for ε selection that was previously blocked by O(n²) memory. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) requires an asymptotic D→∞ and a min-cut assumption on cluster connectivity that may not hold for arbitrarily thin clusters, and the practical m=O(minPts) setting is justified heuristically rather than by the theorem. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-spherical or manifold-structured data where the unit-sphere inner-product geometry is less natural, and whether the 1NN border-point heuristic can be made theoretically sound.
