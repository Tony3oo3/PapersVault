## Problem

Most causal discovery algorithms assume access to infinite interventional samples after each intervention, which is unrealistic in practice where interventional data is costly to obtain. Existing sample-efficient Bayesian methods either impose parametric assumptions on the structural causal model (e.g., linear-Gaussian) or restrict the graph to trees, making them inapplicable to general DAGs where the Markov equivalence class can be exponentially large. This paper addresses causal discovery with a finite, limited budget of interventional samples in a fully non-parametric setting for general chordal DAGs.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input the undirected chordal chain graph (UCCG) derived from the essential graph via PC algorithm and Meek Rules on infinite observational data, a precomputed (n,k)-separating system that cuts all edges, the observational joint distribution, and a total of N interventional samples collected by randomly selecting a target set from the separating system for each sample. It outputs posterior probabilities for every cut configuration (orientation of edges adjacent to each intervention target) and a candidate DAG constructed greedily from the highest-posterior compatible configurations. The algorithm is anytime: a candidate DAG can be returned after any number of samples, with theoretical guarantees that the true DAG is recovered with high probability once N exceeds the bound in Theorem 3.

## Method

The key insight is to track posteriors over cut configurations (orientations of edges adjacent to intervention targets) rather than over all DAGs in the MEC, reducing the hypothesis space from exponentially many DAGs to at most 2^{kd_m} configurations per target. Under faithfulness, each cut configuration induces a unique interventional distribution (Lemma 1), so the posterior over configurations is well-defined and consistent. For each target set in the (n,k)-separating system, the algorithm enumerates all valid cut configurations, computes the corresponding interventional distribution by sampling a DAG from each chordal chain component of the resulting MPDAG using the polynomial-time sampler of Wienobst et al. (2023), and updates posteriors via Bayes' rule as samples arrive. Priors are set proportional to the MEC size of each configuration's MPDAG, computed by the same polynomial-time counting algorithm. The final DAG is assembled greedily by selecting the highest-posterior configuration at each unvisited target that is compatible with previously chosen configurations, and Theorem 3 provides an explicit sample complexity bound of O(β²/D² · log(2^{(k+1)d_m}/δ) + 1/D · log(2^{kd_m}/γ)) per target.

## Results

The paper evaluates on simulated random chordal DAGs (orders n=5,6,7,10,20; densities ρ=0.2,0.3,0.6,1.0) and scale-free Barabási-Albert graphs, measuring Structural Hamming Distance (SHD) against the number of interventional samples. The proposed Bayesian algorithm consistently reaches SHD=0 (exact recovery) with significantly fewer interventional samples than all baselines, and its sample requirement remains stable across graph order and density. On complete graphs (ρ=1) with n=5,6,7, the proposed method converges to zero SHD in a fraction of the samples needed by the DCT and Choo-Shiragur baselines. On large graphs (n=20) and scale-free graphs, the advantage is even more pronounced, with baselines requiring substantially more samples to reach comparable SHD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four baselines: (1) Random Intervention, which randomly selects targets from the separating system and uses independence tests; (2) Squires et al. 2020 (DCT), an adaptive two-stage algorithm using directed clique trees; (3) Choo and Shiragur 2023, an adaptivity-sensitive search algorithm based on clique tree representations; and (4) AVICI (Lorch et al. 2022), a gradient-based Bayesian causal discovery method. The proposed method outperforms all four baselines on SHD across every tested setting (complete, sparse, and scale-free graphs of varying sizes). Against the three non-adaptive/adaptive structural baselines, the proposed algorithm reaches the same low SHD with markedly fewer interventional samples, and its performance is more stable across densities (baselines degrade on denser graphs while the proposed method does not). Against AVICI, the proposed method achieves lower SHD while AVICI fails to learn the causal structure, exhibiting high expected SHD; the authors attribute AVICI's poor performance to its parametric SCM assumptions and lack of convergence guarantees. The proposed method also has the practical advantage of being anytime (can stop and return a candidate DAG after any number of samples), whereas AVICI degrades when given too many samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) non-adaptive causal discovery via separating systems (Shanmugam et al. 2015, Kocaoglu et al. 2017), which provides the intervention design; (2) Bayesian causal discovery with finite samples, previously limited to either parametric SCMs (Heckerman et al. 1997, Toth et al. 2022) or tree-structured graphs (Greenewald et al. 2019); and (3) polynomial-time DAG sampling from Markov equivalence classes (Wienobst et al. 2023), which makes the likelihood computation tractable. The key departure is that prior non-parametric Bayesian methods could only handle forests, while parametric methods risk incorrect conclusions when SCM assumptions are violated; this paper is the first to track posteriors over cut configurations (bounded by 2^{kd_m}) rather than full DAGs, enabling a non-parametric Bayesian approach for general chordal DAGs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: real-world interventional data (e.g., gene perturbation experiments) is expensive and limited, yet most causal discovery theory assumes infinite interventional samples. The explicit sample complexity bounds (Theorem 3, Corollary 4) give practitioners a principled way to determine how many samples are needed for a desired confidence level. Key limitations include exponential scaling with maximum degree for dense graphs, and the reliance on faithfulness and causal sufficiency assumptions. Open questions include reducing the posterior tracking space for large dense graphs, weakening the faithfulness assumption, and extending to latent confounders. The case study on estimating causal effects of non-intervenable vertices demonstrates practical relevance for settings where direct intervention is impossible.
