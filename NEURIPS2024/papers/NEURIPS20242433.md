## Problem

Existing online Bayesian persuasion algorithms require the sender to know either the prior distribution over states of nature or the receiver's utility function (or both). This paper is the first to address the fully unknown setting where the sender has no knowledge of either the prior or the receiver's utilities, and receives only the receiver's best-response action as feedback after each round.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242433/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The sender commits to a signaling scheme each round, observes a state of nature drawn from an unknown prior, sends a signal, and receives only the receiver's best-response action as feedback (not the full utility realization). The algorithm outputs a sequence of signaling schemes over T rounds achieving Õ(√T) regret, and in the PAC-learning variant, a single γ-optimal signaling scheme with probability at least 1−η. The key constraint is that the sender cannot directly query a slice to learn the receiver's response; it must commit to full signaling schemes and wait for the relevant signal to be triggered, which depends on the unknown prior.

## Method

The algorithm is a three-phase explore-then-commit procedure built around a non-standard 'slice' representation of signaling schemes, where a slice is the d-dimensional vector of signal probabilities across all states for a single signal. Phase 1 (Build-Search-Space) estimates the prior via multiplicative Chernoff bounds to identify the subset of normalized slices inducible with probability at least ε. Phase 2 (Find-Polytopes) learns the separating hyperplanes between receiver actions by querying slices through an Action-Oracle, identifying polytopes where each action is the unique best response. Phase 3 (Compute-Signaling) solves a linear program over the learned polytopes to produce an approximately-optimal signaling scheme. The critical insight is that working in slice space allows the algorithm to learn separating hyperplane coefficients (products of prior probabilities and utility differences) exactly, without decomposing them into individually unknown factors, which would be impossible under the no-knowledge assumption.

## Results

The paper's main result (Theorem 1) shows that Algorithm 1 achieves regret R_T ≤ Õ(choose(d+n, d)) n^(3/2) d^3 √(BT), where d is the number of states of nature, n is the number of receiver actions, B is the bit-complexity, and T is the number of rounds. Lower bounds (Theorems 2 and 3) prove this is tight: the exponential dependence on d and n via the binomial coefficient is unavoidable, and the √T dependence is also optimal. For the PAC-learning variant (Theorem 4), the sample complexity is T ≤ Õ(n^3/γ^2 log^2(1/η)(d^8 B + d·choose(d+n,d))), with matching lower bounds (Theorems 5–6) showing 2^Ω(n), 2^Ω(d), and Ω(1/γ^2 log(1/η)) are all necessary.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or direct method comparisons. The paper does not benchmark against prior online Bayesian persuasion algorithms (e.g., Castiglioni et al. 2020b, Bernasconi et al. 2023, Zu et al. 2021) on shared instances. Instead, its contribution is establishing the first no-regret algorithm for the fully unknown setting (no prior, no receiver utilities) and proving matching lower bounds that demonstrate the regret guarantees are tight in T, d, and n. The closest technical predecessor is Bacchiocchi et al. 2024a on Stackelberg games, whose polytope-learning procedure is adapted here, but no head-to-head comparison is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online Bayesian persuasion literature, which has previously relaxed either the sender's knowledge of the prior (Zu et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2022; Bernasconi et al. 2022) or of the receiver's utilities (Castiglioni et al. 2020b, 2021b, 2023), but never both simultaneously. The key technical departure is the introduction of a 'slice' representation of signaling schemes, which decouples the learning problem from the unknown prior and utilities by allowing the algorithm to learn separating hyperplanes directly. The polytope-finding procedure in Phase 2 is adapted from Bacchiocchi et al. (2024a) on Stackelberg games, with modifications to handle the Bayesian persuasion-specific structure of the search space and the need for an Action-Oracle that queries slices through repeated signaling scheme commitments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to achieve sublinear regret in the fully unknown Bayesian persuasion setting, closing a gap that prior online learning approaches could not address because they required at least one of the two pieces of information. The tight lower bounds (matching the algorithm's regret in T, d, and n) establish that the exponential dependence on the number of states and actions is fundamentally unavoidable, which is important for understanding the limits of what is learnable. The slice representation is a reusable conceptual tool that may inform future work on other information-design problems with partial knowledge. Open questions include whether the exponential dependence can be circumvented under additional structural assumptions (e.g., binary actions, known utility differences), and whether the approach extends to multi-receiver or sequential (Markov) settings.
