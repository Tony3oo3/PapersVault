## Problem

Existing biologically plausible sequential memory models (e.g., temporal predictive coding, asymmetric Hopfield networks) suffer from catastrophic forgetting, limited capacity that degrades with sequence correlation, and an inability to represent and stochastically generate multiple valid future continuations from the same context. No prior model operating under local synaptic computations can simultaneously encode high-order context, learn sequences online without revisiting them, avoid overwriting prior knowledge, and produce sharp single predictions from ambiguous contexts.

## Input / Output

PAM consumes sequences of Sparse Distributed Representations (SDRs) — high-dimensional binary vectors with a fixed small number of active bits (e.g., Nc=100, W=5) — and produces auto-regressively generated SDR sequences. In offline mode only the first SDR is supplied and the model generates the full sequence; in online mode a noisy SDR sequence is provided and the model corrects each observation using learned attractor priors before predicting the next step. For vision and protein inputs, a separate SDR autoencoder (CNN encoder/decoder with top-K binarization) maps dense inputs to fixed-sparsity SDRs, and the generated SDRs are decoded back to images or amino-acid sequences.

## Method

PAM couples a predictive state model (inspired by HTM) with a generative asymmetric Hopfield attractor model, both trained online via local Hebbian rules. Each 1-D observation SDR is expanded into a 2-D latent state (Nc × Nk) by an orthogonal context dimension; lateral inhibition within each minicolumn selects a single context neuron, yielding a unique context-dependent representation for every input position. Transition weights A are updated by Hebbian potentiation/depression between consecutive latent states, while emission weights B are updated by a contrastive rule that strengthens excitatory synapses within the observed pattern and bidirectional inhibitory synapses between the observed pattern and all other predicted possibilities, forming fixed-point attractors. The entire framework is derived as a State Space Model with a Gaussian Mixture emission, and Theorem 1 proves that maximizing the GMM log-likelihood is equivalent to the Hopfield recall function, grounding the attractor dynamics in variational free-energy minimization. Sparsity of SDRs is essential: it allows the union of predicted possibilities to be represented as a set of non-overlapping cell assemblies from which a single valid continuation is stochastically sampled at generation time.

## Results

On the catastrophic forgetting benchmark (10 sequences, Nc=100, T=10), PAM with Nk=4 achieves a perfect Backward Transfer (BWT) score of 1.000±0.000 normalized IoU, meaning zero forgetting of previously learned sequences. In contrast, tPC degrades to a BWT of ~0.17 and AHN (d=2, W=0.5Nc) to ~0.69. On offline sequence capacity, PAM scales with context neurons Nk (up to Nk=24) and retains capacity under high sequence correlation, while tPC and AHN capacity drops sharply. On multiple-possibilities generation (100 four-letter words), PAM stochastically generates valid words with increasing dataset recall over repeated generations, whereas tPC and AHN produce only meaningless character repetitions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAM decisively outperforms both tPC [62] and AHN [58,12] across all evaluated tasks. On catastrophic forgetting, PAM (Nk≥4) achieves perfect BWT of 1.000, while tPC collapses to ~0.17 (near-complete forgetting) and AHN retains only ~0.69; on the harder ProteinNet sequences (high correlation >0.9, length ~hundreds), PAM with Nk=16–24 still outperforms both baselines which fail entirely. On offline sequence capacity, PAM's capacity increases monotonically with Nk and is robust to correlation, whereas AHN capacity drops sharply with correlation and tPC is limited by its low-order Markov memory. On multiple-possibilities generation, PAM is the only method that produces valid English words and shows stochastic sampling (recall increases with repeated generations), while tPC and AHN output only degenerate character strings. On noise robustness, PAM can fully replace a corrupted input with its prior belief (even at 40% noise on CLEVRER), whereas tPC and AHN propagate the noise forward. On efficiency, PAM runs entirely on CPU and is 2–3 orders of magnitude faster than 2-layer tPC for long sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAM unifies three previously separate lines of work: the HTM framework (Hawkins & Ahmad, 2016) provides the predictor's lateral-inhibition context encoding in cortical minicolumns; the asymmetric Hopfield network (Sompolinsky & Kanter, 1986) provides the generative attractor dynamics; and the Free Energy Principle (Friston, 2010) supplies the variational-inference derivation that ties both together as a State Space Model with a GMM emission. The key departure from HTM is the addition of an orthogonal context dimension (Nk neurons per minicolumn) and a contrastive attractor-learning rule (Eq. 8) that enables stochastic sampling from multiple valid futures—something HTM cannot do. The key departure from AHN is that context encoding via lateral inhibition prevents new transitions from overwriting old ones, solving the catastrophic-forgetting problem that plagues standard Hopfield recall.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PAM is one of the first biologically plausible architectures to simultaneously satisfy all five constraints the authors identify (no catastrophic forgetting, context-dependent encoding, stochastic multi-possibility generation, online/streaming learning, and noise robustness) using only local Hebbian updates on CPU. The formal equivalence between GMM log-likelihood maximization and Hopfield recall (Theorem 1) provides a clean theoretical bridge between probabilistic generative modeling and associative memory. However, the method is restricted to sparse binary SDRs, requiring an external autoencoder for dense inputs like images, and all key experiments are at small scale (4-letter words, short protein sequences, 10-frame video clips) with no comparison to modern deep sequence models (transformers, RNNs). Open questions include whether the SDR constraint can be relaxed, how to hierarchically compose SDRs for high-dimensional sensory inputs, and whether the approach scales to genuinely long sequences (thousands of steps) or large vocabularies.
