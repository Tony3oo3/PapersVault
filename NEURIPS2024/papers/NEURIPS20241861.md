## Problem

While it is widely accepted that starting neural network training with large learning rates improves generalization, two questions remain unresolved: how large an initial LR is actually required for optimal quality, and what concretely differentiates models trained with different LR values. Prior work has identified three broad training regimes (convergence, chaotic equilibrium, divergence) but lacks a precise characterization of the optimal LR sub-range and a unified account of how loss-landscape geometry and feature learning behavior change across that range.

## Input / Output

The study operates on RGB images (CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 at 32×32, Tiny ImageNet at 64×64) and 32-dimensional synthetic data vectors containing 16 interpretable 2D 'tick' features. Beyond standard class-label prediction, the analysis produces structured diagnostic outputs: test accuracy curves across a sweep of pre-training LRs, angular distances and linear-path error barriers between fine-tuned/SWA checkpoints, per-feature accuracy on single-feature test samples, and per-frequency-band accuracy obtained by masking 2D DFT spectra into four components (background, low, mid, high).

## Method

The paper trains fully scale-invariant models (fixed last layer, no affine normalization parameters) using projected SGD on a sphere of fixed radius, so that a fixed LR corresponds to a fixed effective learning rate. Training is split into a pre-training stage with a constant LR (PLR) spanning all three regimes, followed by either fine-tuning with a small first-regime LR (FLR) or stochastic weight averaging (SWA) of subsequent checkpoints. The authors then characterize the resulting solutions along three axes: (i) generalization quality of fine-tuned/SWA models as a function of PLR, revealing that only a narrow sub-range (subregime 2A) just above the convergence threshold yields optimal results; (ii) local loss-landscape geometry via angular distance and linear connectivity barriers, showing that subregime 2A locates a convex basin of linearly connected, well-generalizing minima while smaller LRs find unstable minima and larger LRs scatter across diverse, disconnected optima; and (iii) feature learning via a synthetic 16-feature classification task and 2D DFT frequency-band masking on real images, demonstrating that subregime 2A produces sparse feature representations biased toward the most task-relevant features (e.g., mid-frequencies for image classification), whereas smaller LRs attempt to learn all features simultaneously and larger LRs degrade feature extraction altogether.

## Results

In the practical ResNet-18 setting on CIFAR-10, fine-tuning from subregime 2A (initial LRs just above the convergence threshold) reaches ~95% test accuracy, matching standard LR schedules. In the controlled scale-invariant setting, fine-tuning from subregime 2A with any first-regime FLR yields minima of identical quality, while pre-training in subregime 2B or regime 3 degrades final accuracy. Feature sparsity (measured via single-feature test accuracy in a synthetic example and mid-frequency band accuracy on CIFAR-10) peaks precisely in subregime 2A, with mid-frequency features reaching substantially higher accuracy than background or low-frequency components. Linear connectivity between fine-tuned solutions is preserved in subregime 2A but lost in subregime 2B, confirming that the optimal LR range localizes a single convex basin of high-quality minima.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead compares training outcomes across four LR regimes (Regime 1, Subregime 2A, Subregime 2B, Regime 3) as the primary axis of comparison. Subregime 2A consistently outperforms all other regimes: fine-tuning from subregime 2A achieves the highest test accuracy and the best SWA quality, while Regime 1 solutions cannot be improved by fine-tuning or SWA, Subregime 2B solutions degrade in quality and lose linear connectivity, and Regime 3 is impractical for SWA. In the practical setting, the ~95% CIFAR-10 accuracy from subregime 2A fine-tuning matches the best standard LR schedule results [49]. For feature learning, subregime 2A uniquely produces sparse feature representations biased toward mid-frequencies (the band most relevant for image classification generalization), whereas Regime 1 treats all features approximately equally and Subregime 2B degrades feature extraction entirely. The findings transfer across architectures (ResNet-18, ConvNet, ViT-S/4) and datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Tiny ImageNet), with the subregime 2A advantage being most pronounced for more complex models and datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the three-regime taxonomy of scale-invariant training on the sphere from Kodryan et al. [35] and the scale-invariant training protocol from Andriushchenko et al. [5], extending their analysis from fixed-LR training to a two-stage pre-training/fine-tuning protocol. It departs from prior sparsity literature (Andriushchenko et al. [6], Chen et al. [12], Ahn et al. [2]) which reported a monotonic increase in sparsity with LR, by showing that feature sparsity is actually non-monotonic, peaking at a narrow subregime just above the convergence threshold. The pattern-learning line of work (Li et al. [41], You et al. [68], Rosenfeld and Risteski [57]) is extended by showing that large LRs do not simply prioritize 'easy-to-fit' features but can favor more complex features (e.g., mid-frequencies) when they are more task-relevant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable guidance for LR scheduling: start training just above the convergence threshold (subregime 2A) to localize a basin of high-quality, linearly connected minima, then fine-tune or SWA. The open question of how feature sparsity mechanistically determines basin geometry (e.g., sharpness, width) remains unresolved and is flagged as an important direction. Limitations include the purely empirical nature of the study, restriction to specific architectures (ResNet, ConvNet, ViT) and datasets (CIFAR-10/100, Tiny ImageNet), and the controlled scale-invariant setup that may not fully capture all practical invariances (e.g., ReLU rescale invariance).
