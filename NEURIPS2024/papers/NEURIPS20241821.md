## Problem

Predicting the dynamics of interacting systems from irregularly sampled time series is difficult because modeling components independently ignores their interactions, while existing graph-based continuous-time methods either require expensive numerical ODE solvers or fail to capture the conditional dependency structure (causal notation) among components. The paper asks: what time series models best capture the interactive nature of different system dynamics while remaining computationally efficient for irregular sampling?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241821/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multivariate time series X(t_0), …, X(t_N) observed at irregular time points (with possible missing rows at individual time points) and initial conditions X(0), and produces continuous predictions X(t) for any t ≥ t_0 along with a learned n×n DAG adjacency matrix A encoding conditional dependencies. Missing data are handled by zero-masking the corresponding entries of A in the GCN encoder rather than extracting subgraphs, preserving tensor dimensions for batching. In latent variable mode, the output additionally includes a latent trajectory Z(t) used for reconstruction, classification, or likelihood estimation.

## Method

GNeuralFlow parameterizes the ODE solution directly as X(t) = F(t, X_0, A) using one of three invertible flow designs (ResNet, GRU, or coupling flow), where the graph encoder is a GCN with a SEM-motivated normalization Â = I − A^T/γ that guarantees bounded spectral norm (‖Â‖₂ ≤ 2) and thus invertibility. The DAG adjacency matrix A is learned jointly with all other parameters via an augmented Lagrangian method enforcing the NO TEARS constraint tr(expm(A⊙A)) = n, making the combinatorial DAG constraint a continuous equality. The key architectural insight is that the GCN aggregates information from parent nodes (per the DAG) at each time point, so each time series' flow is conditioned on its conditional dependencies rather than all neighbors, mirroring the factorization p(y^j | pa(y^j)) of a Bayesian network. For latent variable modeling, the model maintains a pair of hidden states H(t) and H̃(t), where H̃(t) is updated by a second RNN fed with GCN-transformed observations X̃(t_j), so the graph encodes interactions in the data space rather than the latent space.

## Results

On the four synthetic 5-node systems, GNeuralFlow (ResNet) achieves the lowest forecast MSE across all datasets: 3.95×10⁻⁴ (Sink), 2.32×10⁻³ (Triangle), 3.84×10⁻³ (Sawtooth), and 8.24×10⁻³ (Square), outperforming the best non-graph baseline (Neural flow ResNet: 8.41×10⁻⁴, 4.01×10⁻³, 4.73×10⁻³, 9.61×10⁻³) by 2–53%. On the MIMIC-IV filtering task (97 nodes), GNeuralFlow (GRU) achieves MSE 0.335 and NLL 0.606, beating the best graph ODE baseline (GDE: MSE 0.342, NLL 0.657). On Physionet mortality prediction, GNeuralFlow (GRU) reaches AUC 0.812, surpassing all baselines including ODE-RNN (0.781) and CG-ODE (0.783).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GNeuralFlow (ResNet) dominates all 11 baselines on every synthetic system. Against the closest graph-learning baselines NRI and dNRI, it reduces MSE by 25–35% (e.g., Sink: 3.95 vs. 5.25/5.40; Triangle: 2.32 vs. 3.96/3.39). Against graph ODE methods that use the ground-truth graph (GDE, LG-ODE, CF-GODE), the improvement is even larger—up to 53% on Sink (3.95 vs. 8.57 for LG-ODE) and 41% on Triangle (2.32 vs. 3.99 for GDE)—showing that the learned DAG structure plus the ODE-solver-free flow is more effective than simply conditioning on a known graph with a numerical solver. Against non-graph neural flows (the direct ablation), GNeuralFlow consistently wins by 20–50% across all three flow architectures, confirming that the graph encoder captures meaningful systemic interactions. On real-life data (Tables 3–4), GNeuralFlow (GRU) achieves the best Physionet AUC (0.812 vs. 0.788 for Neural flow GRU and 0.783 for CG-ODE) and the best MIMIC-IV NLL (0.606 vs. 0.649 for LG-ODE and 0.657 for GDE), with improvements exceeding the combined standard errors of the compared methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) neural flows, which parameterize ODE solutions directly to avoid costly numerical solvers (Biloš et al., 2021); (2) graph ODE models (GDE, LG-ODE, CG-ODE, CF-GODE) that use graphs to encode interactions among multiple time series but rely on adjoint-based solver calls; and (3) DAG structure learning (NO TEARS, DAG-GNN) for recovering conditional dependence structures. The key departure is enforcing a Bayesian-network (DAG) constraint on the interaction graph while using a solver-free flow parameterization, so that the learned structure carries a conditional-independence interpretation rather than being an arbitrary or time-varying adjacency. Prior graph ODE methods either use undirected or time-dependent graphs without acyclicity, and prior neural flow methods treat trajectories independently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DAG formulation gives the learned interaction graph a causal/conditional-dependence reading, opening a path toward interpretable causal discovery in dynamical systems (e.g., power-grid cascade propagation). A notable open question is identifiability: the authors show empirically that a DAG substantially different from the ground truth can still yield better downstream predictions, but they have not established theoretical conditions under which the true structure is recoverable. Scalability remains a practical bottleneck—the DAG constraint involves a matrix exponential whose cost grows cubically with the number of nodes, limiting applicability to systems with a few hundred components at most. Future work could explore low-rank or sparse structural priors on A to mitigate this.
