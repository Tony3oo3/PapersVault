## Problem

While empirical studies show that LLMs trained on next-token prediction learn hierarchical linguistic structure and exhibit scaling laws, there is no theoretical framework explaining how the depth of learned structure depends on training set size. The relationship between token correlations, the effective context window a model can exploit, and the emergence of deeper representations at specific data scales remains unexplained, leaving both scaling laws and skill emergence as phenomenological observations.

## Input / Output

The model receives a context window of t consecutive tokens and outputs a probability distribution over the masked last token, trained via cross-entropy on P examples. The theoretical analysis centers on the token-token correlation function C̃(t) measured as a function of distance t from the masked token, which in the RHM decays stepwise (mirroring tree depth) and approximately as a power law t^(−β). The key derived quantities are the effective context window t*(P) ~ P^(1/2β) and a context-dependent scaling law L(P,t) = t^(−αz)·f(P/t^z) that predicts loss saturation as a function of both P and t.

## Method

The framework is built on the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM), an ensemble of PCFGs with fixed tree geometry where hidden variables reside on tree nodes and production rules are drawn uniformly at random. The authors analytically derive the stepwise decay of token-token correlations (Eq. 8), showing that a finite training set P limits correlation resolution to an effective range t*(P), with threshold sample complexities P_ℓ = vm^(2ℓ−1) for resolving level-ℓ correlations. They argue that deep networks exploit these measurable correlations to reconstruct hidden variables bottom-up: pairs of tokens sharing the same correlation signature are grouped as descendants of the same hidden symbol, and this process iterates to higher levels as P grows. The resulting sequence of discrete performance steps (each corresponding to a deeper subtree being resolved) conspires to produce the observed power-law scaling of test loss with P, unifying the emergence and scaling paradigms. The conjecture that the P–t*(P) relationship holds beyond synthetic data is validated on Shakespeare and Wikipedia, where BERT-like transformers show loss saturation at a characteristic P that scales as t^z with z = 2β measured from empirical correlations.

## Results

The paper derives an analytical scaling law for next-token prediction loss on hierarchical data: L(P) + log(1−f) ≈ log(1 + v(1−f)·exp[log(f)·log(P)/(2log m)]), which yields a power-law decay P^(−α) with α = log f/(2 log m) at large P. The predicted sample complexities for resolving correlations at distance t_ℓ = s^ℓ − 1 are P_ℓ = v·m^(2ℓ−1)·(1 − m/v^(s−1))^(−1), growing polynomially in the effective context length. On tiny-Shakespeare, the token correlation decay exponent is β ≈ 1.4 (z = 2β ≈ 2.8), and on WikiText-103, z ≈ 3.1 with α ≈ 0.095. The context-dependent scaling hypothesis L(P,t) = t^(−αz)·f(P/t^z) is confirmed by data collapse on both real datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper that validates its own analytical predictions against empirical learning curves rather than comparing against competing methods. The theoretical stepwise loss predictions (Eq. 11) and sample complexity thresholds (Eq. 12) are compared against learning curves of depth-3 transformers and depth-4 CNNs trained on RHM data, showing good qualitative agreement: the vertical dashed lines marking P_ℓ align with the observed steps in test loss, and the horizontal dashed lines (upper bounds L_ℓ) match the plateau values. The conjecture that effective context window size t*(P) ∝ P^(1/z) is tested on tiny-Shakespeare and WikiText-103 by training BERT-like transformers with varying context window lengths t; the predicted saturation of loss decay at P*(t) ∝ t^z is confirmed, and the data collapse under the rescaling L(P,t) = t^(−αz)·f(P/t^z) holds quantitatively. No direct comparison with alternative scaling-law theories (e.g., kernel-limit analyses [46–50]) is performed, though the authors note their framework is the first to account for data-feature learning in scaling laws.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) framework of Cagnetta et al. [20] from supervised classification to self-supervised next-token prediction, shifting the relevant correlations from feature-label to token-token. It builds on PCFG-based analyses of transformer representations [13, 14] that showed how trained models encode grammatical structure, but departs by studying the learning process itself—how increasing training data progressively reveals deeper hierarchical structure—rather than the final representation. The work also connects to the scaling laws literature [17] by proposing a data-structure-based explanation for the power-law decay of test loss, and to empirical observations of hierarchical inductive bias in LSTMs [34] and transformers [35], providing a theoretical account for why short-range dependencies are learned before long-range ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers the first theoretical description of scaling laws in a regime where learning data features (rather than kernel limits) is the bottleneck, unifying the 'emergence' and 'scaling' paradigms into a single picture driven by correlation resolution. Its central conjecture—that a finite training set imposes an effective context window whose size grows as a power of P, determined by the correlation decay exponent—makes a specific, testable prediction about context-dependent scaling that is validated on Shakespeare and Wikipedia. Key limitations include the context-free and fixed-geometry assumptions of the RHM, the absence of a formal proof linking the proposed correlation-based strategy to actual gradient descent dynamics, and the small scale of the real-data experiments; extending the framework to context-sensitive structures and to the role of model size under fixed compute budgets are natural next steps.
