## Problem

Training sparse neural networks is difficult because introducing sparsity narrows the effective network structure, producing a chaotic and sharp loss surface that causes optimization to converge to sub-optimal stationary points. While Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) can find flatter minima to improve generalization, its two-step gradient computation doubles training cost, directly contradicting the efficiency goal of sparse training. The paper asks whether sharpness-aware optimization can be achieved with zero extra computation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241404/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates as a drop-in replacement for the gradient update step within any existing sparse training pipeline. At each iteration it consumes the current sparse weight vector w_{t-1}, the weight gradient g_{t-1} already computed in the previous step, and the loss function, then produces a single updated weight vector w_t. No additional forward or backward pass is required; the only new computation is a normalization of the stored prior gradient and one gradient evaluation at the perturbed point u_t. The method is applied at a fixed hyperparameter ρ (0.05 for CIFAR, 1.0 for ImageNet) across all sparsity levels and sparse training baselines.

## Method

S²-SAM replaces SAM's two-step gradient computation with a single-step scheme by approximating the sharpness perturbation direction using the weight gradient g_{t-1} from the prior iteration rather than computing a fresh gradient at the current point. Specifically, it forms the perturbed point u_t = w_{t-1} + ρ·g_{t-1}/||g_{t-1}||, computes the gradient g_t = ∇L_S(u_t) once, and updates w_t = w_{t-1} − η_t(g_t + λw_{t-1}). The rationale is that in the chaotic loss landscape of sparse networks, the prior gradient direction already captures a sufficiently sharp perturbation to locate a near-maximum-loss neighborhood, making the extra gradient evaluation unnecessary. The paper proves convergence under standard smoothness and Polyak-Łojasiewicz assumptions, showing the excess risk bound scales as O(1/√n) with appropriate learning rate and iteration choices. Experimentally, S²-SAM serves as a plug-and-play module on top of static (LTH, SNIP, GraSP) and dynamic (SET, RigL, MEST, DSR, Top-KAST) sparse training methods, as well as structured sparsity methods (CHEX, Chase), consistently improving accuracy by 0.3–2.0% at 90–98% sparsity while maintaining the same GPU throughput as the unmodified baselines.

## Results

On CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-32 at 98% sparsity, S²-SAM improves baseline accuracy by an average of 0.77% (CIFAR-10) and 1.12% (CIFAR-100) across all sparse training methods. On ImageNet-1K with ResNet-50 at 90% sparsity, top-1 accuracy gains range from 0.36% to 1.18% (e.g., RigL 74.6→75.39%, MEST(EM) 75.7→76.35%). Training throughput is preserved (e.g., 2132 vs. 2148 imgs/s for GraSP), while standard SAM drops to less than half (1021 imgs/s). On ImageNet-C robustness evaluation at 80% sparsity, S²-SAM yields an average 3.23% improvement in corrupted accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S²-SAM consistently outperforms all 11 sparse training baselines it is applied to, with the largest gains observed at higher sparsity (98%) and on CIFAR-100. RigL benefits most on CIFAR-100 (up to +2.07% at 95% sparsity), while MEST(EM) shows the largest CIFAR-10 gain (+2.00% at 98% sparsity). SET and DSR see moderate but consistent improvements of 0.47–1.31% across sparsity levels. For static methods, SNIP and GraSP gain 0.44–1.02%, while LTH shows minimal or slightly negative gains (e.g., 87.51 vs. 88.78 at 98% CIFAR-10), suggesting S²-SAM is most effective for dynamic sparse training. On structured sparsity (CHEX, Chase), improvements are smaller (~0.43–0.55%) but still positive. Compared to standard SAM, S²-SAM achieves comparable accuracy (e.g., 72.44% vs. 72.75% for RigL on ImageNet-1K) at roughly 2× the training throughput (3098 vs. 1508 imgs/s), making it the practical choice for sparse training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM, Foret et al. 2021) by replacing its two-step gradient computation with a single-step update that reuses the prior iteration's gradient as a proxy for the sharpness perturbation. Conceptually, it bridges the sparse training literature (LTH, SNIP, GraSP, SET, RigL, MEST, etc.) with the loss-landscape analysis literature, arguing that sparsity narrows the effective network width and induces a chaotic, sharp loss surface that degrades generalization. Unlike prior efficient-SAM variants (ESAM, LookSAM, SAF) that reduce but still incur nonzero overhead, S²-SAM claims zero extra computation cost, positioning it as the first SAM-style optimizer truly compatible with the efficiency goals of sparse training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-extra-cost property makes sharpness-aware optimization practically deployable in sparse training pipelines where even a 2× compute penalty is prohibitive, and the plug-and-play design (single fixed ρ per dataset) lowers the adoption barrier across heterogeneous sparse training methods. The demonstrated robustness gains on ImageNet-C (average +3.23%) suggest that flatter minima from S²-SAM translate to real-world resilience, which is critical for edge deployment. Open questions include whether the prior-gradient approximation remains valid under aggressive sparsity schedules or in non-convex regimes beyond vision CNNs, and whether the method's benefits compound with other regularization strategies. The authors explicitly note that dense-model training is left for future work, and the fixed ρ hyperparameter (0.05 for CIFAR, 1.0 for ImageNet) has not been ablated across architectures.
