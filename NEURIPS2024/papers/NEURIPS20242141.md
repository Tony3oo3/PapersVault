## Problem

Current XAI evaluation studies are fragmented, examining only a handful of methods and metrics while ignoring critical design parameters like model architecture and input modality, leading to contradictory rankings across studies. Additionally, relying on one or two metrics per evaluation criterion introduces severe selection bias, as metrics frequently disagree in their rankings of the same method. These two shortcomings leave practitioners without a reliable basis for choosing an XAI method for their specific problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242141/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes trained classification models (ResNet50/EfficientNetb0/DeiT ViT for images, 3D ResNet18/3D EfficientNetb0/Simple3DFormer for volumes, PointNet/DGCNN/PC Transformer for point clouds) and applies 17 XAI methods to produce saliency maps on 9 datasets across three modalities, yielding 7,560 unique method–architecture–dataset combinations. The three attention-based methods (Raw Attention, Rollout Attention, LRP Attention) are restricted to Transformer architectures only. The released LATEC dataset contains 326,790 saliency maps and 378,000 metric scores, enabling future work to start at any pipeline stage without recomputation.

## Method

LATEC systematically evaluates 14 attribution and 3 attention XAI methods using 20 metrics grouped into faithfulness (11), robustness (6), and complexity (3) criteria, across 3 modalities, 3 architectures, and 3 datasets per modality. The key methodological contribution is a proposed evaluation scheme that addresses metric disagreement: it aggregates median standardized scores across all model–dataset combinations per metric, ranks methods per metric, then reports mean rank (μ̂), median rank (x_{n/2}), and standard deviation (σ̂) to distinguish strong trends from unreliable ones. A one-sided Levene's test quantifies whether metric rank-variance is significantly lower than random, and Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney tests assess pairwise significance. XAI methods and metrics are adapted for 3D volume and point cloud data (e.g., 3D kernels for Occlusion, 3D SLIC segmentation for IROF, KMeans for point clouds), providing the first large-scale insights into XAI performance on these modalities. The benchmark identifies Expected Gradients as the consistently top-performing method in faithfulness and robustness, a method absent from all 18 prior related studies.

## Results

LATEC evaluates 17 XAI methods across 20 metrics and 7,560 design-parameter combinations (3 modalities × 3 architectures × 3 datasets each). Expected Gradients (EG) consistently ranks among the top methods for faithfulness and robustness across all modalities, yet no single method dominates all three criteria simultaneously. Metric disagreement is substantial: for methods like Occlusion, Input×Gradient, ScoreCAM, and DeepLIFT, ranking variance is significantly lower than random in only ~27% of cases, whereas for Kernel SHAP, EG, and DeepLIFT SHAP, metrics agree in a large majority of cases. The average standard deviation between faithfulness metrics is ~3.2–3.5 (out of 17 methods), robustness ~3.0–3.3, and complexity ~0.4–0.6, indicating that faithfulness and robustness rankings are far less stable than complexity rankings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 14 attribution methods, Expected Gradients (EG) emerges as the most consistently top-ranked method for faithfulness and robustness across all three modalities, with moderate metric agreement (average SD). Integrated Gradients (IG) and DeepLIFT SHAP (DLS) also perform well on faithfulness, while Kernel SHAP (KS) shows strong metric agreement but underperforms EG on high-dimensional image and volume data. CAM methods (GradCAM, ScoreCAM, GradCAM++) achieve moderate faithfulness and high robustness on 2D images but degrade substantially on 3D volume data due to coarser latent representations and up-sampling artifacts. Linear surrogate methods (LIME, Kernel SHAP) underperform on image and volume modalities relative to point cloud, with very low robustness across all settings. Attention methods (Raw Attention, Rollout Attention, LRP Attention) exhibit the highest metric disagreement (very large SD), with faithfulness and robustness metrics ranking them either very high or very low; LRP Attention (LA) outperforms the other two attention methods and is recommended over LRP on Transformer architectures. LRP shows a clear trade-off: it ranks low on faithfulness (due to attributing to very few features) but high on complexity (small attributed set), a pattern the authors attribute to the pixel-independent design of the complexity metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against a fragmented landscape of 18 prior XAI evaluation studies (summarized in Table 1) that each examine only small subsets of methods, metrics, and design parameters, leading to contradictory conclusions (e.g., GradCAM receiving opposing assessments). While prior benchmarks like Hooker et al. [30], Yang and Kim [70], and Li et al. [38] established the paradigm of quantitative XAI evaluation, LATEC departs from them by (1) extending evaluation beyond 2D images to 3D volumes and point clouds, (2) including attention-based methods alongside attribution methods, and (3) introducing a dedicated meta-evaluation of the metrics themselves via Levene's test to quantify and address selection bias. The paper also builds on the Quantus toolkit [27, 28] and Captum [34] for method implementations but restructures the evaluation around a proposed 'aggregate-then-rank' scheme with explicit disagreement measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LATEC provides the most comprehensive answer to 'which XAI method should I use?' to date, with 7,560 evaluated combinations and a publicly released dataset of 326k saliency maps and 378k metric scores that enables standardized future benchmarking. The finding that Expected Gradients consistently outperforms all other methods in faithfulness and robustness yet was absent from every prior evaluation study highlights a significant gap in the field's collective knowledge. Key open questions include whether the complexity metrics (which treat pixels independently) remain valid for high-dimensional inputs, how to resolve the extreme metric disagreement observed for attention methods, and whether the evaluation scheme generalizes to non-computer-vision modalities like language. The benchmark is limited to computer vision and excludes video, symbolic explanations, and axiomatic evaluation criteria.
