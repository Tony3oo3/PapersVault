## Problem

Third-party platforms (e.g., Uber, DoorDash) must set service fees dynamically without knowing buyer demand, observing only equilibrium prices and quantities (which render OLS biased), and facing strategic buyers who may misreport their demand to manipulate future prices. No prior framework simultaneously addresses all three challenges—demand learning under equilibrium-only observation, endogenous action correlation, and far-sighted strategic behavior—while providing optimal regret guarantees.

## Input / Output

Each round the platform observes the linear supply curve P_St(Q) = α_0 + α_1 Q + ε_St (with known or estimable parameters) and the realized equilibrium pair (P_t^e, Q_t^e) after setting a service fee a_t ≥ 0. The algorithm outputs a per-round service fee and running estimates of the linear demand parameters (β_0, β_1). Unusually, the platform never observes the demand curve directly; it must infer it solely from the equilibrium outcomes, and the service fee itself serves as the instrument for identification. The time horizon T is known a priori (or handled via a doubling trick when unknown).

## Method

The AaI (Action-as-Instruments) algorithm uses the platform's own service fee as a non-i.i.d. instrumental variable: it regresses Q^e and P^e on a_t to recover β_1 = b̂_1/â_1 and β_0 from sample means, exploiting the conditional independence between the action and demand noise ε_Dt. A low-switching-cost schedule (policy updates only at powers of 2) implicitly penalizes strategic misreporting without requiring knowledge of the buyer's discount rate γ, bounding the drift η_Dt by O(γ^{(n-i)/2}). When supply randomness σ_S > 0, no artificial noise is injected because supply fluctuations provide free exploration, yielding Õ(1) regret; when σ_S = 0, decaying Gaussian noise of variance O(1/√2^m) is added to the empirical optimal fee to sustain exploration, yielding Õ(√T) regret. The adaptive AAaI variant first runs a hypothesis test on σ_S² relative to 1/√T and then dispatches to the appropriate exploration mode, achieving the unified bound Õ(√T ∧ σ_S^{-2}) and revealing a phase transition at σ_S² ≈ Θ(1/√T).

## Results

The paper establishes that the AaI algorithm achieves Õ(√T) regret when supply is deterministic (σ_S = 0, Theorem 3.1) and Õ(1) regret when supply has randomness (σ_S > 0, Theorem 3.2), with the latter bound being O(log T · log(log T/ι)/σ_S² + log T/(σ_S²(1−γ))). The adaptive AAaI algorithm (Theorem 4.3) achieves Õ(√T ∧ σ_S^{-2}) without knowing σ_S in advance, revealing a phase transition at σ_S² ≈ Θ(1/√T). Numerical experiments with T = 10⁵ confirm these bounds: regret is ~237 (slightly sublinear) at σ_S² = 1 versus >1200 (slope ≈ 0.47 in log-log, matching √T) at σ_S² = 0, and the phase transition is observed at σ_S² ≈ 0.1 for T = 10⁴.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct algorithmic baselines; the 'comparisons' are against information-theoretic lower bounds and prior theoretical results. The paper's Õ(√T) bound in the no-noise case matches the standard dynamic pricing regret of Keskin & Zeevi [44], confirming that the added challenges (equilibrium-only observation, strategic buyers) do not worsen the fundamental rate. Against Golrezaei et al. [37], who achieve an extra O(log²T / log²(1/γ)) regret term for strategic buyers in repeated auctions, this paper improves to O(log T / (1−γ)), which is strictly better since log(1/γ) ≤ 2(1−γ) for γ ∈ [0,1). The lower bounds (Theorems 4.1–4.2) confirm that the gap between Õ(√T) and Õ(1) is fundamental and unavoidable, not an artifact of the algorithm. The adaptive AAaI algorithm closes the remaining gap between the upper and lower bounds in the intermediate regime σ_S² ≲ O(1/√T), where no prior algorithm was known to achieve the correct rate without knowing σ_S in advance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the instrumental variable (IV) framework from Nambiar et al. [56] (offline, external instruments) to an online pricing setting where the platform's own non-i.i.d. service fee actions serve as the instruments, eliminating the need for hard-to-find external variables. It generalizes the standard dynamic pricing problem of Keskin and Zeevi [44] by adding equilibrium-only observation and strategic buyer behavior, and improves upon the incentive-aware learning bounds of Golrezaei et al. [37] by achieving tighter regret in both the log T and 1/(1−γ) terms. The low-switching-cost episode design (doubling schedule) provides a robust mechanism to motivate near-truthful buyer behavior without requiring knowledge of the discount rate, departing from prior work that used explicit buffer periods or assumed γ was known.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first complete theoretical framework for service fee pricing on third-party platforms that simultaneously handles demand learning, equilibrium-only observation, and strategic buyer behavior, with matching upper and lower bounds. The phase transition phenomenon—where supply randomness above a critical threshold (σ_S² ≈ 1/√T) reduces regret from Õ(√T) to Õ(1)—is a practically actionable insight: platforms should explicitly account for market randomness in their pricing algorithms. Open questions include the exact dependence of regret on the discount rate γ (conjectured Ω(1/(1−γ)) lower bound), extension to nonlinear demand models, and whether the phase transition structure persists in high-dimensional or non-parametric settings.
