## Problem

Inferring Markov Jump Processes (MJPs) from noisy, sparse observations has traditionally required training a dedicated model on each target dataset via unsupervised or variational methods, producing parameters that are overly specific to that dataset and fail to generalize to other processes. No prior method could perform zero-shot inference of hidden MJPs across different state space dimensions without retraining, despite the coarse-grained MJP description being simple enough to be encoded in a single model.

## Input / Output

The model consumes K time series of noisy discrete state labels (integers 0 to C−1, padded to a maximum grid length L=100), their corresponding observation times (regular or irregular), and the a priori known state space dimension c (2–6). Internally, all observation times are normalized to the unit interval by dividing by the maximum time, and the predicted rate matrix and variance are rescaled back to the original time scale. The output is a c×c intensity rate matrix (with redundant entries zeroed for c < C), a corresponding variance matrix representing estimation uncertainty, and a c-dimensional initial state distribution.

## Method

The FIM methodology has two components: a synthetic data generation model that samples MJPs over state spaces of sizes 2–6 using Beta-distributed transition rates, Erdős-Rényi or fully-connected adjacency matrices, variable regular/irregular observation grids, and 1% or 10% label noise; and a supervised neural recognition model trained on 45K such synthetic processes. The recognition architecture processes each of K time series with a bidirectional LSTM path encoder, aggregates the K path embeddings via a learnable-query multi-head attention mechanism (whose output dimension is independent of K), and passes the global representation through three feed-forward networks that output the rate matrix (via exp for positivity), its variance, and the initial distribution. Training maximizes a Gaussian log-likelihood on the rates (with a regularizer penalizing non-zero predictions at structurally absent links) plus a cross-entropy loss on the initial distribution. Because the model is trained on a broad synthetic distribution covering many MJP configurations, it amortizes the inference process and performs zero-shot inference on unseen empirical processes of varying state space sizes without any fine-tuning.

## Results

FIM achieves time-averaged Hellinger distances of 0.27 (DFR), 0.41 (IonCh), 1.39 (ADP), and 0.014 (PFold) [×10⁻²], matching or beating NeuralMJP (0.30, 0.48, 1.38, 0.015) across all four datasets — all in zero-shot mode with no fine-tuning on target data. On the DFR rate-matrix inference task, FIM recovers the ground-truth parameters (V=1, r=1, b=1) with values 1.11(7), 0.99(8), 0.98(5), comparable to NeuralMJP's 1.06, 1.17, 1.14. FIM also correctly infers stationary distributions and relaxation time scales for the 6-state ADP process (e.g., slowest relaxation time 0.611 ns vs. NeuralMJP 0.774 ns and VampNets 1.920 ns).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NeuralMJP (Seifner & Sánchez, ICML 2023), the primary baseline trained on each target dataset, FIM wins on DFR (Hellinger 0.27 vs. 0.30) and IonCh (0.41 vs. 0.48), and ties on ADP (1.39 vs. 1.38) and PFold (0.014 vs. 0.015) — all without any target-specific training. Against SDiff (Köhs et al., NeurIPS 2021), FIM matches the inferred ion-channel stationary distribution closely (FIM-NMJP: 0.182/0.102/0.716 vs. SDiff: 0.180/0.150/0.671 for Bottom/Middle/Top states). Against VampNets (Mardt et al., Nature Communications 2017), FIM reproduces the ADP stationary distribution within ~0.03 per state and captures the fast relaxation time scales (0.008, 0.009 ns) identically, though the slowest mode (0.611 ns) differs from VampNets (1.920 ns) and NeuralMJP (0.774 ns). The key distinction is that all baselines require per-dataset training, whereas FIM is a single pretrained model applied zero-shot across state spaces of 2–6 dimensions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the amortized inference framework of Seifner et al. (2024), which demonstrated zero-shot time-series imputation, to the problem of inferring hidden Markov jump processes. It departs from the dominant paradigm in MJP inference—where one trains a complex variational or EM-based model directly on the target empirical data (NeuralMJP, SDiff, VampNets)—by instead training a simple supervised recognition model (BiLSTM + learnable query attention) on a heuristically constructed synthetic ensemble of MJPs. The key conceptual shift is treating MJP inference as a zero-shot recognition problem: a single pretrained model infers rate matrices and initial conditions across different state-space dimensionalities without any fine-tuning on the target dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first zero-shot solution to the classical MJP inference problem, offering a practical tool for estimating stationary distributions, relaxation times, mean first-passage times, and entropy production from noisy, sparse observations without dataset-specific training. The main limitation is the Beta prior over rates, which restricts the model to processes where the ratio of largest to smallest rates stays below ~3 orders of magnitude; performance degrades sharply beyond this (e.g., DFR with V≥3). Open questions include extending to Birth-Death processes, power-law-distributed rates (relevant to glassy systems), and unbounded state spaces. The approach also raises the broader question of how much of the 'master equation inductive bias' can be captured by a generic synthetic distribution versus requiring domain-specific priors.
