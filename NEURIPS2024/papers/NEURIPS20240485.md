## Problem

Standard medical segmentation benchmarks suffer from in-distribution test sets, small test sizes, oversimplified average-only metrics, unfair comparisons due to asymmetric optimization effort, and short-term non-recurring evaluation windows. These issues mean that strong benchmark performance does not guarantee real-world clinical reliability, and the field lacks a fair, large-scale, long-term evaluation protocol for abdominal CT segmentation.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans (slice thickness 0.5–1.5 mm, venous/arterial/post-phases) as input and produces per-voxel segmentation masks for 9 anatomical structures: spleen, liver, left and right kidneys, stomach, gallbladder, pancreas, aorta, and postcava. Training uses 5,195 scans from 76 hospitals across 8 countries (AbdomenAtlas 1.0), while evaluation is performed on 5,903 unseen scans from 11 additional hospitals (5,160 proprietary JHH + 743 TotalSegmentator). Inference speed is constrained to exceed 10⁶ mm³/s, and performance is measured via Dice Similarity Coefficient and Normalized Surface Distance on a fixed A100 GPU.

## Method

Touchstone is a collaborative benchmark rather than a single algorithm: 14 teams from 8 countries each train their own architecture (spanning CNNs in the nnU-Net framework, vision transformers, a diffusion-based Diff-UNet, a graph neural network NeXToU, and vision-language CLIP-driven models) on the shared AbdomenAtlas 1.0 training set, and the benchmark team independently evaluates all 19 submitted models on two held-out OOD test sets. The evaluation protocol is multi-faceted: per-class DSC/NSD rankings, statistical significance testing via one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank with Holm correction, per-metadata-group analysis (age, sex, race, diagnosis, scanner manufacturer) using Kruskal-Wallis with Bonferroni post-hoc tests, and inference-speed benchmarking. The benchmark commits to recurring challenges over at least five years, with the first edition held as an invitation-only challenge at ISBI-2024, and includes a proprietary JHH test set (manually annotated by radiologists) to prevent data leakage and preserve OOD integrity.

## Results

On the proprietary JHH out-of-distribution test set (N=5,160), the top-performing architectures achieve average DSC of 89.5% (ResEncL*), 89.2% (MedNeXt), and 89.0% (STU-Net-B/L/H, MedFormer), with no statistically significant differences among them at p=0.05. On the public TotalSegmentator test set (N=743), STU-Net-L leads at 83.0% average DSC, followed by STU-Net-H (82.7%) and ResEncL (82.7%). Per-organ DSC varies by 10–20% for most architectures (e.g., gallbladder ~85% vs. liver ~96% on JHH), and up to 80% for SAM-Adapter on kidney segmentation across the two test sets, underscoring the importance of diverse OOD evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CNN architectures within the nnU-Net framework (MedNeXt, STU-Net, ResEncL, U-Net) cluster tightly at 88.8–89.5% average DSC on JHH, with no statistically significant differences among them, establishing a strong baseline that is difficult to surpass. MedFormer, a CNN–Transformer hybrid, matches this group at 89.0%, while Diff-UNet (the first diffusion-based 3D medical segmentation model) trails by only 1.0% at 88.5%. MONAI-hosted pure Transformer models (Swin UNETR, UNETR, UNesT) underperform the nnU-Net CNNs by 4–6% on JHH and by 15–36% on TotalSegmentator, suggesting that current Transformer pipelines have not yet closed the gap. SAM-Adapter, a 2D foundation-model-based approach, is dramatically worse (73.4% JHH, 28.8% TotalSegmentator), with an 80-point DSC swing in kidney segmentation between the two test sets. On a per-organ basis, NeXToU (graph neural network) achieves the best aorta DSC (86.4% on JHH) and Diff-UNet leads on kidneys (92.8%), revealing hidden strengths invisible in average rankings. LHU-Net offers the best speed–accuracy trade-off, running 2–4× faster than comparably accurate models (2273 cm³/s vs. ~300–800 cm³/s).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the evaluation methodology for medical segmentation rather than building on a specific algorithmic lineage. It departs from the standard paper-based comparison paradigm (where authors optimize their own method while under-optimizing baselines) and from existing challenges (KiTS, FLARE, MSD) by addressing five identified gaps: in-distribution test sets, small test sizes, oversimplified metrics, asymmetric optimization effort, and short-term outcome pressure. Conceptually, it extends the challenge-based evaluation model (inventors train, third party evaluates) with a much larger proprietary OOD test set (JHH, N=5,160), multi-faceted per-class and per-metadata analysis, and an explicit long-term commitment to recurring editions. The training data (AbdomenAtlas 1.0) itself builds on the human-in-the-loop annotation strategy from prior work, but the benchmark's evaluation philosophy is the primary contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark is likely to become a standard reference for abdominal CT segmentation evaluation given its scale (5,903 OOD test scans, 8.6× larger than any prior challenge test set), its proprietary test set that prevents data leakage, and its five-year commitment to recurring challenges. Key open questions remain: whether CNNs' dominance reflects an intrinsic architectural advantage or merely nnU-Net's superior pipeline maturity; whether the proprietary test set approach is sustainable as feedback times increase; and whether per-case metadata analysis (planned for the next edition) will reveal more nuanced failure modes. The finding that moderate label noise (2.6%) is tolerable but high noise (32.4%) is not provides practical guidance for dataset curation, while the demographic performance gaps (race, age) underscore the need for more diverse training data before clinical deployment.
