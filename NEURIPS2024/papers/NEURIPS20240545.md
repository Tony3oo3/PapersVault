## Problem

Existing portfolio optimization methods either use l1-regularization that cannot enforce an exact number of active assets, or optimize the Sharpe ratio without sparsity constraints. No prior method can directly maximize the original Sharpe ratio under a hard m-sparse (l0) constraint together with self-financing and long-only constraints, due to the nonconvexity and combinatorial complexity of the l0 constraint. This paper is the first to provide a theoretically guaranteed globally optimal solution to this m-sparse Sharpe ratio maximization problem.

## Input / Output

The method takes a T×N sample asset return matrix (monthly excess returns from Kenneth R. French's data library), a sparsity level m (e.g., 10, 15, 20), and a small regularization parameter ε (set to 10⁻³) to ensure positive definiteness of the covariance estimate. It produces a portfolio weight vector w ∈ R^N with at most m nonzero components that satisfies w ≥ 0 and w^T 1_N = 1, along with the corresponding optimized Sharpe ratio. In practice, the algorithm is applied in a rolling-window fashion where the window size T (60 or 120 months) determines how many historical observations feed into each portfolio update.

## Method

The core insight is converting the m-sparse fractional optimization (maximizing p^T w / √(w^T Q_ε w)) into an equivalent m-sparse quadratic programming (minimizing ½v^T Q_ε v − p^T v over v ≥ 0 with ‖v‖₀ ≤ m), which is then cast as a two-term unconstrained problem f(v) + ι_Ω(v). Because the objective is semi-algebraic, the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property guarantees convergence of a Proximal Gradient Algorithm whose proximal step has a closed form: keep the top-m positive components and zero out the rest. The paper proves that under the EPER condition (existence of a feasible portfolio with positive expected return), the algorithm converges to a globally optimal solution when the active set size m_{v*} < m, or at least a locally optimal solution otherwise, with convergence rates O(1/√k) for portfolio weights and O(1/k) for the objective gap.

## Results

On six Kenneth R. French monthly benchmark datasets (FF25, FF25EU, FF32, FF49, FF100, FF100MEINV), mSSRM-PGA (m=10) achieves the highest Sharpe ratio on all six datasets at T=60 and on five of six at T=120. The headline gain is on FF25EU where mSSRM-PGA reaches SR=0.2712 versus 0.1574 for the 1/N baseline (a 72% improvement) and 0.2708 for PLCT. In terms of cumulative wealth (T=60, m=10), mSSRM-PGA attains the best final wealth on 4 of 6 datasets, e.g. 285.02 on FF49 versus 238.27 for PLCT (~20% higher) and 991.89 on FF32 versus 918.62 for PLCT. The method also maintains competitive performance under proportional transaction costs up to 0.5% and produces portfolios with average sparsity of only 5–11 assets regardless of the preset m (10/15/20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

mSSRM-PGA outperforms all nine state-of-the-art competitors and the 1/N baseline on Sharpe ratio across all six datasets at T=60 (and five of six at T=120). Against the strongest competitor PLCT [29], mSSRM-PGA wins on FF25 (0.2481 vs 0.2475), FF25EU (0.2712 vs 0.2708), FF32 (0.2612 vs 0.2600), FF49 (0.2151 vs 0.2119), and FF100 (0.2290 vs 0.2270), but is marginally lower on FF100MEINV (0.2217 vs 0.2220). Against the naive 1/N diversification strategy [14], the SR improvement ranges from 9% (FF100) to 72% (FF25EU), demonstrating that sparse active selection substantially beats equal-weight diversification. The mean-variance-based methods (SSMP [10], MAXER [2]) and EGR-based methods (SSPO [24], S1/S2/S3 [28], SPOLC [22]) are consistently outperformed by 5–20 SR points, with the largest gaps on FF25EU and FF100 where these methods struggle with high-dimensional covariance estimation. IPSRM-D [18], which modifies the SR objective with upside/downside risk terms, is also dominated by mSSRM-PGA on all six datasets, supporting the paper's argument that the original SR formulation is preferable. In cumulative wealth (Table 2), mSSRM-PGA (m=10) achieves the best final wealth on FF25, FF32, FF49, and FF100 at T=60, with a ~20% advantage over PLCT on FF49 (285.02 vs 238.27), while remaining second-best on the two datasets where it does not lead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the proximal gradient algorithm framework (Parikh & Boyd, 2014) and the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz convergence theory (Attouch et al., 2010) to tackle a problem that prior portfolio optimization work has left unsolved: direct Sharpe ratio maximization under an exact ℓ₀ (m-sparse) constraint. It departs from earlier SR optimization methods (Pang's PLCT, which requires μ_i > 0; Hung et al.'s IPSRM-D, which modifies the SR definition) and from sparse portfolio methods (SSMP, MAXER, SSPO) that rely on ℓ₁ regularization rather than exact sparsity. The key conceptual move is converting the m-sparse fractional optimization into an equivalent m-sparse quadratic programming, whose semi-algebraic structure then permits KL-based convergence analysis — a combination not previously applied to this problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to provide a theoretically-sound guarantee of global optimality for m-sparse Sharpe ratio maximization, filling a gap where prior approaches either lacked sparsity control or lacked convergence guarantees for the nonconvex problem. The practical impact is significant for fund managers who need sparse portfolios to reduce transaction and monitoring costs while maintaining competitive risk-adjusted returns. A stated limitation is that the framework cannot directly extend to fractional optimization models with nondifferentiable numerators or denominators, and the EPER condition (existence of a portfolio with positive expected return) is required for the strongest guarantees. Open questions include whether the KL-based approach generalizes to other nonconvex sparse optimization problems in machine learning and whether the method's performance holds under more realistic market microstructure assumptions.
