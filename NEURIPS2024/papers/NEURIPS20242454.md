## Problem

Causal effect estimation from observational data requires that outcomes, treatments, and covariates be manually defined, recorded, and tabulated before any computational analysis can proceed, making studies expensive and slow. Unstructured text data (e.g., patient forum posts, social media) contains rich causal information in natural language form but has no established pipeline for converting it into causal effect estimates. The paper asks how large language models can automate this end-to-end process, turning raw text reports into average treatment effect (ATE) estimates under standard causal assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a collection of unstructured natural language reports (e.g., Reddit posts) and a study design specification that defines the binary treatments, binary outcome, discrete covariates, and inclusion criteria. It produces a scalar ATE estimate (in percentage points) along with the intermediate conditional distributions P(T=t, Y=y | R_i, X_i) and propensity scores. The pipeline is entirely zero-shot: no task-specific training data or labeled examples are required, and the LLM is used purely to approximate conditional distributions via prompting and log-probability scoring over enumerated answer options.

## Method

NATURAL derives a family of consistent ATE estimators by substituting LLM-approximated conditionals P(X,T,Y|R) into classical causal formulas (IPW and outcome imputation), requiring only two additional assumptions beyond standard ignorability and positivity: that reports are jointly distributed with (X,T,Y), and that the true conditional over (X,T,Y) given R is accessible. The practical pipeline first filters raw posts through deterministic rules and LLM-based relevance/treatment-outcome/inclusion-criteria checks, then uses GPT-4 Turbo to extract and impute covariates (guessing unknown values conditioned on inclusion criteria), and finally uses LLAMA2-70B log-probabilities to score all (T,Y) combinations given each report and its covariates, yielding P(T,Y|R,X). The resulting conditionals are plugged into NATURAL IPW (sampling X, computing P(T,Y|R,X)) or NATURAL OI (sampling X,T, computing P(Y|R,X,T)) estimators, with reports weighted by P(X∈I|R) to handle inclusion criteria. The key insight is that LLMs, trained on vast real-world text, can serve as parametric approximators of the conditional distributions that classical causal estimators require, eliminating the need for structured tabular data.

## Results

On four real-world clinical datasets (Semaglutide vs. Tirzepatide, Semaglutide vs. Liraglutide, Erenumab vs. Topiramate, OnabotulinumtoxinA vs. Topiramate), the best-performing NATURAL estimator falls within 3 percentage points of the ground truth ATE from corresponding Phase 3/4 randomized clinical trials (e.g., N-IPW: 9.06% vs. GT 10.11% for Semaglutide vs. Tirzepatide; N-OI: 29.05% vs. GT 28.3% for Erenumab vs. Topiramate). On semi-synthetic datasets, NATURAL IPW achieves the lowest RMSE among all NATURAL variants (1.32 on Hillstrom, 1.39 on Retail Hero), outperforming trained Bag-of-Words (2.23, 2.42) and Sentence Encoder (6.09, 2.10) baselines, though the structured-data oracle IPW remains superior (0.39, 0.30). The uncorrected (no causal adjustment) baseline performs poorly, with RMSE of 43.67 on Semaglutide vs. Tirzepatide, confirming the necessity of confounding correction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On semi-synthetic data (Table 1), NATURAL IPW outperforms both trained baselines: Bag-of-Words (RMSE 2.23 on Hillstrom, 2.42 on Retail Hero) and Sentence Encoder (RMSE 6.09, 2.10), despite requiring no labeled training data. The Sentence Encoder baseline collapses to a zero ATE on Hillstrom, having learned a constant outcome predictor. The structured-data oracle IPW (RMSE 0.39, 0.30) remains the upper bound, as expected with full tabular access. On real-world clinical data, the comparison is against ground truth RCT ATEs rather than competing methods: N-IPW achieves the best RMSE on three of four settings (1.26, 2.68, 2.57), while N-OI wins on Semaglutide vs. Liraglutide (RMSE 2.15). The uncorrected baseline (no propensity weighting or imputation) is dramatically worse, with RMSE up to 68.87, confirming that the LLM-extracted conditionals and classical causal estimators are both essential. The selection-biased N-IPW variant (RMSE 9.58 on Hillstrom) demonstrates that systematic reporting bias degrades performance, though the method still recovers reasonable estimates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes LLMs as parametric approximators of conditional distributions P(X,T,Y|R) to plug into classical causal estimators (IPW, OI), rather than using them as causal reasoners or text feature extractors. It departs from prior text-for-causality work (which uses text to measure confounders, outcomes, or produce interpretable features) by requiring no task-specific training data and by treating the LLM purely as a conditional distribution oracle over structured variables. It also distinguishes itself from LLM-as-causal-discovery approaches (querying models for causal statements) by grounding estimation in the Neyman-Rubin framework with explicit ignorability and positivity assumptions. The theoretical contribution derives consistent text-conditioned versions of IPW and OI under two new data-access assumptions (Assumptions 3–4), bridging classical causal inference with LLM-based extraction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: NATURAL reduces ATE estimation from years and tens of millions of dollars (Phase 3/4 clinical trials) to days and a few hundred dollars of compute, while achieving estimates within 3 percentage points of ground-trial ATEs on four real clinical comparisons. Key limitations include dependence on LLM calibration (which post-training can compromise), selection bias from self-reporting, network interference in social data, and the requirement that outcomes be self-reportable. Open questions include extending to real-valued variables, weakening the strong Assumption 4, automatic prompt tuning, and validating the pipeline across more diverse study designs and domains beyond medicine.
