## Problem

In mixed-motive multi-agent games, agents must simultaneously cooperate altruistically with some co-players and protect themselves from exploitation by others, yet existing approaches either require centralized training (impractical for self-interested agents) or converge to suboptimal Nash equilibria like mutual defection. The paper identifies a missing computational model of empathy—a mechanism that modulates an agent's response based on inferred social relationships—and shows that no prior MARL algorithm explicitly decomposes co-players' value contributions to guide reward-sharing decisions.

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a local observation as a multi-channel binary tensor (e.g., 7 channels in Cleanup encoding agent positions, waste, apples, and a boundary mask) plus the joint action vector of all agents and a one-hot agent ID. The agent outputs a discrete action policy (e.g., up/down/left/right/stay/clean/pick), a gifting weight vector over co-players (continuous values in [0,1] summing to 1, where w^ij is the fraction of agent i's reward gifted to j), and a social relationship estimate per co-player. The gifting is strictly zero-sum: the total group reward is conserved, and the agent retains w^ii = 1 − Σ_{j≠i} w^ij for itself.

## Method

LASE consists of two modules: Social Relationship Inference (SRI) and Gifting. SRI computes each co-player's social relationship via counterfactual reasoning: it compares the SR value network's Q-value of the current joint action against a counterfactual baseline that marginalizes the co-player's action (weighted by the co-player's inferred policy), normalized by the Q-value range. To handle partial observability, a perspective-taking module simulates the co-player's observation from the focal agent's local observation plus the co-player's ID (via a convolutional observation conversion network) and predicts the co-player's action distribution (via an SR policy network), both trained within an actor-critic framework using TD-error on individual extrinsic rewards. The Gifting module directly uses the computed social relationship as the zero-sum gifting weight, so the agent's total reward (extrinsic plus received gifts) becomes the optimization target for the main policy. The observation conversion network is trained with a combined loss balancing cross-entropy on predicted co-player actions and L1 reconstruction of the simulated observation, with a hyperparameter δ controlling the trade-off.

## Results

In IPD, LASE escapes the (D,D) Nash equilibrium and converges to ~0.93 cooperation probability with high collective reward, outperforming A2C (which converges to full defection) and matching LOLA's collective reward with faster convergence. In the four sequential social dilemmas (SSH, SSG, Coingame, Cleanup), LASE nearly reaches the group-optimal upper bound (total reward 20 in SSH, 120 in SSG) while all baselines fall short. In the extended 8-agent environments, LASE achieves 56.5 total reward in Cleanup.Extn (vs. 20.8 for IA, 1.3 for LIO, 0.1 for A2C) and 232.6 in SSG.Extn (vs. 227.8 for IA, 20.3 for LIO, 135.0 for A2C). On fairness, LASE achieves an Equality score of 0.802 in Cleanup versus 0.496 for GO, demonstrating that gifting-based reward redistribution narrows the gap between cleaners and collectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LASE outperforms all five baselines across the four standard SSDs and both extended environments. A2C (independent actor-critic) converges to the defection equilibrium in IPD and yields near-zero collective reward in Coingame and Cleanup, confirming that pure self-interest fails in mixed-motive settings. LIO (Learning to Incentivize Others) struggles in all four environments and requires manually specified gifting hyperparameters, while LASE's empathy-driven gifting adapts automatically. IA (Inequity Aversion) and SI (Social Influence) improve over A2C but still fall well below LASE in Cleanup (20.8 and 3.5 vs. 56.5 in the extended version) and SSG. LOLA achieves comparable collective reward to LASE in IPD but with slower convergence and less stability. GO (Group Optimal) reaches the theoretical upper bound in SSG, Coingame, and Cleanup but suffers from the lazy problem in SSH (delaying hunting to the last steps) and sacrifices individual fairness (Equality 0.496 vs. LASE's 0.802 in Cleanup), as it forces specific agents to bear all cleaning costs without compensation.

## Relation to Prior Work

LASE builds on the zero-sum gifting mechanism of Lupu & Precup (2020), which allows agents to redistribute their own rewards to co-players, and on the Self-Other Modeling (SOM) paradigm of Raileanu et al. (2018) for inferring others' policies. The key departure is the introduction of a computational empathy model: social relationships are inferred via counterfactual reasoning (comparing joint-action Q-values against a baseline that marginalizes each co-player's action), and these inferred relationships directly modulate gifting weights. Unlike prior gifting methods (LIO, LToS) that learn gifting weights end-to-end or use handcrafted schemes, LASE ties gifting to an explicit, interpretable measure of each co-player's contribution, and unlike COMA-style counterfactual methods that assess one's own influence on the team, LASE assesses others' influence on the focal agent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LASE provides a principled, decentralized mechanism for agents to balance altruism and self-interest in mixed-motive games without requiring centralized coordination or access to others' reward functions, which is a practical constraint in realistic multi-agent deployments. The fairness results (Equality ≈ 0.80 vs. GO's ≈ 0.50 in Cleanup) demonstrate that empathy-based gifting can achieve near-optimal collective reward while maintaining intra-group equity, addressing a gap where group-optimal methods sacrifice individual interests. Open questions include extending to continuous action spaces, enabling agents to refuse gifts, and reducing the uncertainty in social-relationship estimates under partial observability, all of which the authors identify as future work.
