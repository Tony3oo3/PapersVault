## Problem

Existing VQA benchmarks are predominantly English-only and Western-centric in both language and imagery. While a few multilingual VQA datasets exist, they typically translate questions from the same Western-centric images or cover only a handful of high-resource languages, leaving low-resource languages and non-Western cultural contexts largely unrepresented. This makes it impossible to assess whether multimodal models genuinely understand diverse cultures or merely recognize Western visual scenes.

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a culturally specific RGB image with a text question and four answer options (one correct, three plausible distractors), presented in both the local language and a parallel English translation. Evaluation uses two prompt variants—location-aware (specifying the country) and location-agnostic—and two language settings (English vs. local). For generative models (LLaVA, mBLIP, InstructBLIP), the model's output token probabilities over A/B/C/D determine the prediction; for embedding models (CLIP, M-CLIP), the prediction is the option whose concatenated question+option text has the highest cosine similarity to the image embedding. An open-ended variant (no options shown) is also evaluated by selecting the option whose full answer phrase the model assigns the highest generation probability.

## Method

CVQA is constructed through a grassroots, cross-community crowd-sourcing effort involving native speakers and cultural experts across 30 countries, 31 languages, and 39 country-language pairs, yielding 10,374 multiple-choice questions over 5,239 images. Annotators select culturally relevant images (personal photos preferred, supplemented by open-license web sources), write 1–3 questions per image that require the image to answer, and provide three plausible distractors; each entry is then validated by a second annotator from the same country-language pair. Questions span 10 categories (from Vehicles to Pop Culture) and are written in the local language with manual English translation. The benchmark is then used to zero-shot evaluate eight MLLMs—six open-source (CLIP, M-CLIP, LLaVA-1.5-7B, mBLIP-mT0, mBLIP-BLOOMZ, InstructBLIP) and two closed-source (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Flash)—across English and local-language prompts, with ablations by category, country-language pair, image source, and prompt type.

## Results

On CVQA, GPT-4o achieves the highest accuracy at 75.4% (English) and 74.3% (local language), followed by Gemini-1.5-Flash at 66.9% and 68.5% respectively. Among open-source models, LLaVA-1.5-7B performs best at 49.6% (EN) and 35.5% (LOC), still trailing closed models by over 10 percentage points. A notable finding is the substantial performance degradation when questions are posed in local languages: open models drop by 10–18 points (e.g., LLaVA-1.5-7B drops from 49.6% to 35.5%), while closed models show minimal degradation (GPT-4o drops only 1.1 points). In an open-ended setting without multiple-choice options, LLaVA-1.5-7B's accuracy further drops to just 30%, highlighting the brittleness of current models on culturally specific knowledge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o dominates all other models, achieving 75.4% (EN) and 74.3% (LOC), outperforming the best open model LLaVA-1.5-7B by approximately 26 points in English and 39 points in local languages. Gemini-1.5-Flash is the second-best model (66.9% EN, 68.5% LOC) and notably shows a slight *improvement* in local language over English, unlike all open models. Among open-source models, LLaVA-1.5-7B (49.6% EN) and InstructBLIP (49.0% EN) are the strongest, but both suffer large drops in local languages (35.5% and 31.9% respectively). CLIP (42.7% EN) and mBLIP-BLOOMZ (39.3% EN) perform moderately, while M-CLIP (38.0% EN) and mBLIP-mT0 (31.3% EN) are the weakest, with mBLIP-mT0 showing almost no difference between English and local language (31.3% vs. 30.9%), suggesting it fails to leverage its multilingual training. The consistent pattern across all open models is a 10–18 point accuracy drop when switching from English to local-language prompts, whereas closed models remain largely unaffected, indicating a significant gap in multilingual cultural understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

CVQA extends the VQA benchmarking lineage (VQA, VQA-2, GQA, OK-VQA) by introducing culturally-native images and questions rather than translating Western-centric content into other languages. It departs from prior multilingual VQA efforts (xGQA, MaXM, MTVQA, MaRVL) that typically keep images fixed and only vary the linguistic surface, instead grounding both the visual and textual content in 30 distinct cultural contexts. The data collection methodology follows the grassroots crowd-sourcing paradigm of Masakhane, NusaCrowd, and AI4Bharat, but scales it across communities rather than within a single linguistic group, and adopts the common-ground-knowledge-as-culture proxy from Adilazuarda et al.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CVQA provides the first large-scale (10k questions, 31 languages, 39 country-language pairs) benchmark specifically designed to probe cultural and linguistic bias in MLLMs, filling a clear gap where prior evaluations were English-dominant or translation-based. The consistent finding that all open models drop below 50% accuracy and degrade further in local languages and open-ended settings highlights a substantial, measurable gap in current model capabilities that is unlikely to close without targeted data and training. Open questions include whether city-level or demographic-level cultural granularity would reveal finer-grained biases, whether the benchmark can eventually support training (not just evaluation), and how to move beyond the common-ground-knowledge proxy toward richer cultural modeling.
