## Problem

Current vision-language models struggle to integrate subtle visual cues with commonsense and world knowledge to interpret complex visual scenarios. Existing benchmarks like VisIT-Bench and Encyclopedic VQA rely on pre-existing images, risking overlap with pre-training data and limiting the creative range of scenarios tested. This paper asks whether models can detect nuanced visual clues and combine them with cultural, physical, or social knowledge to solve riddles that humans find straightforward.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244427/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each riddle presents a synthetic image (generated by text-to-image models such as Midjourney, DALL-E 3, or SDXL) containing deliberately embedded subtle visual clues, paired with a text question and optionally a concise textual hint directing attention to specific image regions or a full webpage attribution providing external world knowledge. The three tasks produce different output formats: free-text answers with justification (open-ended VQA), a single selection from five candidate answers including a 'cannot determine' option (multiple-choice), and a binary yes/no correctness judgment on a candidate answer (automatic evaluation, in both reference-free and reference-based variants).

## Method

The benchmark is constructed by inverting the typical image-to-question pipeline: seven designers first devise a scenario with embedded visual clues, then generate a matching image using text-to-image models, and finally formulate a question whose answer requires detecting the clue and applying commonsense or world knowledge. Each of the 400 riddles is peer-reviewed by at least three other designers for solvability and hint clarity, and is categorized into one of 16 knowledge domains (cultural principles, safety, biological knowledge, etc.) with a difficulty index from 0 to 3. The evaluation protocol uses human raters via Amazon Mechanical Turk (Krippendorff's alpha = 0.79) to score open-ended answers, and the best-performing model (Gemini-Pro-1.5 at 87% agreement with humans) serves as an automatic reference-based judge for scalable leaderboard scoring. A key finding is a 27% 'visual recognition gap' (models miss critical image details in captions) and a 32% 'reasoning gap' (models fail to reason correctly even given perfect captions), with the top model achieving only 40% versus 82% human accuracy.

## Results

On the primary open-ended VQA task, the best-performing model (Gemini-Pro-1.5) achieves only 40% human-rated accuracy, compared to 82% for human annotators, revealing a substantial commonsense reasoning gap. In the multiple-choice variant, GPT4o leads at 55% overall accuracy (rising to 83% with hints), while GPT-4 reaches 45% (82% with attributions). For automatic evaluation of open-ended answers, Gemini-Pro-1.5 as a reference-based auto-rater agrees with human judgment 87% of the time, making it a viable scalable evaluator. The Caption→LLM ablation decomposes the gap into a 27% visual recognition gap and a 32% reasoning gap, showing that even oracle human captions only lift Gemini-Pro-1.5 to 50%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-Pro-1.5 is the strongest open-ended solver at 40%, outperforming GPT-4 (34%) and Gemini-Pro-Vision (30%), while open-weight models (LLaVA-1.6-34B at 15%, LLaVA-1.5-7B at 13%, InstructBLIP at 13%) trail by more than 25 points. In the multiple-choice setting, GPT4o (55%) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (46%) surpass the original submission models, with GPT-4 at 45% and Gemini-Pro-Vision at 41%; all models remain well below the 82% human ceiling. With auxiliary hints, GPT4o jumps to 83% and GPT-4 to 69%, whereas Gemini-Pro-1.5 reaches 66% with hints and 72% with attributions, showing that auxiliary information narrows but does not close the human gap. For auto-evaluation, Gemini-Pro-1.5 (87%) and GPT-4 (86%) are the top reference-based judges, while reference-free performance drops sharply (52% and 51% respectively), indicating that models struggle to judge answers without a ground-truth reference. The ablation with modified images (Table 5) further reveals that Gemini-Pro-1.5 shows the largest sensitivity to visual cue removal (60-point gap), suggesting it relies more on visual evidence than text priors, whereas LLaVA-1.5-7B shows the smallest gap (16 points), implying weaker visual grounding overall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior VQA benchmarks (VisIT-Bench, Encyclopedic VQA, OK-VQA) that formulate questions over pre-existing natural images, instead adopting a scenario-first design where riddles are conceived as embedded-clue narratives and then rendered into synthetic images via text-to-image models. This inversion avoids pre-training contamination and broadens the creative scope of testable scenarios. The benchmark also introduces structured auxiliary inputs (textual hints and webpage attributions) as first-class evaluation axes, a design choice not present in prior commonsense VQA benchmarks, and connects to the factuality-evaluation literature by proposing reference-free and reference-based auto-rater tasks for open-ended answers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 42-point gap between the best model (Gemini-Pro-1.5 at 40%) and humans (82%) on a 400-item benchmark provides a concrete, reproducible measure of how far current VLMs are from human-level commonsense visual reasoning, and the decomposition into a 27% visual-recognition gap and a 32% reasoning gap gives practitioners a clearer diagnostic. The finding that attributions (external knowledge) improve multiple-choice accuracy to 82% yet degrade open-ended accuracy to 29% highlights an unresolved tension in how models integrate retrieved context. Limitations include the small dataset size, reliance on synthetic images that may not transfer to natural photography, and the fact that all riddles were designed by seven individuals from a single country, raising cultural-bias concerns. Open questions include whether automated riddle generation can scale the benchmark, whether a dedicated training set derived from riddles improves model performance, and whether the auto-rater (Gemini-Pro-1.5 at 87% agreement) is robust enough for leaderboard use without human oversight.
