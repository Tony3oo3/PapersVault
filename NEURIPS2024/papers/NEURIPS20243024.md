## Problem

Generative adversarial imitation learning (GAIL) is notoriously brittle and unstable because its discriminator produces reward signals that overfit to expert demonstrations and fail to provide useful guidance when the agent deviates from expert behavior. The paper asks whether integrating a diffusion model into the GAIL discriminator can yield more robust, smoother, and better-generalizing reward functions for policy learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243024/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes state-action pairs where states range from low-dimensional vectors (6D in MAZE, 17D in WALKER) to high-dimensional vectors (132D in ANTREACH, 68D in HANDROTATE) or 96×96 RGB images (CARRACING, preprocessed to 64×64 grayscale with two consecutive frames concatenated). The diffusion discriminator additionally takes a binary condition label (real c⁺=1 or fake c⁻=0) concatenated to the noisy state-action input. The policy outputs continuous actions of varying dimensionality (2D to 20D), and the discriminator outputs a scalar realness probability in [0,1] computed via a single random denoising step rather than the full T-step generation process.

## Method

DRAIL replaces the standard MLP discriminator in GAIL with a conditional diffusion model (U-Net with 3 down/up-sampling blocks) that is trained to denoise state-action pairs under two conditions: real (c⁺) and fake (c⁻). For any state-action pair, the classifier computes D_φ(s,a) = σ(L_diff(s,a,c⁻) − L_diff(s,a,c⁺)), where L_diff is the single-step denoising loss, yielding a bounded [0,1] realness score. The policy is trained with PPO using the logit reward r_φ = log(D_φ) − log(1−D_φ), following the adversarial IRL objective of Fu et al. The key computational insight is that a single randomly sampled denoising step (rather than T=1000 steps) suffices to approximate the diffusion loss, making per-step reward computation feasible for online RL at billion-sample scale. Unlike DiffAIL's unconditional diffusion model, the conditional formulation explicitly compares expert and agent likelihoods, providing a relative boundary that adapts as the agent improves, leading to more stable adversarial training.

## Results

On the six main environments, DRAIL achieves 96.83% success rate on MAZE, 95.78% on FETCHPUSH, 93.88% on HANDROTATE, 39.02% on ANTREACH, 5381 return on WALKER, and 746.35 return on CARRACING. DRAIL outperforms the closest competitor DiffAIL on 5 out of 6 tasks (e.g., MAZE: 96.83% vs. 94.06%; CARRACING: 746.35 vs. 689.62) and substantially outperforms GAIL and WAIL across all environments. In generalization experiments at 2.0× noise, DRAIL maintains 87.22% on FETCHPICK and 99.09% on HANDROTATE, while GAIL drops to 39.17% and 16.89% respectively. In data efficiency tests, DRAIL retains >80% success on FETCHPUSH even with 90% less expert data, whereas other AIL baselines fall below 50%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BC, DRAIL wins on 5 of 6 tasks (e.g., MAZE: 96.83% vs. 61.60%; CARRACING: 746.35 vs. 534.92), with BC only edging ahead on WALKER (5431 vs. 5381), which the authors attribute to WALKER being a monotonic task requiring less generalization. Against Diffusion Policy, DRAIL consistently outperforms on all tasks except HANDROTATE where they are nearly tied (93.88% vs. 93.24%), with the largest gap on MAZE (96.83% vs. 68.80%). Against GAIL and WAIL, DRAIL achieves dramatic improvements across all environments—GAIL and WAIL suffer from high variance and frequent training failures (e.g., GAIL: 33.73%±37.63% on FETCHPUSH; WAIL: −72.39 return on CARRACING). Against the closest competitor DiffAIL, DRAIL wins on 5 of 6 tasks (MAZE: 96.83% vs. 94.06%; ANTREACH: 39.02% vs. 29.59%; CARRACING: 746.35 vs. 689.62), losing only on HANDROTATE (93.88% vs. 97.49%), and the gap widens substantially under generalization stress (at 2.0× noise on FETCHPICK: DRAIL 87.22% vs. DiffAIL 0.00%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016) by replacing its standard MLP discriminator with a conditional diffusion-based discriminative classifier, drawing on DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) for the diffusion architecture and the ELBO-as-classifier insight from Li et al. (2023). The closest prior work, DiffAIL (Wang et al., 2024), also embeds a diffusion model in AIL but uses an unconditional model whose single denoising loss only implicitly encodes 'realness'; DRAIL departs by conditioning on explicit real/fake labels, yielding a proper two-class probability that aligns with GAIL's binary cross-entropy objective. The reward signal follows the adversarial IRL formulation of Fu et al. (2018), and policy optimization uses PPO, keeping the overall AIL training loop unchanged.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GAIL's brittleness and unstable training have been a persistent practical barrier in adversarial imitation learning; DRAIL's diffusion discriminator produces measurably smoother and more robust reward landscapes (visualized in the SINE experiment), which translates into better generalization to unseen states/goals and superior data efficiency (maintaining >80% success with 90% less expert data in FETCHPUSH). The approach is limited to learning-from-demonstration settings requiring paired state-action sequences, assumes expert optimality, and incurs additional compute from the diffusion model. Open questions include whether the conditional-diffusion discriminator extends to learning-from-observation, whether other divergences (Wasserstein, f-divergences) further improve stability, and whether the technique transfers to real-world robotic deployment with image-based observations.
