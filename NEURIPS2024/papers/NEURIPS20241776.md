## Problem

Online classification guarantees are typically worst-case, characterized by the Littlestone dimension, which makes even simple classes like 1D thresholds unlearnable. However, real-world example streams are often predictable, and the accuracy benefits of machine-learned predictions for online classification were previously unknown. This paper asks: can black-box access to a Predictor that forecasts future examples yield online learning guarantees that gracefully improve with prediction quality while never worsening the worst-case bound?

## Input / Output

At each round t, the learner receives the current example x_t, the full history of labeled examples (x_1,y_1),...,(x_{t-1},y_{t-1}), and a distribution over the entire future sequence x_{t+1:T} from an adaptive Predictor that updates its predictions as new examples are revealed. The learner outputs a (potentially randomized) label ŷ_t and suffers 0-1 loss. The theoretical output is an expected mistake/regret bound that is the minimum of three terms: the Littlestone-dimension worst-case bound, a product of Predictor mistakes and offline-learner mistakes, and a refined partition-based bound, all interpolating between offline-optimal and worst-case rates.

## Method

The core mechanism reduces online classification with predictions to transductive online learning: whenever the Predictor makes a mistake, the learner initializes a fresh copy of a no-regret offline learner B using the Predictor's predicted future sequence as its 'known' example sequence, then delegates all subsequent predictions to B until the next Predictor error. To handle cases where the Predictor's mistakes are evenly spaced (making each offline sub-problem long), a family of experts partitions the stream into c+1 roughly equal segments, each running this restart strategy on a shorter horizon, and the Deterministic Weighted Majority Algorithm (DWMA) combines them. The final learner runs DWMA over three experts—the Standard Optimal Algorithm (achieving L(H) mistakes), the single-restart learner, and the partitioned expert ensemble—yielding a mistake bound of 3(L(H) ∧ (M_P+1)·M_B(T,H) ∧ 6((M_P+1)·M̄_B(T/(M_P+1)+1,H) + log T)) + 5. A matching lower bound via a reduction to Online Classification with Peeks shows the product dependence on M_P and M_B is tight up to log factors.

## Results

The main quantitative result (Theorem 3.1) shows that the expected mistake bound of the proposed learner is at most 3(L(H) ∧ (M_P(x_{1:T})+1)·M_B(T,H) ∧ 6((M_P(x_{1:T})+1)·M̄_B(T/(M_P(x_{1:T})+1)+1, H) + log₂T)) + 5, where L(H) is the Littlestone dimension, M_P is the Predictor's expected mistakes, and M_B is the offline learner's mistake bound. When the Predictor is perfect (M_P = 0), the bound reduces to the offline learner's rate (e.g., O(VC(H)·log₂T) for binary classes), and when predictions are poor, it gracefully degrades to the worst-case O(L(H)) bound. A matching lower bound (Theorem 3.8) shows the product dependence on M_P and M_B is tight up to an additive log₂T factor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or datasets. The comparisons are between theoretical guarantees: (1) the worst-case online learning bound of O(L(H)) mistakes (via the Standard Optimal Algorithm), (2) the offline/transductive learning bound of O(VC(H)·log₂T) for binary classes, and (3) the prediction-augmented bound that interpolates between the two. The proposed learner never exceeds the worst-case bound (robustness), matches the offline rate when predictions are perfect (consistency), and smoothly interpolates as a function of prediction quality (smoothness). Corollary 3.2 establishes that offline learnability (VC-dimension finiteness) suffices for online learnability under predictable examples, analogous to smoothed online classification results by Haghtalab et al. [2020] and Block et al. [2022], but under a different model where the adversary is constrained to predictable streams rather than anti-concentrated distributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of research: Algorithms with Predictions (AwP), where classical online algorithms are augmented with machine-learned predictions, and online classification, whose learnability is characterized by the Littlestone dimension. The key technical building block is transductive online learning (Ben-David et al. 1997; Hanneke et al. 2024), where the learner sees all examples before the game begins and whose minimax rates are governed by the VC dimension. The paper's proof strategy reduces online classification with predictions to transductive online learning by restarting an offline learner each time the Predictor errs, then combining multiple such learners via the Deterministic Weighted Majority Algorithm. This departs from prior AwP work (e.g., Elias et al. 2024) by assuming black-box access to an adaptive Predictor rather than hand-crafted predictions, and from smoothed online classification (Haghtalab 2018; Block et al. 2022) by replacing distributional assumptions on the adversary with a predictability condition on the example stream.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that offline (transductive) learnability, characterized by the VC dimension, is sufficient for online learnability when the example stream is predictable—provides a clean, beyond-worst-case characterization that parallels the smoothed online classification literature but under a different and arguably more natural assumption. The tightness of the product structure M_P(x_{1:T}) · M_B(T,H) (shown via a lower bound) rules out the hope of a sum-type bound and clarifies the fundamental tradeoff between predictor quality and offline learnability. Open questions include extending the framework to online regression, handling continuous example spaces with ℓ_p prediction losses rather than 0-1 mistakes, and understanding whether the adaptive Predictor assumption is essential or whether static predictions suffice. The work is purely theoretical with no empirical validation, so its practical impact depends on whether real-world prediction systems can be cast as the kind of consistent, lazy Predictor the theory requires.
