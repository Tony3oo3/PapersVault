## Problem

Existing LLM bias evaluation metrics (e.g., CrowS-Pairs) measure only the average stereotype frequency in fixed contexts, ignoring the generation inconsistency that causes LLMs to display contradictory stereotypes for the same profession across varied contexts. This oversight leads to misleading alignment evaluations and prevents accurate quantification of the true risk that LLM applications pose in perpetuating or amplifying social stereotypes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243494/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes an LLM, a set of context templates (sentence skeletons mined from 10,000 Wikipedia articles, weighted by their corpus frequency), occupation words as evidence X, and attribute word lists (e.g., 40+ male/female terms for gender) as Y. It produces a scalar overall discrimination risk R decomposed into bias risk R^b (from the mean of the stereotype distribution) and volatility risk R^v (from its variation), along with per-occupation risk vectors r_x, r_x^b, r_x^v for fine-grained attribution. The stereotype itself is a continuous random variable s_{y|x}^M(c) = p_{y|x}^M(c)/p_{y|x}^*(c) − 1, estimated non-parametrically across the context distribution.

## Method

The Bias-Volatility Framework (BVF) models an LLM's stereotyped behavior as a probability distribution over contexts, where each context c yields a stereotype value s_{y|x}^M(c) defined as the ratio of the model's conditional token probability to an unbiased reference probability minus one. Contexts are mined automatically from Wikipedia by extracting sentence skeletons where gender-specific words corefer with occupation subjects, then weighted by their empirical frequency to approximate real-world usage. The discrimination criterion J applies the l^∞ norm over the positive part of the stereotype vector, capturing the most extreme pro-group bias while discarding anti-stereotypes. The core mathematical contribution is the decomposition R = R^b + R^v, where R^b = E_x[J(E_c(s_{Y|x}^M(c)))] isolates systematic bias (the model's persistent prejudice) and R^v = R − R^b captures volatility (context-dependent inconsistency), enabling causal attribution of discrimination to either learned bias or generation randomness. Applied to 12 LLMs, the framework reveals that bias risk dominates overall discrimination, most models exhibit strong pro-male stereotypes across nearly all occupations, and RLHF alignment reduces bias risk but increases volatility risk.

## Results

Under the BVF framework, T5 exhibits the highest overall gender discrimination risk (R = 0.8703) and bias risk (R^b = 0.8691), approaching the theoretical Stereotyped Model bound of 1.0, while BERT achieves the lowest overall risk (R = 0.3049) and ALBERT the lowest bias risk (R^b = 0.2531) but the highest volatility risk (R^v = 0.0756). Across all 12 models, bias risk dominates over volatility risk (e.g., T5: R^b = 0.8691 vs. R^v = 0.0012), confirming that systematic learned bias is the primary driver of discrimination. RLHF alignment (Llama2 base → chat) consistently reduces bias risk but increases volatility risk, indicating that alignment corrects average prejudice without stabilizing context-dependent behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The BVF framework evaluates 12 LLMs spanning 11.8M (ALBERT) to 30B (OPT-IML) parameters. T5 (220M) and XLNet (117M) show the most severe pro-male stereotypes (R > 0.73), while BERT (110M) and ALBERT (11.8M) are comparatively fairer (R < 0.33), though ALBERT's lower bias risk is offset by the highest volatility risk (0.0756 vs. ~0.001–0.037 for others). The rankings of R and R^b are not perfectly aligned: ALBERT has the lowest R^b (0.2531) yet BERT has the lowest R (0.3049), demonstrating that ignoring volatility leads to misleading model comparisons. In the RLHF ablation, Llama2-chat models show lower bias risk than their base counterparts across 7B/13B/70B sizes, but higher volatility risk, revealing that RLHF reduces systematic prejudice without improving prediction consistency. Fine-tuning Llama2 on toxic data increases bias risk while decreasing volatility risk, confirming that harmful training data entrenches systematic stereotypes. Discrimination risk correlates positively with occupational salary (WLS regression), suggesting LLMs encode socio-economic gender stereotypes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two established lines of LLM bias measurement—word-embedding metrics (WEAT, cosine-similarity approaches) and probability-based metrics (CrowS-Pairs, StereoSet)—by identifying a shared blind spot: both capture only the mean stereotypical preference across contexts, ignoring the variation (volatility) of predictions as contexts shift. Rather than building on a single prior method, BVF repurposes the statistical decomposition of a random variable into mean and variance, applied to the LLM's stereotype distribution over mined context templates. The framework is positioned as a generalization of existing discrimination metrics, adding a volatility-risk component that prior work explicitly does not model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bias/volatility decomposition gives practitioners an actionable attribution: bias risk points to training-data or architecture fixes, while volatility risk flags unpredictable behavior that is harder to mitigate and more dangerous in high-stakes deployments (medical, legal, hiring). The finding that RLHF alignment reduces bias but increases volatility challenges the assumption that alignment uniformly improves model reliability, and the framework's generality (applicable to race, religion, disability, knowledge mastery, and multimodal settings) makes it a potentially reusable auditing tool. Open questions include how to practically reduce volatility risk, whether the uniform-occupation assumption distorts real-world risk estimates, and whether the Wikipedia-mined context distribution adequately represents deployment contexts.
