## Problem

Episodic future thinking—the ability to anticipate and simulate upcoming events—remains underexplored in multi-agent reinforcement learning, despite being critical for social decision-making. Existing model-based approaches require a known or learnable state transition model, which is intractable in multi-agent settings where transitions depend on the joint actions of heterogeneous agents. The paper addresses how an agent can proactively simulate future scenarios in a partially observable, character-heterogeneous multi-agent environment without explicitly learning a global dynamics model.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes its own partial observation (relative velocities, positions, and lane indices of neighboring vehicles in a 6-neighborhood), collected observation-action trajectories of target agents over a window of T timesteps, and its own 3-dimensional character vector (weights on velocity, safety-distance, and lane-change reward terms). It produces a hybrid action (continuous acceleration in [a_min, a_max] plus a discrete lane-change in {-1, 0, 1}), inferred character vectors for each target agent, and a simulated next observation generated by applying others' predicted actions while holding its own action as null. The character vector parameterizes the reward function, so different characters yield distinct behavioral policies over the same observation space.

## Method

The EFT mechanism comprises two modules: a multi-character policy and a character inference module. The multi-character policy is a TD3-based actor-critic network that takes (observation, character) as input and outputs a hybrid action, with a post-processor g(·) that quantizes the continuous proto-action into a discrete value via a floor-based mapping. The character inference module applies inverse rational control: it maximizes the log-likelihood of the target agent's observed trajectory over the character space using gradient ascent (Algorithm 2), reducing to summing log-probabilities of continuous and discrete actions under the inferred character. At decision time, the agent (1) predicts each neighbor's action via the multi-character policy conditioned on the inferred character, (2) simulates its next observation by applying all predicted neighbor actions while setting its own action to null, and (3) selects its action by querying the multi-character policy on the simulated observation. This null-action simulation isolates the effect of others' behaviors, allowing the agent to respond adaptively to the anticipated future state rather than reacting to the current one.

## Results

On the FLOW autonomous driving task (21 agents, hybrid action space), the proposed EFT mechanism achieves an average reward of 2899–3051 across 1–5 character groups, consistently outperforming all baselines at diversity levels n ≥ 2. The method's advantage grows with diversity: at n=5, it scores 3051 versus 2856 (MADDPG), 2763 (MAPPO), 2701 (Dreamer), and 2511 (ToMC2). On SMAC, the proposed method achieves 93.8% win rate on 6h_vs_8z (vs. 85.9% MAPPO, 68.0% MADDPG) and 84.3% on 3s5z_vs_3s6z (vs. 63.3% MAPPO, 18.9% MADDPG). On MPE, it achieves the best score in 2 of 3 tasks (Spread: −149.12, Adversary: 10.01).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed EFT mechanism outperforms all seven external baselines at diversity levels n ≥ 2 on the FLOW task, with the gap widening as character diversity increases. MADDPG is the closest competitor (within ~100 reward at n=2 and n=4) but still trails the proposed method at every level; MAPPO and Dreamer show progressive degradation with diversity (e.g., Dreamer drops from 2911 to 2701, while the proposed method stays above 2948). ToMC2 achieves the highest score at n=1 (3016 vs. 2899) but degrades sharply to 2511 at n=5, whereas the proposed method remains stable. Model-based RL baselines (MBPO, Dreamer) suffer the largest performance drops and highest variance (MBPO std up to ±948), confirming the difficulty of learning a global dynamics model in heterogeneous multi-agent settings. Q-MIX and LIAM underperform across all diversity levels, with Q-MIX limited by its discrete-action assumption and LIAM failing to scale beyond small agent counts. On SMAC, the proposed method wins 2 of 3 scenarios outright (84.3% on 3s5z_vs_3s6z vs. 82.8% Q-MIX; 93.8% on 6h_vs_8z vs. 85.9% MAPPO) and is competitive on 2s3z (98.8% vs. MAPPO's 100%). The FCE-EFT ablation (assuming all agents share the ego character) consistently underperforms the baseline at n ≥ 2, confirming that accurate character inference is essential for the EFT mechanism to be beneficial.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on inverse rational control (IRC) for character inference, extending it from single-agent settings to a multi-agent context where a pre-trained multi-character policy (actor-critic with hybrid action space) serves as the likelihood model. It departs from model-based RL approaches (Dreamer, MBPO) that require learning full state transition dynamics, arguing these are infeasible under agent heterogeneity and partial observability. It also extends Theory of Mind and agent-modeling work (ToMC2, LIAM) by handling continuous-plus-discrete hybrid action spaces and larger agent populations, while positioning episodic future thinking as a distinct and underexplored complement to the well-studied counterfactual thinking in MARL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The EFT mechanism offers a practical pathway for agents to make foresighted decisions in heterogeneous multi-agent settings without requiring a learned world model, which is a significant practical advantage over model-based RL. A key open question is scalability: experiments involve only one EFT agent among otherwise non-EFT agents, and policies are assumed stationary at execution time, leaving the multi-EFT-agent and non-stationary-policy settings unexplored. The finding that the False Consensus Effect (assuming others share your character) actively degrades performance below the no-EFT baseline underscores that character inference is not merely helpful but necessary for the mechanism to function, raising the question of how robust the approach is when inference accuracy drops below ~90% in real-world noisy observation settings.
