## Problem

Existing privacy amplification by subsampling guarantees are only tight in a mechanism-agnostic sense, meaning they cannot exploit additional information about the specific base mechanism to yield stronger privacy bounds. Moreover, there is no unified framework for deriving subsampling guarantees across the major privacy accounting paradigms (approximate DP, Rényi DP, and dominating pairs), and group privacy under subsampling has not been tightly analyzed beyond a single special case.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242495/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a subsampled mechanism M = B∘S specified by a subsampling scheme (Poisson rate r, or batch size q for sampling with/without replacement), a base mechanism (Gaussian, Laplace, or randomized response with given noise and sensitivity), a neighboring relation on datasets and batches, and a group size K. It produces tight privacy parameters (ε′, δ′), RDP parameters (α, ρ), or dominating pairs (P′, Q′) for the subsampled mechanism under the specified group relation. The dominating pairs are directly consumable by standard PLD accountants (e.g., Google's dp_accounting library) for tracking privacy under composition, and the bounds are expressed as divergences between explicit Gaussian or Laplace mixtures that can be evaluated to arbitrary numerical precision.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-step procedure based on conditional optimal transport: (1) partition the batch space into I and J events (e.g., the number of inserted or deleted elements present in a batch), (2) construct a simultaneous coupling γ between the I+J conditional subsampling distributions, and (3) bound the resulting cost function—a divergence between two small mixtures of base mechanism densities—by maximizing over worst-case mixture components subject to pairwise batch distance constraints. This yields Theorem 3.4, which generalizes the mechanism-agnostic bound of Balle et al. (recovered by conditioning on a single event) and tightens it by limiting the recursion depth of joint convexity. For group privacy, the events correspond to how many of the K group members' data appear in a batch, and the coupling is constructed via a generative process that iteratively adds inserted/deleted elements in random order. The framework subsumes prior mechanism-agnostic ADP and RDP subsampling results, derives novel dominating pairs for subsampling with replacement, and produces tight mechanism-specific group privacy bounds that outperform both mechanism-agnostic bounds and post-hoc application of the group privacy property.

## Results

The paper's headline quantitative result is on MNIST image classification with DP-SGD (group size 2, ε=8, δ=10⁻⁵): the tight mechanism-specific group privacy analysis achieves 91.2% validation accuracy after 8 epochs, while the post-hoc group privacy baseline terminates after fewer than 200 iterations with only 79.6% accuracy. For PLD accounting with Gaussian mechanisms (σ=5, r=10⁻³, ε=2, group size 16, δ=10⁻⁶), the mechanism-specific analysis enables over 1000 training iterations versus fewer than 100 for the post-hoc baseline. For subsampling with replacement (Gaussian, σ=1, N=100, q=8), the mechanism-specific bound yields privacy profiles more than an order of magnitude smaller than the tight mechanism-agnostic bound for ε≥2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the tight mechanism-agnostic RDP bound of Wang et al. [28] for substitution with subsampling without replacement, the paper's mechanism-specific bound for randomized response eliminates constant factors and achieves substantially smaller ρ across α∈(1,10⁴], with the gap widening for smaller batch-to-dataset ratios (q/N=0.001). Against the tight mechanism-agnostic ADP/RDP framework of Balle et al. [15], the mechanism-specific group privacy bound is identical for group size K=1 but strictly stronger for K≥2, as the fine-grained partitioning captures that it is unlikely for many group members' data to simultaneously appear in a batch. Against the post-hoc application of the group privacy property (Vadhan [23]) combined with tight single-element subsampling bounds, the joint mechanism-specific analysis yields dramatically stronger guarantees under composition: for PLD accounting at group size 16, the post-hoc baseline diverges after <100 iterations while the mechanism-specific bound sustains >1000 iterations. Against Zhu et al. [30]'s Poisson subsampled RDP bound used in the post-hoc RDP baseline, the mechanism-specific analysis delays the phase transition from high- to low-privacy regime to larger α values, which is beneficial for RDP-to-ADP conversion after composition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanism-agnostic subsampling amplification framework of Balle et al. (NeurIPS 2018) by introducing conditional optimal transport between multiple subsampling distributions, enabling mechanism-specific guarantees that exploit structural information about the base mechanism beyond its (ε,δ) parameters. It unifies three previously separate lines of privacy analysis—approximate DP, Rényi DP, and dominating pairs (Zhu et al., AISTATS 2022)—under a single optimal transport formulation, and subsumes the Balle et al. framework as a special case. The group privacy analysis departs from the standard post-hoc application of Vadhan's group privacy property by jointly analyzing subsampling and group composition, generalizing earlier results for subsampled matrix mechanisms (Choquette-Choo et al., ICLR 2024) to arbitrary mechanisms and both insertion/removal and substitution relations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that mechanism-specific and mechanism-agnostic tightness are qualitatively different properties in privacy amplification—a distinction that had gone unnoticed because the two coincide for group size K=1. Practically, this translates to a 10× increase in allowable DP-SGD training iterations (100 vs. 1000+) under group privacy constraints, demonstrated with a 79.6%→91.2% accuracy improvement on MNIST. Limitations include the need to manually select conditioning events (no automated procedure), potential restrictiveness for continuous batch spaces, and the fact that the MNIST experiment uses a small model and dataset. Open directions include applying the framework to correlated subsampling (e.g., shuffling), epoch-level guarantees, and generalizing the matrix mechanism analysis to substitutions and non-Gaussian distributions.
