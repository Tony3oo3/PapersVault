## Problem

Measuring per-unit interpretability in vision DNNs has relied on human psychophysics experiments (2-AFC tasks), which are expensive and limit evaluations to a few hundred units across a handful of models. This bottleneck prevents large-scale comparisons across architectures, fine-grained analyses of layer properties, and tracking interpretability dynamics during training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241534/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For each unit, the method consumes two sets of visual explanation images (positive: strongly activating; negative: weakly activating, K=9 each) and two query images (the most extreme activating and deactivating dataset examples) drawn from ImageNet-2012. It outputs a scalar Machine Interpretability Score (MIS) in [0,1], averaged over N=20 independently constructed tasks per unit. The score is explanation-method-dependent: it can be computed for dataset examples, feature visualizations, or SAE features, with the same hyperparameters reused across methods.

## Method

The MIS replaces human participants in the 2-AFC matching task with an automated pipeline built on DreamSim, a perceptual similarity function aligned with human visual judgments. For each task, it computes the mean DreamSim similarity between each query image and the positive vs. negative explanation sets, yielding two difference scores (Δ+ and Δ−). A hard-coded sigmoid classifier with a single calibrated parameter (α=0.16, tuned on 5% of IMI annotations) maps the difference Δ+ − Δ− to a probability of correct classification. The final MIS is the mean of these probabilities over 20 tasks. This design requires no learned components beyond the pre-trained DreamSim backbone, making it computationally cheap (dominated by a single forward pass over the dataset) and scalable to 70+ million units across 835 models.

## Results

The Machine Interpretability Score (MIS) achieves a Pearson correlation of r=0.98 (Spearman r=0.94) with Human Interpretability Scores (HIS) at the model level, and ρ=0.80 at the per-unit level—nearly matching the estimated noise ceiling of 0.82. In an interventional experiment on 84 previously untested units, MIS correctly predicted interpretability rankings with Pearson ρ=0.85 and Spearman ρ=0.81 on newly collected human data. Across 835 models (70M+ units), the paper finds a significant anticorrelation between ImageNet classification accuracy and per-unit interpretability (Pearson r=−0.5, Spearman r=−0.55), and shows that deeper and wider layers are more interpretable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare MIS against competing automated interpretability metrics, as it is the first such measure. Instead, validation is performed against human psychophysics annotations (HIS) from the IMI dataset. At the per-model level, MIS reproduces the exact model ranking from prior human evaluations (r=0.98). At the per-unit level, MIS reaches ρ=0.80, essentially saturating the noise ceiling (0.82) imposed by the finite number of human annotators (~30 per unit). For the underlying perceptual similarity function, DreamSim outperforms LPIPS and DISTS at the per-unit level (higher correlation, tighter point cloud), though all three perform similarly at the per-model level. In the SAE analysis (Appx. I), neither standard SAEs nor Gated SAEs show a systematic MIS advantage over the original layer (original layer MIS ≈ 91.21%), though SAEs on a less interpretable ResNet-50 layer reach up to 0.922 MIS with 10× more units.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the 2-AFC psychophysics paradigm introduced by Borowski et al. (ICLR 2021) and scaled by Zimmermann et al. (NeurIPS 2023) for quantifying per-unit interpretability in vision models, replacing human participants with a fully automated evaluation based on DreamSim perceptual similarity. It also draws conceptual inspiration from the 'automated interpretability' line of work in NLP (Bills et al., 2023), which used LLMs to generate textual unit descriptions, but applies a different strategy: leveraging learned image similarity functions as a proxy for human perceptual judgment. The key departure from prior work is the elimination of human labor entirely, enabling evaluation at a scale (70M+ units across 835 models) that would cost over a billion dollars via psychophysics experiments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MIS provides a practical, near-free tool that unblocks large-scale interpretability research previously constrained by human evaluation costs, enabling hypothesis testing across hundreds of models and during training dynamics. The anticorrelation between downstream classification accuracy and per-unit interpretability (r = −0.55 across 835 models) is a notable finding that challenges the intuition that better models are more interpretable, though the causal mechanism remains speculative. Key limitations include the per-unit correlation approaching the noise ceiling of existing human annotations (ρ = 0.80 vs. 0.82 ceiling), dependence on a single calibration parameter, and restriction to vision models. Open directions include differentiable approximations for interpretability-aware training, VLM-based extensions providing textual descriptions, and generalization to LLMs.
