## Problem

Whether teams of self-interested agents can reach Team-Nash Equilibrium (TNE) in multi-team games remains largely unexplored. Classical fictitious play dynamics do not necessarily reach the best team outcome even in the absence of opponents (e.g., in potential games), and existing log-linear learning approaches lack convergence guarantees under the non-stationarity induced by evolving opponent strategies. This paper asks whether a simple, decentralized behavioral rule can drive implicit team coordination toward TNE without explicit intra-team communication.

## Input / Output

Each agent i in team m takes as input the previous actions of its own team members a_{k-1}^{-i}, its own previous action a_{k-1}^{i}, and beliefs π_k^{-m} about the joint strategies of all opponent teams (updated via weighted empirical averages of observed joint action profiles). The output at each stage is a joint action profile per team, and the long-run output is a strategy profile whose Team-Nash Gap (TNG) is bounded by τ log|A| for Team-FP or τ log|A| + |T|²φ̄·Λ(δ,ε) for Independent Team-FP. Notably, agents in networked settings only need local observations from their neighborhood, as local empirical averages correspond to marginalizations of the full team belief.

## Method

Team-FP combines two mechanisms: (i) agents respond via a smoothed best response (temperature τ) to the *last actions* of their own team members and to beliefs about opponent teams, and (ii) agents retain inertia by repeating their previous action with some probability (or, in the independent variant, each agent updates with probability δ independently). This design is essential because responding to teammates' last actions drives coordination, while inertia prevents destructive oscillation. The convergence proof handles non-stationarity by dividing time into epochs, constructing a reference scenario where opponent beliefs are frozen (reducing the dynamics to log-linear learning with a known stationary distribution), bounding the coupling error via the optimal coupling lemma, and then showing that the relaxed belief dynamics (with additive bounded error) converge using stochastic differential inclusion approximation and a Lyapunov function L(π) = Σ_m max_μ {φ^m(μ, π^{-m}) + τH(μ)}. The extension to multi-team Markov games replaces payoffs with Q-function estimates updated via model-based or model-free temporal-difference learning, with beliefs maintained per (state, stage) pair.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.2) establishes that Team-FP reaches near TNE almost surely in ZSPTGs, with the Team-Nash Gap bounded by τ log|A| for Team-FP and τ log|A| + |T|²φ̄·Λ(δ,ε) for Independent Team-FP, where τ is the temperature parameter and Λ(δ,ε) decays to zero as δ→0⁺. Numerically, in a 2-team ZSPTG with 4 agents per team, Team-FP and Independent Team-FP both converge to near TNE (TNG approaching the theoretical bound), while MWU and SFP fail to achieve team coordination. Against a stationary opponent in a 3-team setting (3 agents per team), Team-FP converges significantly faster than in the competitive setting, confirming the rationality guarantee of Corollary 4.3. In a large-scale experiment (3 teams × 9 agents, joint action space of size 2²⁷), Team-FP still converges to near TNE at a comparable rate despite sparse 2-hop network interconnections.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Team-FP against two primary baselines: Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU) [Cai et al., 2016] and Smoothed Fictitious Play (SFP) [Fudenberg and Kreps, 1993]. In the 2-team ZSPTG (4 agents per team, τ=0.1), both Team-FP and Independent Team-FP converge to near TNE, while MWU and SFP fail to achieve team coordination — their TNG values remain elevated and do not approach the theoretical bound (Figure 3b). This is the central empirical finding: classical no-regret and FP-based dynamics cannot drive self-interested agents within a team to coordinate, whereas Team-FP's two key features (response to last team-member actions and inertia in updates) are sufficient. The paper also compares different levels of explicit coordination within Team-FP: fully coordinated teams (group size 4, equivalent to standard FP in a 2-person zero-sum game) converge fastest, followed by pairs (group size 2), with fully independent agents (group size 1, i.e., Team-FP) converging slowest but still reaching near TNE (Figure 3a). Against a stationary opponent, Team-FP converges much faster than in the competitive multi-team setting, validating the rationality property (Corollary 4.3).

## Relation to Prior Work

Team-FP unifies two previously separate learning paradigms: fictitious play (for adapting to opponent strategies) and log-linear learning (for intra-team coordination via response to teammates' last actions and inertia in updates). It extends the two-team zero-sum game literature (Farina et al. 2018, Zhang et al. 2022), which typically models teams as single agents with ex ante communication, to a multi-team setting with self-interested agents and networked interconnections. The convergence proof adapts the reference-scenario-with-frozen-beliefs technique from Donmez et al. (2024) for Markov team problems, but introduces a new challenge: the lack of a contraction property in the multi-team competitive dynamics, which is resolved via stochastic differential inclusion approximation rather than direct error decay.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first provable result showing that teams of self-interested agents, following simple decentralized behavioral rules without explicit communication, can reach near-Team-Nash Equilibrium in multi-team games. The practical implication is significant for domains like robotics, security, and resource management where treating uncoordinated agents as a single decision-maker (team-minimax) is standard but previously lacked a learning-theoretic justification. Key limitations include the absence of convergence rate guarantees (only asymptotic almost-sure convergence), restriction to zero-sum potential team games, and the stationary-opponent assumption for the rationality corollary. Open questions include non-asymptotic analysis, extension to infinite-horizon Markov games, and whether different intra-team dynamics (beyond log-linear learning) preserve convergence.
