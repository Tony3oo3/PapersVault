## Problem

Learning 3D particle dynamics from 2D video is fundamentally ill-posed because multiple 3D particle configurations can project to the same 2D image, and existing GNN-based simulators attempt to learn the entire dynamics mapping as a black box, making optimization difficult and generalization poor under pixel-level supervision. The paper addresses this by embedding the Discrete Element Analysis (DEA) mechanical framework as strong physical priors, so that neural networks only need to approximate specific force operators rather than the full evolution of dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multiview 2D video sequences (4 cameras per scene) along with camera intrinsics/extrinsics. The scene is first initialized as a particle set via the Generalizable Point Field (GPF) renderer at the first timestamp, with initial velocities approximated from the first three frames. At each subsequent timestep, the dynamics module predicts updated 3D particle positions, which are rendered back to images; training is supervised by the L1 rendering loss between predicted and ground-truth frames, plus auxiliary losses on normal-force-only renderings and velocity-direction gradients.

## Method

DEL replaces the handcrafted constitutive equations in the classic Discrete Element Analysis framework with learnable graph network kernels that predict only the magnitudes of contact, bond, and dissipative forces, while the DEA structure precomputes force directions (normal and tangential unit vectors) and the Newton-Euler integration. The architecture uses two graph kernels (Φⁿ and Φᵗ) to map intrusion scalars, relative velocities, and material embeddings into force magnitudes, with separate heads for contact and bond forces and sigmoid-activated MLPs modeling dissipation as a reduction coefficient. This decomposition contracts the solution space dramatically compared to learning full dynamics, making the inverse-rendering optimization tractable from 2D supervision alone. Training uses AdamW with a StepLR schedule, and the system is renderer-agnostic—any fully differentiable particle-based renderer can be plugged in without architectural changes.

## Results

On the particle-view metrics (Chamfer Distance and Earth Mover Distance, both ×10²), DEL achieves the best or near-best results across all five scenarios. The largest gains are on Plasticine (CD 7.54 vs. 16.20 for EGNN*) and Multi-Objs (CD 8.48 vs. 13.21 for EGNN*), where the physics-informed decomposition of contact and bond forces provides a substantial advantage. On the render-view metrics (PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS), DEL leads on four of five scenarios, with VPD remaining competitive on rendering quality due to its jointly trained renderer. DEL also outperforms Neurofluid on the Fluids scenario (CD 4.18 vs. 10.7, EMD 2.97 vs. 11.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEL outperforms all baselines on the core particle-dynamics task in 4 of 5 scenarios. Against EGNN* (the strongest GNN baseline under 2D supervision), DEL reduces CD by 53% on Plasticine (7.54 vs. 16.20) and 36% on Multi-Objs (8.48 vs. 13.21), demonstrating that the DEA force decomposition (separating contact, bond, and dissipative components with physically constrained directions) dramatically shrinks the solution space. Against VPD, the closest competitor on particle metrics, DEL wins on Plasticine (CD 7.54 vs. 16.96), Multi-Objs (8.48 vs. 14.26), and FluidR (1.72 vs. 3.22), while VPD edges out DEL slightly on Bear CD (3.41 vs. 3.54) but DEL still wins on Bear EMD (3.33 vs. 3.71). SGNN* and 3DIntphys are substantially weaker, with SGNN* particularly struggling on Plasticine (CD 35.91) because its sub-equivariant message passing must simultaneously learn both force directions and magnitudes from 2D supervision. On the render-view metrics, VPD remains competitive (e.g., PSNR 30.52 vs. 30.08 on Bear) because it jointly optimizes its own renderer, but DEL achieves the best PSNR on Plasticine (28.09) and Multi-Objs (27.06), confirming that better dynamics prediction translates to better rendering. On the Fluids scenario, DEL substantially outperforms Neurofluid (CD 4.18 vs. 10.7), which the authors attribute to Neurofluid's lack of physical priors and its coupled renderer-dynamics training causing overfitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

DEL sits at the intersection of two research threads: classical Discrete Element Analysis (DEA), which decomposes inter-particle forces into normal/tangential contact and bond components with user-defined constitutive laws, and GNN-based particle simulators (GNS, EGNN, SGNN) that learn full dynamics mappings from 3D particle tracks. The key departure is architectural: rather than using a GNN to predict next-frame positions end-to-end, DEL restricts the GNN to approximating only the force-magnitude operators within the fixed DEA skeleton, with force directions and decompositions imposed by physics. This contrasts with prior inverse-rendering approaches (Neurofluid, VPD) that either handle a single material class or couple the dynamics module tightly to a specific renderer, whereas DEL is renderer-agnostic and unifies multiple materials under one framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By contracting the solution space through mechanical priors, DEL addresses the core ill-posedness of inferring 3D dynamics from 2D observations, enabling robust learning even from a single camera view and limited training episodes. The material-swapping capability (exchanging GNN kernels and embeddings to alter simulated material behavior without retraining) opens a practical path for rapid prototyping of multi-material interactions. However, the method is validated only on synthetic MPM-generated data, struggles with non-Newtonian fluids whose properties vary with stress, and cannot handle phenomena governed by thermodynamics (e.g., smoke). A key open question is whether the framework can be adapted to real-world multiview video where per-particle initialization and material labels are unavailable.
