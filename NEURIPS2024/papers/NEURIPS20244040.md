## Problem

Selective state-space models (Mamba, GLA, HGRN2) empirically surpass attention-based models on language tasks, yet the theoretical reason their input-dependent gating mechanism unlocks in-context learning remains unexplained. Prior expressivity results only covered non-selective SSMs (S4), which are equivalent to fixed convolutions, leaving a gap in understanding what the selectivity mechanism actually enables and how architectural choices like diagonal vs. dense recurrences affect modeling capacity.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a d-dimensional input path X: [0,1] → R^d (discretized as a length-L token sequence) and produces either a same-dimensional output token sequence or a scalar path functional at a fixed time t. Crucially, the input is transformed by two continuous gating functions ω and ξ into potentially higher-dimensional driving signals (dimensions d_ω and d_ξ), which determine how the hidden state evolves. The final output is a linear projection C·Z_t^X of the N-dimensional hidden state, with the option of an MLP readout for path-to-path learning.

## Method

The paper casts selective SSMs as Linear CDEs of the form dZ_t = Σ A_i Z_t dω_t^i + B dξ_t, where the gating functions ω (state-to-state) and ξ (input injection) are nonlinear maps of the input path. Using the Signature Transform from Rough Path Theory, the CDE solution is expanded as an infinite linear combination of iterated integrals of the driving path, revealing that expressivity is governed almost entirely by the choice of ω. This yields a full characterization of the uniform closure: dense Linear CDEs approximate arbitrary continuous functions of the seen input path (Thm 4.1), random Glorot-initialized matrices suffice with high probability (Thm 4.2), diagonal recurrences as in Mamba have a strictly smaller closure limited to pairwise comparisons of transformed inputs (Thm 4.3), and chaining K diagonal CDEs recovers full expressivity (Prop 4.5). Empirical validation on anti-symmetric signature prediction and the A5 state-tracking benchmark confirms the theoretical gap between diagonal and dense recurrences.

## Results

On the anti-symmetric signature prediction task (2D and 3D), the Linear CDE with dense random matrices and a single trainable linear readout achieves the highest validation accuracy without any stacking or non-linearity, while S4 requires a non-linearity between stacked layers to perform competitively and Mamba benefits from stacking but not from added non-linearities. On the A5 state-tracking benchmark (sequence lengths 3–20), the Linear CDE and a standard RNN both exceed 90% validation accuracy at all sequence lengths using only a single block, whereas S4, Mamba, and the Transformer fail to reach 90% accuracy at length 20 even with up to 4 blocks. The number of blocks required for S4, Mamba, and the Transformer to reach 90% accuracy grows with sequence length, confirming the theoretical expressivity gap between diagonal and dense recurrences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Linear CDE (dense, random matrices, linear readout) outperforms S4 on the anti-symmetric signature task: S4 with a linear readout performs poorly and requires stacking with a non-linearity (GeLU) to close the gap, while the Linear CDE achieves strong accuracy with a single layer and no non-linearity. Against Mamba, the Linear CDE again wins on the signature task without stacking, whereas Mamba's diagonal recurrence requires two stacked layers with a linear mixing layer to approach comparable performance; adding a non-linearity between Mamba layers provides no further gain. On the A5 benchmark, the Linear CDE matches the RNN (both achieve >90% accuracy at all lengths with one block) and decisively outperforms S4, Mamba, and the Transformer, none of which reach 90% accuracy at sequence length 20 within 4 blocks. The Transformer, despite its quadratic attention mechanism, shows the same scaling limitation as the diagonal SSMs, suggesting that the bottleneck is not attention per se but the lack of dense hidden-state mixing in a single layer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies selective SSMs (Mamba/S6, GLA, HGRN2, Griffin) under the Linear CDE framework and applies Rough Path Theory to characterize their expressivity, generalizing the linear RNN universality results of Li et al. (2022b) and Orvieto et al. (2023b) which only covered non-selective S4. The mathematical tools draw on the Signature transform literature (Lyons, Kidger, Fermanian, Salvi) and the Neural CDE framework (Kidger et al. 2020), while the empirical A5 benchmark builds on Merrill et al. (2024)'s formal-language-theoretic analysis of SSM limitations. The key conceptual departure is identifying the input-dependent gate ω as the sole source of expressivity beyond convolution, and showing that the diagonal structure used in Mamba strictly reduces the closure compared to dense matrices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first closed-form characterization of what selective SSMs can and cannot compute, explaining theoretically why Mamba surpasses S4 and identifying the gating mechanism as the critical architectural choice. The finding that diagonal recurrence (Mamba's core design) is strictly less expressive than dense matrices—recoverable only by stacking—offers a concrete theoretical argument for future architecture improvements, particularly cross-channel mixing within the recurrent block. Limitations include the continuous-time, real-valued setting and the impracticality of dense matrices at scale, leaving open how to achieve dense-level expressivity with computationally efficient (e.g., structured or low-rank) alternatives. The reservoir computing result (random matrices + linear readout suffice for dense CDEs) suggests a potential training-free initialization strategy worth exploring empirically at scale.
