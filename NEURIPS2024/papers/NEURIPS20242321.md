## Problem

Goal-conditioned RL agents are typically conditioned on target states (limited to Markovian tasks) or natural language (ambiguous semantics), leaving a gap for temporal tasks that require both formal guarantees and interpretability. Deterministic finite automata (DFAs) offer a natural formalism for such tasks, but they form a countably infinite concept class where subtle structural changes yield drastically different behaviors, making them difficult to encode and condition policies on. Additionally, the binary accept/reject reward signal is extremely sparse, and monolithic product DFAs grow quadratically with each conjunction, creating computational barriers.

## Input / Output

At each timestep the policy receives two inputs: an environment observation (a 7×7 grid with letter labels in Letterworld, or lidar/accelerometer/velocimeter sensor data in Zones) and a cDFA task specification — a set of DFAs whose conjunction defines the temporal goal. The cDFA is featurized as a directed graph where each DFA transition becomes an intermediate node with one-hot symbol encodings, edges are reversed to propagate information toward initial states, and a dedicated AND node links all initial states. The output is a single action (4-way discrete movement or continuous steering/acceleration). After each action, the cDFA is updated by advancing each constituent DFA along the observed symbol and re-minimizing, so the goal embedding evolves over the episode.

## Method

The core architecture uses a GATv2 graph attention network to embed the cDFA featurization into a 32-dimensional goal vector, which is concatenated with a CNN/MLP observation embedding and fed to a PPO policy. The key pre-training insight is that every path through a DFA decomposes into a sequence of local reach-avoid sub-tasks; the authors therefore define reach-avoid derived (RAD) DFAs — SRA DFAs mutated m times and minimized — and pre-train the GATv2 on RAD cDFAs in a dummy single-state MDP where the only dynamics are the DFA transitions themselves. This decouples embedding learning from control learning and encourages the encoder to capture the structural navigation patterns common to all DFAs. Conjunctions of DFAs (cDFAs) are encoded directly as a single graph with an AND node rather than as the monolithic product DFA, yielding an exponential reduction in graph size. Experiments show that frozen RAD-pre-trained GATv2 embeddings generalize zero-shot to unseen task classes (reach, reach-avoid, parity, LTL-derived) with ~90–100% satisfaction, outperforming both non-pre-trained policies and RGCN baselines, and avoiding the myopic suboptimality of hierarchical planning approaches.

## Results

In Letterworld (discrete), frozen RAD-pre-trained GATv2 policies satisfy sampled RAD cDFA tasks over 95% of the time within 5 timesteps, while non-pre-trained policies barely learn the tasks. GATv2 achieves approximately 90% satisfaction across all tested cDFA task classes (Reach, RA, RAR, Parity, PO, AV), reaching nearly 100% in most cases, compared to ~80% for RGCN on harder compositions (cRA.1.5.1.5, cRA.1.1.1.10). Even very long cRA tasks of up to 10 symbols are satisfied above 90% of the time, despite exceeding the message-passing depth of the GNN. In the continuous Zones environment, RAD-pre-trained policies generalize to cDFAs with up to 10 conjunctions (fully out-of-distribution), though performance degrades for individual DFA task lengths of 5+ due to harder continuous control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is LTL2Action [38], which uses LTL syntax embeddings to condition RL policies on temporal tasks. The RAD-pre-trained cDFA policy outperforms LTL2Action on cAV.1.3.1.2, the very task class the LTL2Action policy is specifically trained on, demonstrating that RAD pre-training provides stronger generalization even in-distribution. On cPO.1.5.1.4, both methods achieve 100% satisfaction, but the RAD policy was never specifically trained on this task class, indicating zero-shot transfer. On harder (longer) versions of both PO and AV tasks, the RAD policy maintains high satisfaction while LTL2Action degrades. Internally, GATv2 consistently outperforms RGCN across all task classes, with the largest gaps on harder compositions (e.g., ~90% vs. ~80% on cRA.1.5.1.5), and frozen pre-training outperforms non-frozen pre-training for RGCN but shows a smaller gap for GATv2, suggesting the attention mechanism learns more robust embeddings that benefit from continued fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends goal-conditioned RL [33] from target-state and natural-language goals to temporal tasks specified as compositional deterministic finite automata (cDFAs), building directly on LTL2Action [38] for the pre-training protocol and evaluation environments. It departs from LTL-based conditioning by arguing that DFAs are strictly more expressive over finite traces and that their local reach-avoid structure provides a natural inductive bias for generalization, whereas LTL's syntactic structure makes subclass separation trivial but generalization unclear. The RAD pre-training distribution is a novel contribution that replaces the ad-hoc task sampling in prior work with a principled distribution derived from the observation that all DFA paths decompose into sequential reach-avoid subtasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing unambiguous Boolean semantics for temporal goals, cDFAs offer a path toward verifiable and interpretable AI agent control in safety-critical settings where natural language is too ambiguous and target states too limited. The zero-shot generalization from RAD pre-training to unseen task classes (including LTL-defined tasks the model was never trained on) suggests a viable route to flexible, formally-specified agent behavior without per-task retraining. Key limitations include the assumption of a known state-to-symbol labeling function, evaluation restricted to simple grid and continuous navigation environments, and the open question of how far the RAD inductive bias generalizes to substantially larger or more complex automata.
