## Problem

In unsupervised goal-conditioned RL, existing Go-Explore methods (MEGA, PEG) select exploration goals at the frontier of known states, but the training policy often lacks the capability to actually reach these rare frontier goals, resulting in wasted Go-phase timesteps and reduced exploratory behavior. The core gap is the absence of a systematic mechanism to identify under-explored regions that remain accessible to the current policy, rather than simply targeting the least-visited or highest-exploration-potential states regardless of reachability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment state observations as state vectors (ranging from 2D in Point Maze to 61D in Block/Pen Rotation) and goal commands, with the assumption S=G so any state can serve as a goal. It produces a trained goal-conditioned policy π^G that maps (state, goal) pairs to actions, an exploration policy π^E for the Explore-phase, and at each training episode a selected exploration goal command g^E chosen from the boundaries of latent state clusters. The world model (RSSM) operates on encoded latent representations, and the temporal distance network D_t predicts normalized step counts between state embeddings to structure the latent space.

## Method

CE² trains a latent space with an augmented loss L_latent = L_rec + L_dt, where the temporal distance loss (Eq. 2) enforces that Euclidean distances between state embeddings approximate the number of action steps needed to transition between them, making the latent space reflect reachability. States in the replay buffer are then clustered using a Gaussian Mixture Model initialized via Farthest Point Sampling, grouping states that are easily mutually reachable by the current policy. Exploration goals are sampled from the boundaries of these clusters (the N_edge points with lowest total GMM probability out of N_candidate=1000 samples), ensuring they lie at the frontier of known regions while remaining within the policy's capability. A PEG-style filter then evaluates the exploration potential P^E(g) of each boundary candidate by simulating Go-phase rollouts in the world model and averaging the exploration value V^E at the terminal states, selecting the goal with highest potential. The selected goal drives a Go-Explore episode: π^G navigates toward g^E for T timesteps, then π^E explores from the resulting state for T_E timesteps, with the full trajectory added to the replay buffer for iterative retraining of the world model, GMM, and both policies.

## Results

In the unsupervised setting (goal distribution revealed only at test time), CE² significantly outperforms both PEG and MEGA in goal-reaching success rate across all six environments except Point Maze, where performance is comparable. In the supervised setting (goal distribution available at training time), CE²-G achieves a success rate exceeding 90% on the challenging 3-Block Stacking task, while MEGA-G, PEG-G, and GC-Dreamer all remain below 40%. All results are averaged over 5 random seeds with 1M total training steps per environment. The evaluation metric is goal-reaching success rate, with the most challenging goals (e.g., farthest locations, hardest stacking configurations) used for evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PEG (Hu et al., 2023), CE² wins in Ant Maze, Walker, 3-Block Stacking, Block Rotation, and Pen Rotation, while tying on Point Maze. PEG's weakness is that it samples goals from the full state space via MPPI, and the learned world model is unfamiliar with rarely observed states, causing it to select goals that appear promising in imagination but are unreachable in reality (visualized in Fig. 4 where PEG targets fall outside maze corridors). Against MEGA (Pitis et al., 2020), CE² similarly wins in all environments except Point Maze; MEGA's KDE-based frontier selection lacks a mechanism to verify whether the current policy can actually reach the chosen low-density goals, leading to wasted Go-phase timesteps. In the supervised setting, CE²-G substantially outperforms L3P (Zhang et al., 2021) on 3-Block Stacking (>90% vs. <40%), as L3P uses graph-search planning over cluster centroids rather than a Go-Explore strategy, limiting its ability to progressively expand exploration. Notably, GC-Dreamer (without any Go-Explore phase) sometimes outperforms MEGA-G and PEG-G on Block Rotation and Pen Rotation, demonstrating that poorly chosen exploration goals can actively hinder learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

CE² sits at the intersection of the Go-Explore exploration framework (Ecoffet et al., 2019), Dreamer-style model-based RL (Hafner et al.), and temporal-distance-structured latent spaces (LEXA/Mendonca et al., 2021; L3P/Zhang et al., 2021). Its key departure from prior Go-Explore instantiations (MEGA, PEG) is the introduction of GMM clustering in a reachability-aware latent space to identify cluster boundaries as 'accessible frontier' goals, rather than relying on density estimates (MEGA) or unconstrained state-space sampling (PEG). The exploration potential filtering step (Eq. 6) directly adapts PEG's imagined-rollout evaluation, but applies it only to the constrained candidate set from cluster edges, addressing the core failure mode where frontier goals are unreachable by the current policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical and well-motivated gap in unsupervised GCRL: frontier goals selected by density or exploration-potential heuristics may be unreachable by the still-weak training policy, wasting exploration budget. The >90% success rate on 3-Block Stacking (vs. <40% for all baselines) demonstrates that constraining goal selection to accessible cluster boundaries can dramatically improve sample efficiency in dexterous manipulation. However, the method is tightly coupled to model-based RL (Dreamer/RSSM) and requires a well-trained temporal distance predictor, limiting immediate applicability to model-free settings. The authors explicitly flag extending CE² to model-free GCRL as future work, and the computational overhead of per-episode GMM sampling and imagined-rollout evaluation (though modest at ~0.56s/episode) remains a consideration for large-scale deployment.
