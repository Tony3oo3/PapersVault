## Problem

Formal specifications in signal temporal logic (STL) are widely claimed to be human-interpretable for system validation, yet prior empirical work showed humans achieve less than 50% accuracy validating even simple robot behaviors. This paper investigates whether pedagogical active learning—requiring users to generate example trajectories to 'show their work'—can close the gap between interpretability claims and actual human validation performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243782/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each validation task pairs an STL specification (43–97 symbols, AST depth 3–5, expressed in linguistic form with ALWAYS/EVENTUALLY temporal operators and spatial constraints on a 2D grid) with a grid-world map containing a blue agent, a green objective, and static red opponents. The user intent is codified as game-winning conditions: reach the objective within 30 steps while never being within one space of a red player. In active learning conditions, participants must additionally produce concrete trajectories (sequences of grid positions) that satisfy the specification before committing to a valid/invalid determination, with a confidence rating accompanying every answer.

## Method

The paper introduces ManeuverGame, a custom grid-world testbed where participants validate whether an STL policy guarantees the blue agent always wins. The key experimental design is a three-condition between-subjects study (n=55): a control group that simply judges validity, an active-learning-without-feedback group (AL-NF) that must generate spec-satisfying trajectories but receives no verification feedback, and an active-learning-with-feedback group (AL-WF) where runtime verification (via PyTelo) checks each submitted trajectory against the specification in real time. The active learning protocol decomposes validation into two Bloom's-taxonomy-aligned steps—behavior exploration (generate trajectories within the spec's allowed set) followed by determination (judge whether the full allowed set matches intent)—effectively requiring proof-by-contradiction for invalid specs and exemplification for valid ones. The study measures overall accuracy, confidence calibration, trajectory-specification adherence, and engagement, finding no significant performance difference across conditions (overall accuracy 65% ± 15%) despite significantly longer engagement times in active learning conditions.

## Results

Overall validation accuracy across all conditions was 65% ± 15% (mean ± SD), with AL-NF at 66% ± 16%, AL-WF at 67% ± 16%, and Control at 62% ± 13%. All three conditions performed significantly better than random chance (50%), with Cohen's d effect sizes of 0.86–1.00. However, there was no significant difference in accuracy among the three conditions (F(2,42) = 0.0804, p = 0.453), and no significant effect of STEM background, formal methods familiarity, or education level on performance (all p > 0.05).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a human-subjects study, so the 'baselines' are the three experimental conditions rather than competing algorithms. The key finding is that neither active learning condition (AL-NF or AL-WF) significantly outperforms the Control condition on validation accuracy (p = 0.453 omnibus test), despite active learning conditions requiring substantially more engagement time (36–40 min vs. 20 min for Control). The AL-WF condition, which used runtime verification to provide real-time feedback on trajectory correctness, showed the highest mean accuracy (67%) but the difference from Control (62%) is not statistically significant. STEM experience showed a marginal positive correlation with performance in the Control condition (r = 0.599, p = 0.030) but not after Bonferroni correction, and this effect was attenuated in the active learning conditions. The paper also notes that 34% of trajectories submitted in AL-NF did not actually satisfy the specification, and that trajectory satisfaction was correlated with correctness only for valid ground-truth specifications (p = 6.25×10⁻⁵), not for invalid ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct follow-up to Siu et al. [38], which demonstrated that humans achieve less than 50% accuracy validating even simple STL policies, and to Greenman et al. [17] on LTL misconceptions. It repurposes pedagogical active learning (Bonwell & Eison [2], Freeman et al. [16])—a well-validated educational technique—into a system-validation intervention, combined with runtime verification monitors (Cardona et al. [8]) for feedback. The paper also builds on Vinter et al. [39] and Loomes & Vinter [29], who showed that natural-language renderings of formal specifications evoke heuristic rather than logical reasoning, motivating the decision to present specifications in their native (linguistic-form) STL notation without translation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a well-established pedagogical technique (active learning) fails to improve validation accuracy beyond ~65% in a single session challenges the broad assumption in the formal methods and XAI communities that formal specifications are inherently human-interpretable. The paper identifies edge-case discovery as the core cognitive skill needed for validation and suggests both human-side (training in adversarial case-finding) and machine-side (automated generation of minimally-robust trajectories) interventions as future directions. Key open questions include whether multi-session active learning would show effects (as the education literature suggests), whether domain experts in adversarial reasoning (lawyers, compliance officers) outperform formal methods experts, and how results generalize beyond the simplified grid-world setting to real-world stakeholder intents.
