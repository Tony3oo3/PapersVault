## Problem

Accurate Gradient Noise Scale (GNS) estimation requires per-example gradient norms, but existing methods either incur redundant computation (O(T²) memory in 3D tensor regimes) or depend on specific training configurations like DDP or gradient accumulation. This paper asks whether per-example gradient norms can be computed with minimal overhead in a configuration-independent way, and whether all layers are necessary for a practical GNS estimate.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242964/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard transformer backward-pass tensors: input activations x of shape (B,...,K), output gradients g of shape (B,...,L), and token IDs for embedding layers. It produces per-example squared gradient norms ‖w_b'‖² as a byproduct of the weight gradient computation, requiring only a 1/B² correction factor. For the practical GNS application, only LayerNorm layers (with K = hidden_size) need to be instrumented, reducing the per-example norm accumulation from hidden_size² to hidden_size elements, which fits within shared memory for the custom kernel.

## Method

The core algorithm accesses the intermediate batch-dimension tensor w'_bkl = Σ x_{b...k} g_{b...l} during the linear layer backward pass (via einsum 'b...k,b...l→bkl'), computes per-example squared norms by summing over the trailing dimensions, then reduces over the batch to obtain the standard weight gradient — adding FLOPs that scale identically to the normal backward pass. The key empirical finding is that the GNS of LayerNorm layers alone predicts the total model GNS with a slope of ~1.4 and high Pearson correlation across EMA smoothing factors, eliminating the need to instrument expensive linear layers. A custom CUDA kernel fuses the LayerNorm backward pass with per-example norm accumulation using warp-level reductions and shared-memory atomics, achieving zero throughput overhead (even outperforming PyTorch's LayerNorm at larger dimensions). Applied to a 111M Chinchilla-optimal LLM on OpenWebText, tracking LayerNorm GNS to drive a linear batch size schedule reduces training wall-time by 18%.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that a custom fused CUDA kernel computing LayerNorm backward pass and per-example gradient norms simultaneously achieves zero throughput overhead (tokens/sec) compared to PyTorch's native LayerNorm, even outperforming it at larger hidden dimensions (Figure 8). Tracking GNS using only LayerNorm layers, a linear batch size schedule reduces wall-clock training time by 18% on a 111M-parameter Chinchilla-optimal language model trained on OpenWebText (Figure 9). The GNS of LayerNorm layers alone predicts the total model GNS with a regression slope of approximately 1.4 and high Pearson correlation across all EMA smoothing settings (Figure 7). In FLOP cost, the simultaneous per-example gradient norm method is strictly dominant over Li et al.'s O(T²) approach across all model scales and sequence lengths (Figure 3).

**Table 1: FLOP cost of computing per-example gradient norms (from Appendix E).**

| Algorithm | Weight Gradient FLOPs | Gradient Norm FLOPs |
|---|---|---|
| Simultaneous (this work) | BKL(2T−1) + KL(B−1) | BKL + B(KL−1) |
| Li et al. [36] | KL(2BT−1) | BT²·(2K+2L−2) + BT² |

**Table 2: I/O cost of computing per-example gradient norms (from Appendix E).**

| Algorithm | Weight Gradient I/O | Gradient Norm I/O |
|---|---|---|
| Simultaneous (this work) | BKL + BKT + BLT | BKL + B |
| Li et al. [36] | BKT + BLT + KL | 2BT² + B |

Where B = batch size, T = sequence length, K = input dim, L = output dim. The simultaneous method's FLOP cost is independent of T; Li et al.'s scales as O(T²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Li et al. [36], the proposed simultaneous method is strictly FLOP-dominant: its gradient norm FLOPs (BKL + B(KL−1)) are independent of sequence length T, while Li et al.'s scale as O(T²) (BT²·(2K+2L−2) + BT²). In I/O cost, the simultaneous method is cheaper for long contexts (T > √(2KL)/2) but more expensive for short contexts at large model sizes due to materializing a large intermediate tensor; the crossover is at T = √(2KL)/2. Against the traditional DDP-based GNS estimation from McCandlish et al. [39], the per-example method provides lower variance (independent of DDP node count) and is universally applicable (works on single-GPU setups). On a 1.3B GPT model trained on 8 H100s, the LayerNorm-only per-example GNS tracks the DDP-based GNS closely (after correcting a constant-factor bug), confirming the correlation generalizes to larger scales. The practical batch size schedule experiment (18% speedup) has no direct baseline comparison in the paper beyond the fixed-batch-size ablation, which the schedule consistently outperforms across all three seeds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the per-example gradient norm computation trick of Goodfellow (2015) and its 3D generalization by Li et al. (2022) by baking the norm computation directly into the backward pass, eliminating the redundant O(T²) intermediate tensor. It feeds these norms into the GNS estimators of McCandlish et al. (2018), which had previously been limited to DDP or gradient-accumulation configurations. The key empirical departure is the finding that LayerNorm layers alone carry enough signal to predict total model GNS, a simplification not explored in prior GNS work that typically aggregates across all layers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-overhead CUDA kernel makes GNS measurement universally available regardless of training configuration, removing a practical barrier that had limited its adoption outside large-scale DDP setups. The 18% wall-time reduction from GNS-guided batch size scheduling demonstrates concrete training efficiency gains. However, the LayerNorm-sufficiency finding is validated primarily at 111M scale (and a 1.3B run with a known bug), leaving open whether the correlation holds at frontier model scales or in non-Transformer architectures. The paper also notes that the theoretical prediction that GNS should vary with batch size is not borne out empirically, suggesting the McCandlish et al. model may need refinement.
