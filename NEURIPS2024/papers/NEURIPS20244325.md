## Problem

Evaluating causal effect estimators requires ground-truth counterfactual outcomes, but existing simulators are either hand-designed and too simplistic to reflect real-world complexity, or purely data-driven and lacking the structural causal constraints needed to reason about identification strategies. The paper asks how to build simulators that are both realistic (fit to real data) and structurally rich (with explicit causal graphs) to create genuinely challenging estimation benchmarks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244325/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The simulator takes a cross-sectional tabular dataset (Adult, 30,162 samples × 14 variables) plus a user-specified causal DAG for initial states and transitions, and a hand-designed intervention variable (studies, with 4 categorical levels). It produces sequential trajectories of all variables over T=7 time steps, along with paired counterfactual outcome samples (all-treated and all-control) for the same subjects, yielding the IncomeSCM-1.0.CATE benchmark of 50,000 i.i.d. observation sequences. The key structural detail is that the input is a single time-point snapshot while the output is a multi-year time series, with the intervention applied at t=2 and the outcome measured at t=7.

## Method

IncomeSCM is a discrete-time Markov structural causal model where each variable has a separate initial-state sampler (fitted to the Adult data via regression or classification on its causal parents) and a transition sampler (hand-designed, typically a stochastic blend of the previous value and a draw from the fitted initial-state model). The core insight is that composing simple, hand-crafted transitions over multiple time steps yields causal effects that are complex, non-trivial functions of the initial state, even though each individual transition is easy to specify. The intervention variable 'studies' is constructed (not present in Adult) with a logistic-regression initial-state distribution and hand-designed transitions that affect education, income, and future study propensity. Three estimation tasks are defined: ATE/CATE with full pre-intervention covariates, ATE/CATE with only the direct causes of the intervention (minimal adjustment set), and CATE conditioned on education alone (an invalid adjustment set requiring the full set for identification). The paper then benchmarks T-learners, S-learners, IPW, DML, and nearest-neighbor matching estimators across these tasks, showing that methods with comparable factual fit (R²≈0.80, AUC≈0.98) can differ substantially in causal effect quality (best CATE R² only 0.30).

## Results

On the IncomeSCM-1.0.CATE benchmark (n=50,000, 6-year horizon), the best CATE estimator (T-learner with Random Forest) achieves an R² of only 0.30 on Task 1 (full adjustment set) and 0.16 on Task 2 (minimal adjustment set), while the best ATE estimator (T-learner with XGBoost) achieves an absolute error of just $207 against the ground-truth ATE of $40,919. Despite strong factual fit (propensity AUC ≈ 0.98, outcome R² ≈ 0.80), all methods struggle substantially with causal estimation. IPW estimators perform worst, with IPW(RF) producing ATE errors exceeding $57,000, while IPW-W(LR) is the best IPW variant at $4,878 AE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks estimators from two main families: meta-learners (T-learner and S-learner, Künzel et al. 2019) and double/debiased machine learning (DML, Chernozhukov et al. 2018), each with Ridge, Random Forest, and XGBoost backbones. T-learners consistently outperform S-learners on CATE R² (best: 0.30 vs. 0.15) and ATE AE (best: 207 vs. 400), with T-learner(XGB) achieving the lowest ATE error by a wide margin. DML is highly inconsistent: DML(XGB) achieves R² = −1.13 on Task 2 (minimal adjustment), indicating it explains less variance than a constant, while DML(Linear) reaches R² = 0.21 on the same task. No single estimator dominates across all three tasks, and the best factual model (S-learner RF, R² CV = 0.84) does not yield the best causal estimates, underscoring that standard model-selection criteria based on observational fit are insufficient for causal inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by proposing a generalizable strategy for converting cross-sectional tabular data into sequential structural causal models (SCMs) for causal estimation benchmarks, blending data-driven initial-state fitting with hand-designed transition mechanisms. It departs from purely synthetic simulators (ACIC challenges), semi-synthetic approaches (IHDP, Twins), and fully data-driven simulators (Medkit-Learn) by imposing explicit causal structure while still fitting complex initial-state distributions to real data. The causal graph for the Adult dataset follows the analyst-constructed DAG of Nabi and Shpitser (2018), and the partially data-driven design echoes the unpublished ACIC 2022 challenge, but the paper is the first to publish and open-source this specific strategy applied to a well-known tabular dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark demonstrates that methods with comparable factual fit (R² ≈ 0.80, AUC ≈ 0.98) can produce causal effect estimates of vastly different quality, with best CATE R² only around 0.30 and no single method dominating across tasks. This concretely motivates the need for dedicated causal estimators and model selection criteria beyond standard predictive performance. Key limitations include the fixed horizon (no censoring or dropout), absence of missing values, and a single hyperparameter configuration for initial-state samplers, all of which limit external validity. The generalizable strategy—fit initial states to data, hand-design transitions, compose for complexity—opens a path to creating domain-specific causal benchmarks from any cross-sectional dataset, though its applicability to systems where transitions are as complex as initial states remains unexplored.
