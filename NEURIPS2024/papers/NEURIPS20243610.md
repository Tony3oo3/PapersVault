## Problem

NeRF suffers from rendering defects in complex scenes with heavy occlusions because rays that are invisible to a 3D point still contribute gradients during training, causing interference. Existing multi-NeRF methods either partition the scene in the point dimension (ignoring per-ray visibility) or rely on manually defined ray allocation rules that require prior scene knowledge, limiting their applicability to diverse scene types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243610/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard multi-view RGB images with known camera poses, from which rays are parameterized by their origin and direction. The gate module takes only the ray origin and direction (o, d) as input to produce per-sub-NeRF gating scores, requiring no additional scene annotations. The output is a fused rendered novel-view image (post-volume-rendering weighted combination of K sub-NeRF renderings) and a corresponding depth map, with the depth also serving as an intermediate self-supervision signal during training.

## Method

Rad-NeRF constructs an ensemble of K sub-NeRFs sharing a single multiresolution hash-encoding feature grid while maintaining independent MLP decoders, so that spatial encoding benefits from cross-ray weight sharing while view-dependent rendering is decoupled per sub-NeRF. A soft gate module (4-layer MLP + Softmax) takes each ray's origin and direction and outputs continuous gating scores that weight the post-volume-rendering fusion of sub-NeRF outputs, enabling end-to-end learnable ray allocation without manual rules. A depth-based mutual learning loss regularizes each sub-NeRF's rendered depth toward the gate-guided fused depth of all sub-NeRFs, acting as geometric regularization that reduces depth ambiguity and overfitting. A coefficient-of-variation (CV) balancing loss on the gating scores prevents the gate from collapsing onto a single sub-NeRF, and the entire system is trained jointly with a standard color rendering loss plus the two regularization terms.

## Results

On the TAT dataset, Rad-NeRF achieves 21.708 PSNR / 0.672 SSIM / 0.398 LPIPS, outperforming Instant-NGP (20.722 PSNR) by ~1.0 dB and Block-NGP (20.783 PSNR) by ~0.9 dB. On NeRF-360-v2, Rad-NeRF reaches 27.871 PSNR, surpassing F2-NeRF (26.393) by 1.5 dB and Switch-NGP (26.524) by 1.3 dB. On the Free-Dataset, Rad-NeRF obtains 26.449 PSNR, beating Instant-NGP (25.951) and Block-NGP (26.015). The method adds only 0.2% extra parameters over Instant-NGP while delivering consistent gains across all three complex-scene benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rad-NeRF consistently outperforms all fast-training single-NeRF baselines (Instant-NGP, DVGO) and all multi-NeRF baselines (Switch-NGP, Block-NGP, Mega-NGP) across TAT, NeRF-360-v2, and Free-Dataset, with the largest margin over Switch-NGP (+1.35 dB on NeRF-360-v2). Against F2-NeRF, Rad-NeRF wins by 1.48 dB on NeRF-360-v2 and 0.13 dB on Free-Dataset. The only method that beats Rad-NeRF on these three datasets is MipNeRF360 (by 0.35–0.86 dB), but MipNeRF360 requires nearly one day of training versus Rad-NeRF's under-one-hour budget. On the MaskTAT object dataset, Rad-NeRF (29.774 PSNR) surpasses both Instant-NGP (28.752) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (27.363). On ScanNet indoor scenes, Rad-NeRF (28.870 PSNR) again beats 3DGS (26.781) and Instant-NGP (28.074), with the gap over 3DGS widening due to 3DGS's sensitivity to texture-less regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

Rad-NeRF extends the Instant-NGP hash-encoding framework into a multi-NeRF ensemble with a learnable soft gate module that allocates training rays to sub-NeRFs, drawing on the mixture-of-experts paradigm but operating at the ray level rather than the point level. It departs from prior ray-based multi-NeRF methods (Block-NeRF, Mega-NeRF) that require manually defined allocation rules tied to specific scene types, and from point-based methods (NeRF++, Switch-NeRF, F2-NeRF) that ignore the visibility of regions to different rays. The depth-based mutual learning loss is a novel regularization that leverages inter-sub-NeRF depth consistency as geometric supervision, complementing the standard color rendering loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that ray-wise training decoupling is a more parameter-efficient scaling dimension for NeRF than increasing MLP width or feature grid size, achieving up to 1.5 dB PSNR gain with only 0.2% extra parameters. This offers a practical recipe for improving complex-scene rendering without prohibitive compute costs. However, the method's reliance on sufficient training data limits its applicability to few-shot settings, and the fixed number of sub-NeRFs (default 2) is not yet adaptive to scene complexity. Open questions include integration with 3D Gaussian Splatting and generalizable NeRF frameworks, where ray-related predictions could enable similar decoupling.
