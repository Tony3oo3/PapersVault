## Problem

Current navigation systems implement semantic routing (multi-objective, natural-language-specified route planning) through ad hoc ensembles of classical graph algorithms like cost modifiers and shortest paths, which only support limited linear criteria and cannot scale to rich, open-ended queries. The paper identifies two gaps: the absence of a large-scale benchmark for evaluating semantic routing systems, and the lack of a scalable learning-based approach that can handle road networks with 10,000+ vertices.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a free-form natural language query (e.g., 'find a bakery and car repair shop, avoiding highways'), a source-destination edge pair, and a 512-edge receptive field — a local neighborhood around the head of the partially-constructed route — rather than the full 10,000-node road graph. Each edge is encoded via a 2-layer MLP over its metadata (road type, speed limit, colocated businesses, POI categories) rather than a learned embedding, enabling generalization to unseen cities. The output is a complete navigation route as a sequence of road segments, generated one edge at a time.

## Method

The core contribution is an autoregressive decomposition of semantic routing into next-edge prediction subproblems: a decoder-only transformer is trained with cross-entropy loss on 3M (query, graph, optimal route) tuples, where routes are truncated at random positions to create prediction targets. At each step, the model receives the user query, destination, partial route, candidate next edges, and a 512-edge receptive field, reducing memory footprint by over 2,000× compared to processing the full graph. Inference uses width-10 beam search followed by a secondary 2-layer MLP scoring model (trained contrastively with ground-truth routes as positives and model-sampled routes as negatives) to select the final path. This local-neighborhood autoregressive strategy is the key mechanism that makes standard transformer architectures viable on large-scale road networks where full-graph GNNs are impractical.

## Results

On the Semantic Routing Benchmark (evaluated on previously unseen queries in a previously unseen city, Berkeley, CA), the autoregressive transformer achieves a 92.0% ± 3.7% success rate on waypoint routing with errands, 38.0% ± 6.6% on waypoint routing with specific locations, 34.0% ± 6.9% on mixed waypoint routing, and 68% ± 4% on trip planning. The median excess penalty over the optimal route score is only 0.8 seconds for successfully completed mixed waypoint routing problems, with a median total travel time of 509 seconds. These results are obtained using beam search (width 10) with a secondary scoring model, on a 512-edge receptive field.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two classical semantic routing methods on the waypoint routing task. The cost modifiers method (Delling et al., 2009), which rescales edge weights based on user preferences and runs shortest paths, achieves a 0% ± 0% success rate — it is provably unable to solve waypoint routing problems since the task cannot be decomposed into per-edge costs. The electrical flows method (Sinop et al., 2021), which samples 4,096 diverse candidate routes and checks if any is successful, achieves only 1.3% ± 1.3% success rate due to the intractably large search space. The autoregressive transformer dramatically outperforms both baselines, achieving 92.0% on errand-based waypoint routing, 38.0% on location-based, and 34.0% on mixed waypoint routing — representing improvements of 34–92 percentage points over the best classical baseline. The paper notes these baselines were given 'unfair advantages' (e.g., brute-force search over all cost modifier schemes for the cost modifiers method), making the gap even more striking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of classical route planning, graph learning, and autoregressive modeling. It departs from the industry paradigm of ad hoc cost modifiers and combinatorial ensembles (Delling et al., 2009; Ratliff et al., 2009) by reframing semantic routing as a supervised learning problem, and departs from prior GNN-based combinatorial optimization work (Kool et al., 2018; Veličković et al., 2019) by showing those methods are intractable at road-network scale (10,000+ nodes vs. the ~100 nodes they were tested on). The key conceptual move is repurposing the autoregressive next-token-prediction paradigm from language modeling to next-edge prediction, combined with a local receptive field that reduces the input from the full 25,000-edge graph to a 512-edge neighborhood—effectively treating routing as a sequence generation problem over a sliding window rather than a global graph reasoning problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The released benchmark (1M+ natural language queries over 375K miles of OpenStreetMap road networks in 23 US cities, Apache 2.0 licensed) is the first large-scale public platform for evaluating semantic routing systems and establishes the first non-trivial baseline where classical methods score near zero. The autoregressive decomposition with receptive fields provides a general, practical recipe for scaling any sequential decision-making model to large graphs, a principle likely transferable beyond routing. Open questions include handling longer trips (currently capped at 600 segments), richer graph representations via GNNs or graph transformers, alternative unrolling strategies (destination-first, middle-out), and whether the simple transformer architecture is truly optimal given the observed saturation in scaling gains.
