## Problem

Existing GCIL methods preserve information from old models or graph data to mitigate catastrophic forgetting, but there is no clear theoretical understanding of what specifically matters in information preservation. The paper identifies that model divergence manifests as node-level semantic shifts and graph-level structural shifts, and asks which graph information, when preserved, can calibrate both.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240824/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a replayed memory subset of old graph nodes (with their features and adjacency), the frozen old model parameters, and the current new graph with its node features and adjacency matrix. It produces updated GNN parameters that classify all old and new classes. Unusually, the preservation losses are computed by running the same replayed nodes through both the old (frozen) and new (training) models, then comparing their spatial-domain decomposed representations rather than raw outputs or logits.

## Method

GSIP decomposes graph information into low-frequency local-global and high-frequency components in the spatial domain (via Lemma 1's graph convolution factorization) and preserves each with a dedicated loss. Low-frequency local preservation aligns old node representations with new representations of the node and its first-hop neighbors via MSE; low-frequency global preservation matches mean-pooled old and new representations; high-frequency preservation uses KL divergence to match softmax-normalized neighbor-pair distance distributions between old and new models. The theoretical grounding (Proposition 1) shows that maximizing mutual information between old and new graph information is upper-bounded by the squared Euclidean distance, justifying the MSE-based objectives. The framework is plug-and-play: it adds a weighted graph information preservation loss (L_gip) to the standard node classification and replay losses, and can be combined with any existing replay-based GCIL method (ERGNN, SSM, CaT) without architectural changes.

## Results

GSIP combined with ERGNN achieves the most dramatic gains: up to +46.76% AP and +57.94% AF on CoraFull (Equally 10), and +14.22% AP / +17.17% AF on Reddit (Unequally). On large-scale datasets, GSIP delivers approximately 10% improvement in the forgetting metric (AF) over prior replay methods. CaT-GSIP attains the highest absolute performance, reaching 97.55% AP on Reddit (Equally 2) and 81.10% AP on CoraFull (Equally 2), approaching or even surpassing the Joint upper bound (e.g., 68.80 AP vs. 63.5 Joint on Arxiv Equally 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Pure regularization methods (EWC, GEM, MAS, LwF, TWP) all fail severely on graph data, achieving AP below 25% on CoraFull and AF below −70%, confirming that parameter-level preservation without topology awareness is insufficient for GCIL. The hybrid method SSRM improves over regularization but still lags far behind replay-based approaches (e.g., 63.62 AP vs. 60.91 for ERGNN on CoraFull Unequally, but only −16.24 AF vs. −19.47). GSIP's largest gains come when combined with ERGNN: on CoraFull Equally(10), AP jumps from 24.39 to 71.15 (+46.76) and AF from −69.31 to −11.37 (+57.94), and on Reddit Unequally, AP rises from 76.60 to 90.82 (+14.22). SSM-GSIP provides consistent but more modest improvements (typically +0.5 to +5% AP, +1 to +8% AF), as SSM already preserves structural information via subgraph replay. CaT-GSIP shows the smallest relative gains (+0.5 to +2.6% AP) since CaT already achieves near-Joint performance, but it still improves AF in most settings and occasionally surpasses Joint (e.g., 68.80 AP vs. 63.5 Joint on Arxiv Equally(2)).

## Relation to Prior Work

GSIP builds on the graph spatial information factorization of Wu et al. (AAAI 2023), which decomposes graph convolution into low-frequency (neighborhood aggregation) and high-frequency (neighborhood difference) spatial components, and on the variational information distillation framework of Ahn et al. (CVPR 2019), which provides a mutual-information upper bound that reduces to MSE matching. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior GCIL methods that preserve model parameters (EWC, TWP), output distributions (LwF, SSRM), or replayed data (ERGNN, SSM, CaT) by arguing that the *graph information* encoded in node representations—decomposed into spatial frequency bands—is the fundamental quantity whose preservation calibrates both semantic and structural shifts. This reframes information preservation in GCIL from a model-centric to a signal-centric perspective, making GSIP a plug-in module rather than a standalone method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical justification for why replay-based GCIL methods work: preserving graph information (via spatial frequency decomposition) is sufficient to bound the mutual information loss between old and new models, thereby reducing catastrophic forgetting. The modular design means practitioners can adopt GSIP alongside any existing replay strategy without architectural changes, and the finding that even 10 replay nodes per task suffice (vs. 400) suggests significant memory savings. However, the theoretical bound is loose (MSE as a special case of a variational bound), evaluations are limited to node classification on citation/social networks up to ~230K nodes, and it remains open whether the spatial frequency decomposition generalizes to other graph tasks (link prediction, graph classification) or to dynamic/streaming graph settings where topology itself evolves.
