## Problem

Commonly used Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) such as QAOA and UCCSD are not natively designed to handle hard constraints, typically resorting to soft penalty terms in the Hamiltonian that are inefficient and sensitive to penalty-weight tuning. Additionally, there is no established mechanism to integrate quantum error correction techniques (like parity checks from LDPC codes) directly into variational ansatz circuits for both error mitigation and constraint enforcement. This paper addresses both gaps by showing how parity checks can be seamlessly embedded into Hamming Weight Preserving (HWP) ansatz circuits.

## Input / Output

For quantum chemistry, the method takes a molecular Hamiltonian (STO-3G basis, Jordan-Wigner transformed) and outputs the ground state energy, operating within an HWP subspace of dimension d_k = C(n,k) where n is the number of orbitals and k the number of electrons. For QAP, the input is a pair of m×m symmetric matrices (flow F and distance D) mapped onto m² working qubits plus m ancilla qubits arranged in a 2D lattice matching the superconducting processor topology; the output is the optimal permutation matrix. For TSP (a special case of QAP), the input is a single m×m distance matrix and the output is the minimum-cost Hamiltonian cycle. The QAP loss is computed exclusively on in-constraint states where all ancilla qubits measure |1⟩, with the probability of obtaining such states scaling as m!/m^m ≈ √(2πm)/e^m.

## Method

The paper proposes a unified framework combining HWP ansatz with parity checks in two complementary roles. First, as error mitigation: cascades of CNOT gates (parity check blocks) are inserted at regular intervals between HWP layers to detect and correct bit-flip errors, leveraging the fact that HWP gates guarantee constant Hamming weight in intermediate states. Second, as hard constraint enforcement for QAP: HWP layers are applied along rows of the qubit lattice (ensuring one |1⟩ per row), while parity check projective measurements on ancilla qubits enforce the column constraint (one |1⟩ per column), restricting the state to the m!-dimensional QAP subspace. The authors introduce the NBS gate, a two-qubit HWP gate whose Dynamical Lie Algebra dimension reaches d_k² (or d_k²/2 − 1 when n=2k) under nearest-neighbor connectivity, making it nearly universal and simpler than prior HWP gates. The overall circuit alternates L HWP layers with T parity check blocks, and the loss is evaluated only on post-measurement states where all ancilla qubits read |1⟩, ensuring optimization occurs strictly within the feasible subspace without penalty terms.

## Results

On QAP (the primary task), the proposed NBS-HARD method achieves an approximation ratio η of 1.0000 (m=3), 0.9991 (m=4), 0.9919 (m=5), and 0.9826 (m=6), with optimal-solution probabilities p_optimal of 1.0000, 0.9448, 0.8500, and 0.5000 respectively — consistently outperforming all soft-constraint baselines that degrade to η < 0.70 and p_optimal < 0.12 at m=4. On ground-state energy estimation, the universal NBS HWP ansatz achieves energy errors below 10⁻¹⁰ Ha for H₂ and LiH (noiseless), surpassing UCCSD (7.62×10⁻⁴ for LiH) and UCCSDT (2.79×10⁻⁵ for H₂O) with far fewer gates (441 vs. 1067/5871 for LiH/H₂O). On the superconducting quantum processor (m=3 QAP), NBS-HARD outperforms all soft-constraint methods on η_in and p_in metrics, with the topology-aware design avoiding SWAP gates entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HEA [46] and QAOA [27] (both soft-constraint, full 2ⁿ search space), NBS-HARD dominates on every metric: at m=4, NBS-HARD achieves η=0.9991 and p_optimal=0.9448 versus HEA's η=0.0000/p_optimal=0.0000 and QAOA's η=0.5491/p_optimal=0.0000. Against XYmixer [39] in its hard-constraint form (XYmixer-HARD), NBS-HARD wins on p_optimal for all m≥4 (0.9448 vs. 0.8498 at m=4; 0.5000 vs. 0.3500 at m=6) and on η for m≥5, confirming the superior expressivity of the NBS gate (full DLA dimension d_k²) over the XYmixer gate. In the quantum chemistry domain, the NBS HWP ansatz outperforms UCCSD [67] on noiseless energy (error <10⁻¹⁰ vs. 7.62×10⁻⁴ for LiH) and UCCSDT [41] on H₂O (1.59×10⁻¹⁰ vs. 2.79×10⁻⁵) while using 441 gates versus 1067 (UCCSD) or 5871 (UCCSDT). Under simulated noise with error mitigation, the combined SV+PC strategy for NBS-HWP yields lower energy error than SV alone for all three molecules (e.g., 0.601 vs. 0.732 Ha for LiH), whereas SV with deep UCCSD/UCCSDT circuits becomes ineffective (p≈0.5, near-random outputs). On the superconducting processor, NBS-HARD consistently outperforms all soft-constraint baselines on η_in and p_in, with the topology-matched design (no SWAP gates) providing a practical advantage over full-connectivity methods like QAOA and XYmixer-FC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the authors' prior work on HWP ansatz [78] (ICLR 2024), which established the expressivity and trainability of symmetry-preserving circuits, and on quantum LDPC/surface code parity check mechanisms [53, 29] from quantum error correction. The key departure is repurposing parity check from its conventional error-correction role into a dual-purpose tool: in-circuit error mitigation for HWP ansatz and projective measurement for enforcing hard constraints beyond what HWP alone can express. For QAP specifically, this contrasts with prior quantum approaches that rely on soft penalty terms [49, 50, 21] or classical post-processing [80], and extends the XYmixer-based hard constraint paradigm [39] with a topology-aware mapping to physical qubit lattices that avoids SWAP gates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuinely open problem in VQAs—how to natively incorporate hard constraints without penalty terms—and positions the HWP + parity check combination as a leading candidate for demonstrating quantum advantage in the NISQ era. However, practical impact is currently limited: hardware experiments are restricted to m=3 (12 qubits) due to coherence constraints, and the probability of obtaining feasible states decreases exponentially with problem size (Eq. 17). Open questions include extending to inequality constraints, scaling to larger instances as hardware improves, and whether the topology-aware mapping generalizes to other constrained problems beyond QAP/TSP.
