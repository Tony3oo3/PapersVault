## Problem

Decoder-only Transformers, the backbone of modern LLMs, exhibit surprising failures on simple tasks like counting and copying, yet prior theoretical analyses relied on assumptions (infinite precision, hard attention) that do not match practical deployments. This paper asks: what information can actually be contained in the final-token representation used for next-token prediction, and how do architectural choices (causal masking, softmax normalization, finite floating-point precision) fundamentally limit it?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243113/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a sequence of n token embeddings (v^(0) ∈ R^{n×d}) fed through a Pre-LN decoder-only Transformer with L layers and bounded positional encodings, and focuses exclusively on the final token's representation y_n at the last layer—the vector that drives next-token prediction. The outputs are (i) a representational collapse bound showing two sequences differing only by a repeated last token become indistinguishable below machine precision as n grows, (ii) a path-sum upper bound on ∂y_n/∂v_i^(0) that quantifies over-squashing as a function of token position and attention weights, and (iii) an impossibility result for counting under softmax normalization. Empirically, the authors measure L_∞ distances between final-layer representations in Gemma 7B and evaluate Gemini 1.5 on copying and counting prompts of varying sequence length.

## Method

The paper performs a signal propagation analysis of the last token's representation through a Pre-LN Transformer, using the total variation (L_1 distance) between softmax attention distributions as the key analytical tool. Theorem 4.2 proves representational collapse: when positional encodings decay with distance, two sequences that differ only by a repeated final token have final-layer representations whose L_1 distance vanishes as sequence length grows, and low-precision formats (bf16) make this practically catastrophic at moderate lengths. Theorem 5.1 derives an over-squashing bound by recursively differentiating through layers, showing that sensitivity ∂y_n/∂v_i^(0) is upper-bounded by a sum over all weighted paths from token i to the final token, with earlier tokens having exponentially more paths and thus more preserved information. Proposition 6.1 shows that without positional encodings and causal masking, softmax normalization makes the representation depend only on token ratios, rendering counting impossible; Corollary 6.2 extends this to an impossibility result under finite precision. The authors validate these results on Gemma 7B (measuring representation distances) and Gemini 1.5 (copying/counting accuracy), and propose simple mitigations: inserting delimiter tokens to prevent collapse and adopting local sliding-window attention to limit softmax spread.

## Results

The paper provides theoretical proofs (Theorem 4.2, Theorem 5.1, Corollary 6.2) establishing that decoder-only Transformers suffer from representational collapse and over-squashing, rather than reporting standard benchmark metrics. Empirically, Gemini 1.5 fails to copy the last element of a sequence of ones once the sequence reaches ~300 tokens, while copying the first element remains reliable. In Gemma 7B, the L1 distance between representations of sequences differing only by a repeated final token reaches machine precision (bf16) at a sequence length of approximately 50 for constant sequences, and adding commas every third digit keeps representations well-separated. For counting tasks, Gemini 1.5 outputs '100' as the most frequent answer for sequences of length ≥100, suggesting crude subitising rather than mechanical counting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis and empirical validation paper rather than a method comparison study, so no direct baseline comparisons are made. The empirical evidence is structured as a series of diagnostic experiments on Gemini 1.5 and Gemma 7B that confirm the theoretical predictions: (1) copying the last token of a constant sequence degrades at ~300 tokens while copying the first token does not, consistent with the over-squashing bound in Theorem 5.1; (2) providing hints ('It's not necessarily a 1, check carefully') or interleaving 0s and 1s significantly improves copying performance, matching the prediction that attention distribution and token diversity affect information propagation; (3) representational collapse is directly measured in Gemma 7B's internal representations, with the L1 distance between final-token representations of sequences of length n and n+1 (differing only by a repeated last token) converging to zero. The paper also shows that local sliding window attention (as in Gemma 2) addresses the root cause by limiting softmax spread, and that the phenomenon persists across multiple positional encoding schemes (RoPE, sinusoidal, ALiBi, absolute).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges the well-studied GNN over-squashing literature (Di Giovanni et al. 2024; Topping et al. 2022) to decoder-only Transformers, adapting the partial-derivative sensitivity analysis and commute-time framework to the causal attention setting. It deliberately departs from prior Transformer theory that assumes infinite precision or hard attention (Pérez et al. 2021; Weiss et al. 2021), instead analyzing the practical regime of soft attention with finite floating-point arithmetic. The representational collapse result connects to Peng et al. (2024) on finite-precision limitations, while the over-squashing bound draws on vanishing-gradient analyses for RNNs (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997; Pascanu et al. 2013), positioning the causal mask as an analogous topological bottleneck.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for well-known LLM failures on counting and copying, showing these are not merely training deficiencies but architectural impossibilities under finite precision. The representational collapse theorem implies no amount of additional training data or compute can fix the issue for sufficiently long sequences, which has direct implications for how the field should think about LLM reliability. Open questions include a precise mathematical treatment of RoPE's decay properties, how to directly measure over-squashing in practice, and whether sliding-window attention (as in Gemma 2) fully resolves the problem at scale. The work also raises the broader question of whether the causal attention topology is fundamentally suboptimal for tasks requiring uniform sensitivity across all input positions.
