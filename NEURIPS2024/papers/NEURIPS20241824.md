## Problem

Existing privacy auditing methods for ML models require retraining the target model multiple times, full access to the training data, control over the training pipeline, or in-distribution non-member data obtained by withholding samples from training. This makes post-hoc privacy measurement infeasible for individual data contributors (e.g., a hospital in federated learning) who only know their own subset of the training data and cannot modify the already-trained model. The paper asks: can we estimate the privacy loss of a trained model with respect to a known member subset, using only that subset and no retraining?

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained target model f (black-box access to its loss on any input) and a subset D_f of its training data, with no requirement for non-member data, full training set access, or training pipeline control. It outputs a privacy leakage estimate tilde-epsilon and a lower bound c_lb on the generator's c-closeness parameter. The measurement semantics are: PANORAMIA could not reject c-closeness for the generator, and if that claim is tight, then f cannot be the output of a tilde-epsilon-DP mechanism — a weaker guarantee than a strict lower bound on epsilon-DP, but practically useful when the baseline is strong.

## Method

PANORAMIA operates in two phases: (1) train a generative model G (StyleGAN2 for images, fine-tuned GPT-2 for text) on a subset D_G of the known member data to synthesize non-member samples, and (2) train a loss-based MIA classifier on member data D_in and generated non-member data D_out, comparing its precision-recall curve against a baseline classifier b that sees only the data points without access to f. The key theoretical contribution is a c-closeness definition for the generator that handles the lack of independence between members and non-members (since non-members come from G ≠ D), enabling a joint hypothesis test that simultaneously bounds c (generator quality) and c+ε (total distinguishability). The baseline is strengthened with a helper model h trained on synthetic data to supply loss-based features, isolating the privacy leakage signal from the synthetic-data-detection signal. The final measurement tilde-ε = {c+ε}_lb − c_lb is extracted by finding the recall level that maximizes the lower bound, with a union bound over all recall thresholds for valid simultaneous inference.

## Results

PANORAMIA measures privacy leakage (ε̃) that is consistently lower than but comparable to the O(1) audit and RM;RN (real non-member) baselines across all target models. On the most overfitted CIFAR-10 model (ResNet101_E100), PANORAMIA detects ε̃=1.16±0.19, close to RM;RN (ε=1.22±0.19) and O(1) (ε=1.41±0.18). On GPT-2_E150 (WikiText), PANORAMIA measures ε̃=2.40±0.53 versus O(1)'s ε=5.73±0.08. For DP ResNet18 models, PANORAMIA correctly detects monotonically increasing leakage as ε grows (ε̃=0.00 at ε=1,2; 0.26 at ε=10; 1.13 at ε=15; 1.43 at ε=∞), matching O(1)'s qualitative trend.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PANORAMIA is compared against two stronger baselines: O(1) (Steinke et al., 2023), which requires controlling training data inclusion and access to real non-member data, and RM;RN, a hybrid using real non-members with PANORAMIA's ML-based MIA. O(1) consistently measures higher privacy loss than PANORAMIA (e.g., ε=1.41 vs. ε̃=1.16 on ResNet101_E100; ε=5.73 vs. ε̃=2.40 on GPT-2_E150), which is expected since O(1) has access to real non-members and can exploit non-member detection. The gap narrows for more overfitted models, confirming PANORAMIA's measurements track true leakage. On DP models, both PANORAMIA and O(1) fail to detect any leakage at ε=1 and ε=2, and both show monotonically increasing detected leakage as ε grows. PANORAMIA's key advantage is that it requires no retraining, no real non-member data, and no control over the training pipeline, while still detecting meaningful privacy loss. With larger test sets (up to 35k), PANORAMIA can surpass O(1) on image models (2.62 vs. 2.23 on ResNet101_E100), though on text data it hits a power ceiling due to the one-sided c-closeness definition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the O(1) privacy auditing framework of Steinke et al. (2023), which requires one training run with randomly included/excluded auditing examples and in-distribution non-member data obtained by withdrawing real samples. PANORAMIA departs from this by replacing real non-members with synthetic data from a generative model (StyleGAN2 for images, fine-tuned GPT-2 for text) trained on a subset of the known member data, thereby eliminating the need for retraining, full training-set access, or control over the training pipeline. The theoretical contribution introduces 'c-closeness' as a formal generator-quality notion analogous to DP, and adapts the hypothesis-testing analysis to handle the non-independence between data points that arises when non-members are generated rather than independently sampled. The broader privacy auditing lineage (Jagielski et al. 2020, Carlini et al. 2022a, Nasr et al. 2023) typically requires repeated retraining or canary-based detection, which PANORAMIA explicitly avoids.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PANORAMIA enables individual data contributors (e.g., a hospital in federated learning, or a user of a text service) to measure the privacy leakage of their own known data in a final model without the model owner's cooperation or retraining, addressing a practical gap that prior auditing methods cannot fill. A key limitation is that the returned value tilde-epsilon is not a true lower-bound on epsilon-DP (it is {c+epsilon}_lb minus c_lb), which the authors acknowledge as the primary open problem; bounding c from above would yield a proper lower bound. The paper also highlights that the same non-member-data scarcity problem affects MIA evaluation on foundation models, where all in-distribution data is included in training, suggesting PANORAMIA's framework could inform future privacy benchmarks for such models. Empirically, PANORAMIA fails to detect meaningful leakage on tabular data and on strongly private DP models (epsilon=1,2), indicating the approach's sensitivity to generator quality and the inherent difficulty of auditing well-protected models.
