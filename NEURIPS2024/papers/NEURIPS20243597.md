## Problem

The Bahncard problem is a canonical online rent-or-buy problem, but the only prior learning-augmented algorithm (PDLA) was restricted to slotted time, uniform ticket prices, and required a complete predicted solution as input—making it impractical for real deployment. This paper addresses the general continuous-time setting with diverse ticket prices and shows that short-term cost predictions suffice to achieve both strong consistency and bounded robustness.

## Input / Output

At each regular request (t, p) when no valid Bahncard is held, the algorithm receives a single scalar prediction: the total regular ticket cost over the upcoming interval [t, t+T). The prediction error η is defined as the maximum absolute deviation between predicted and true T-future-cost across all such predictions. The algorithm outputs a binary Bahncard purchase decision at each regular request, and the paper derives the competitive ratio as a closed-form piecewise function of η, yielding 2/(1+β)-consistency and 1/β-robustness.

## Method

PFSUM purchases a Bahncard at a regular request (t, p) only when two conditions hold simultaneously: (i) the total ticket cost in the past window (t−T, t] is at least the break-even threshold γ = C/(1−β), and (ii) the predicted total ticket cost in the future window [t, t+T) is also at least γ. The key design insight is using past and future conditions as a logical AND rather than summing them (as in SUM_w), which prevents the algorithm from buying a Bahncard when the prediction is wildly wrong while still leveraging accurate predictions to improve over SUM. The competitive analysis employs a divide-and-conquer strategy over six structural patterns of time intervals where at least one of PFSUM or OPT holds a valid Bahncard, with a backtracking argument to handle the case where PFSUM buys one more Bahncard than OPT (Pattern VI). The resulting competitive ratio is continuous in η, increasing smoothly from 2/(1+β) at η=0 to 1/β as η→∞.

## Results

PFSUM achieves a competitive ratio of 2/(1+β) under perfect predictions (e.g., 1.11 for β=0.8, 1.25 for β=0.6) and degrades smoothly to a worst-case bound of 1/β (e.g., 1.25 for β=0.8, 1.67 for β=0.6) as prediction error η→∞. In experiments across commuter and occasional-traveler profiles with Uniform, Normal, and Pareto ticket price distributions (β=0.8, T=10, C=100 and β=0.6, T=5, C=100), PFSUM consistently achieves the lowest average cost ratio (ALG/OPT) over 100 runs for a wide range of perturbation probabilities, with the narrowest 95% confidence intervals among all algorithms. PFSUM's cost ratio rises gradually and smoothly with increasing prediction error, whereas SUM_w and FSUM deteriorate rapidly, and PDLA generally performs worse than even the prediction-free SUM baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PFSUM outperforms the prediction-free SUM algorithm (2−β-competitive) in both consistency and practical cost ratio: with perfect predictions, PFSUM's 2/(1+β) is strictly better than SUM's 2−β for all 0<β<1 (e.g., 1.11 vs. 1.2 at β=0.8). Against PDLA [12], PFSUM dominates decisively in experiments—PDLA 'generally has much higher cost ratios than PFSUM and even the conventional online algorithm SUM'—and PFSUM avoids PDLA's restrictive assumptions (slotted time, unit ticket prices, C→∞, full-solution prediction). Compared to FSUM (which shares PFSUM's 2/(1+β) consistency), PFSUM achieves bounded 1/β-robustness while FSUM has unbounded worst-case cost ratio; experiments confirm FSUM's cost ratio deteriorates rapidly with prediction error while PFSUM degrades smoothly. PFSUM also consistently outperforms SRL (adapted from ski-rental [4]) across all settings, particularly under high prediction errors where SRL's performance remains inferior to SUM. The intermediate SUM_w algorithm is shown to be strictly worse than SUM in consistency (≥(3−β)/(1+β) > 2−β) and has no bounded robustness, motivating PFSUM's design of using past and future costs as separate conditions rather than a sum.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Fleischer's optimal SUM algorithm for the Bahncard problem by incorporating short-term predictions of future ticket costs, departing fundamentally from Bamas et al.'s primal-dual approach (PDLA) which required slotted time, uniform ticket prices, and a complete predicted solution. The algorithm design draws conceptual inspiration from Wang et al.'s cloud instance reservation work, but the key departure is using past and future cost conditions *separately* (both must independently exceed the break-even threshold γ) rather than summing them, which the paper shows is critical for achieving bounded robustness. The competitive analysis introduces a divide-and-conquer technique over six temporal patterns with a backtracking argument for the most complex case, a methodological contribution distinct from the primal-dual framework used in prior learning-augmented Bahncard work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple, easily implementable threshold-based algorithm using only short-term cost predictions (a single scalar per decision point) can outperform both the classical optimal online algorithm and the more complex primal-dual learning-augmented approach, highlighting that problem-specific structure matters more than general-purpose frameworks. The finding that PDLA underperforms even the prediction-free SUM algorithm in experiments is a cautionary result for the broader learning-augmented algorithms community. Open questions include whether the competitive ratio is tight, whether the approach extends to multi-kind Bahncard or other rent-or-buy variants with different temporal structures, and whether the divide-and-conquer pattern analysis generalizes to other online problems with recurring purchase decisions.
