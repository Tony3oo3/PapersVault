## Problem

Prior work established that linear transformers trained on fixed-noise linear regression implement a form of preconditioned gradient descent (GD++), but their capability on more complex problems remained unexplored. This paper asks whether linear transformers can discover more sophisticated optimization algorithms when the problem requires handling varying noise levels across sequences, and what structural properties constrain what they can represent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241545/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of n feature-label pairs (x_i, y_i) with x_i ∈ R^d and y_i ∈ R, followed by a query token (x_t, 0), and outputs a scalar prediction ŷ = -y_{n+1}^L (the negated last coordinate of the final token in the last layer). Attention is causally restricted so the query token is invisible to other positions. Data is generated from a noisy linear regression model where each sequence shares a ground-truth weight vector w_τ ~ N(0,I) but can have a different noise variance σ_τ drawn from a distribution p(σ_τ), making the problem non-stationary across sequences.

## Method

The paper proves that every layer of a linear transformer maintains an implicit linear regression weight vector w^l, with layer updates interpretable as a complex variant of preconditioned gradient descent with momentum-like behavior (Theorem 4.1, Lemma 4.3–4.4). For the diagonal parameterization, each attention head reduces to four scalar parameters (ω_xx, ω_xy, ω_yx, ω_yy) governing information flow between features and labels across layers. The key empirical finding is that training on mixed noise variance (uniform or categorical σ_τ) causes linear transformers to discover an algorithm incorporating momentum-like terms (via ω_xy adapting step sizes based on residual norm) and adaptive rescaling (via ω_yy shrinking predictions proportional to estimated noise level), outperforming GD++ and closed-form ridge regression baselines. The authors further prove that GD++ itself is a second-order method with O(log κ + log log 1/ε) convergence (Theorem 5.1), and that a 2-layer diagonal transformer can exactly match ridge regression for two-level categorical noise in the n→∞ limit (Theorem 5.2).

## Results

On the mixed noise variance problem (σ_τ ~ U(0, σ_max)), 7-layer linear transformers (both FULL and DIAG) achieve adjusted evaluation loss of 0.047 at σ_max=5 and 0.057 at σ_max=7, substantially outperforming the best closed-form baseline TUNEDRR (0.049 and 0.068 respectively) and the ADARR estimator (0.068 and 0.092). For categorical noise σ_τ ∈ {1,3}, the 7-layer FULL model reaches 0.010 loss versus 0.021 for TUNEDRR and 0.051 for ADARR. GD++ performs nearly identically to the constant-noise baseline CONSTRR (e.g., 0.344 vs. 0.365 at σ_max=5), confirming it cannot adapt to varying noise levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares linear transformers against three ridge-regression-based baselines: CONSTRR (constant noise variance), ADARR (adaptive noise estimation via the unbiased estimator from Cherkassky & Ma, 2003), and TUNEDRR (ADARR with two additional tuned hyperparameters). Linear transformers (FULL and DIAG) consistently outperform all three baselines as noise variance increases: at σ_max=7, the 7-layer FULL model achieves 0.057 loss versus 0.068 for TUNEDRR and 0.092 for ADARR. For categorical noise {1,3,5}, FULL reaches 0.035 versus 0.054 for TUNEDRR. GD++ is essentially equivalent to CONSTRR across all settings (e.g., 0.530 vs. 0.530 at σ_max=7), revealing that GD++ implicitly estimates only a single constant noise level. The ADARR baseline (Cherkassky & Ma, 2003) performs well at low noise but degrades significantly at higher variances, while TUNEDRR partially compensates through tuned thresholds but still lags behind linear transformers by 15–30% in adjusted loss at moderate-to-high noise levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work showing that linear transformers implicitly perform gradient descent during in-context learning (von Oswald et al., 2023a; Ahn et al., 2023). While prior work characterized the learned algorithm as preconditioned gradient descent (GD++), this paper proves a stronger structural result: every layer maintains an implicit linear regression weight vector, and the multi-layer dynamics correspond to a complex variant of preconditioned GD with momentum-like behavior. The paper then departs from the fixed-noise setting studied previously by introducing mixed noise variance (inspired by Bai et al., 2023), a harder problem with no closed-form solution, where the transformer must implicitly estimate noise levels. A key practical departure is the analysis of diagonal attention matrices, showing they retain most of the representational power of full matrices for this task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that linear transformers spontaneously learn momentum and noise-adaptive step-size rescaling suggests that even minimal attention architectures can serve as algorithm discovery engines, potentially uncovering optimization strategies not obvious to human designers. The proof that GD++ achieves second-order convergence rates (Theorem 5.1) reframes a previously understood-as-first-order method and has implications for understanding what transformers can compute in few layers. However, the findings are confined to simplified linear regression with small dimensions (D=10, N=20) and stripped-down architectures without MLPs or LayerNorm, leaving open whether these algorithmic discoveries persist in the nonlinear, high-dimensional regime of practical transformers. The gap between the elegant diagonal-parameterization theory and the full-matrix empirical results (particularly for categorical noise) also remains unexplained.
