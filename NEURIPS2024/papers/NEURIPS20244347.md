## Problem

Offline imitation learning typically suffers from limited expert demonstrations that cover only a small fraction of the state-action space, while larger sets of sub-optimal demonstrations are more readily available. Existing approaches either overfit to the scarce expert data, inadvertently imitate sub-optimal trajectories, or assume expert-like state-action pairs can be extracted from the sub-optimal set—losing transition dynamics information and overlooking the sequential nature of decision-making. SPRINQL addresses this by formulating a Q-learning-based objective that leverages multiple ranked levels of sub-optimal demonstrations without requiring expert-like trajectories to be identifiable within them.

## Input / Output

The method takes N ranked offline demonstration sets (D^1 expert, D^2...D^N sub-optimal) as trajectories of state-action-next-state tuples, where the expert set is typically orders of magnitude smaller (e.g., 1k vs. 25k transitions for MuJoCo). It produces both a policy (state-to-action mapping via a soft actor-critic parameterization) and a recovered reward function, the latter being a unique advantage over BC-based baselines. The reference reward is automatically estimated from the ranked demonstrations using a Bradley-Terry preference model, and the per-level weights w_i are inferred from the learned reference reward values rather than set manually.

## Method

SPRINQL extends the inverse soft-Q learning framework of IQ-learn by adding two components to the MaxEnt IRL objective: (1) a weighted occupancy distribution matching term across N expertise levels, and (2) a reward regularization term that penalizes deviation from a learned reference reward assigning higher values to higher-expertise demonstrations. The reward regularizer breaks the convexity-in-Q and closed-form-policy properties of the original IQ-learn objective; SPRINQL restores these by constructing a concave lower-bound objective using a ReLU-based decomposition of the squared regularization term, guaranteeing a unique saddle point and non-adversarial training. A conservative Q-learning penalty (à la CQL) is incorporated to mitigate over-estimation in the offline setting. The reference reward is learned via a strictly convex loss combining within-group consistency and between-group Bradley-Terry preference ordering, and the per-level weights are automatically computed as normalized expected reference reward values per demonstration set.

## Results

SPRINQL achieves a normalized return (score) of 67.7 on average across six Mujoco and Panda-gym tasks with three expertise levels (N=3), substantially outperforming all baselines. The closest competitor, DWBC, reaches only 14.6 average, while TRAIL and DemoDICE score 3.9 and 7.9 respectively. On individual tasks, SPRINQL scores 73.6 (Cheetah), 77.0 (Ant), 82.9 (Humanoid), 72.0 (Push), 63.2 (PnP), and 37.7 (Slide), with the metric normalized so that 100 corresponds to expert-level performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPRINQL achieves state-of-the-art performance on all six evaluated tasks, outperforming every baseline by a wide margin. Against the strongest competitor DWBC (14.6 avg), SPRINQL leads by approximately 53 points on average, with the largest gaps on Mujoco tasks (e.g., 77.0 vs. 10.4 on Ant, 82.9 vs. 3.7 on Humanoid). TRAIL (3.9 avg) and DemoDICE (7.9 avg) struggle significantly, particularly on Mujoco locomotion tasks where they score near or below zero, indicating failure to learn meaningful policies. The BC-based baselines (BC-E, BC-O, BC-both, W-BC) achieve moderate scores (2.7–15.4 avg) but are far outperformed, confirming that simple behavior cloning cannot effectively leverage sub-optimal demonstrations. Notably, IQ-learn and SQIL variants (IQ-both, SQIL-both) perform worst when given all data, scoring negative averages (-8.6 and -12.6), suggesting that naively combining expert and sub-optimal data degrades Q-learning-based methods without the reward regularization that SPRINQL provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPRINQL extends the inverse soft-Q learning framework of IQ-learn (Garg et al., 2021) from a single expert level to multiple ranked expertise levels, while incorporating a reward regularization term estimated via a Bradley-Terry preference model. It departs from prior offline IL methods with sub-optimal demonstrations (DemoDICE, DWBC, TRAIL) that assume expert-like trajectories can be extracted from the supplementary set; instead, SPRINQL treats all sub-optimal data as informative for environment dynamics without requiring such extraction. The conservative Q-learning penalty from CQL (Kumar et al., 2020) is adapted to the inverse soft-Q objective to mitigate overestimation in the offline setting, and the MaxEnt IRL formulation (Ziebart et al., 2008) is generalized with weighted occupancy matching across N expertise levels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: in domains like healthcare and robotics, expert demonstrations are scarce while sub-optimal ones are abundant, yet prior methods either overfit to limited expert data or inadvertently imitate sub-optimal behavior. SPRINQL's ability to recover a reward function correlated with ground-truth rewards (beyond just a policy) opens the door to using the method for inverse RL, which BC-based approaches cannot do. Key limitations include the requirement for well-identified expertise levels and large sub-optimal datasets, and the absence of theoretical analysis on how dataset size ratios affect Q-learning performance—questions the authors flag as challenging open problems.
