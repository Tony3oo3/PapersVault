## Problem

In the nucleus accumbens, dopamine distributes reward prediction errors synchronously in regionally homogeneous concentrations, which does not support the sequential, layer-by-layer error propagation that backpropagation requires. It remains unclear whether such distributed error signals alone are sufficient for synapses to make coordinated updates and solve complex, nonlinear reward-based learning tasks.

## Input / Output

The agent receives flat state vectors from the environment (10×10×n pixel frames for MinAtar, continuous state vectors for DMC, and standard Gymnasium observations for classic control). Each AD cell independently produces a vector of Q-value predictions (one per action), and the final action is selected by averaging Q-values across all layers. A key structural constraint is that the number of tanh attention heads per cell scales linearly with the action space size, limiting scalability to very large discrete action spaces.

## Method

ARTIFICIAL DOPAMINE (AD) is a deep Q-learning algorithm that replaces backpropagation with synchronously distributed, per-layer TD errors, inspired by Forward-Forward's finding that layers can learn useful representations trained independently. Each AD cell computes hidden activations via a ReLU layer, then uses an attention-like mechanism—tanh weight layers (one per action) applied to the concatenated input—to produce a nonlinear Q-value prediction without any error propagation across layers. Upper layers communicate with lower layers via forward-in-time activation connections (sending their hidden state at t to the layer below at t+1), providing an information pathway that empirically improves performance without sequential error dependencies. Training follows standard DQN with experience replay and double Q-learning, but each layer's weights are updated independently using only its own local TD error, making all layer updates parallelizable.

## Results

AD achieves comparable episodic return to DQN on MinAtar (Breakout, Asterix, Freeway) and approaches the performance of SAC and TD-MPC2 on all five DMC tasks (Walker Walk, Walker Run, Hopper Hop, Cheetah Run, Reacher Hard), though with marginally lower sample efficiency. On classic control tasks, AD more consistently reaches the maximum 500 return in Cart Pole and achieves significantly higher return in Mountain Car than both DQN baselines. On supervised benchmarks, a 4-layer AD network (2000 units per layer) reaches 98.74% test accuracy on MNIST and 57.25% on CIFAR-10, with per-layer accuracy steadily increasing from layer 1 to layer 4, confirming inter-layer coordination without backpropagation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DQN on MinAtar, AD matches or slightly exceeds DQN on Breakout, Asterix, and Freeway, but DQN outperforms AD on the two hardest tasks (Seaquest and Space Invaders), where the forward-in-time connections and multi-layer coordination are most critical. On DMC, AD's episodic returns are close to or on par with both SAC and TD-MPC2 across all five tasks, though AD requires more environment steps to reach comparable performance (lower sample efficiency). On the four classic control environments, AD outperforms both CleanRL's DQN and ANGC's DQN in average episodic return across all tasks, with the largest gap in Mountain Car where AD achieves significantly higher return. The ablation results further show that removing forward-in-time connections causes devastating performance drops on Seaquest and Asterix, and a single-layer AD cell fails entirely on those tasks, confirming that the multi-layer coordination via distributed errors is essential rather than a single wide layer being sufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts Hinton's Forward-Forward (FF) algorithm—originally designed for binary classification via per-layer 'goodness' scores—to the regression task of Q-value prediction in deep RL, introducing an attention-like mechanism within each AD cell to achieve nonlinear regression without backpropagation. It inherits FF's core principles (independent per-layer errors, forward-in-time downward activation connections) and wraps them in a standard DQN training loop with experience replay and double Q-learning. The key departure from both FF and prior biologically-plausible RL work (e.g., ANGC) is the explicit framing of per-layer TD errors as a computational model of synchronously distributed dopamine in the nucleus accumbens, positioning the algorithm as a test of whether distributed errors alone can solve credit assignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first computational demonstration that synchronously distributed, per-layer TD errors—without any sequential error propagation between layers—can solve a range of complex discrete and continuous RL tasks at performance comparable to backpropagation-based methods. This has direct implications for understanding biological reward-based learning in the mesolimbic system and for the broader question of whether backpropagation is a necessary ingredient for deep credit assignment. Key open questions include whether the approach scales to large continuous action spaces (currently limited by per-action tanh heads), whether the hierarchical layer structure is truly necessary or an artifact of deep learning conventions, and how AD might integrate with other biologically plausible learning signals (Hebbian plasticity, active inference) to form a more complete model of brain learning.
