## Problem

Classic inverse optimization estimates a single point estimate of unknown parameters from decision data, but this point estimate can produce prescribed decisions with arbitrarily large actual and perceived optimality gaps, especially when the forward problem has multiple optimal solutions. The paper shows that the resulting decisions are misaligned with the decision maker's intuition, reducing algorithm adoption in prescriptive settings such as rideshare routing.

## Input / Output

The method takes a dataset of N historical decision-exogenous parameter pairs {x̂_k, u_k} where the unknown ground-truth parameters θ* and the DM's perceived parameters θ̂ are never directly observed—only the resulting decisions are available. The objective is assumed linear in θ and convex in x, with θ constrained to the unit sphere. The output is a decision policy x̄(u) generated by solving a robust forward optimization problem, together with a learned angular uncertainty set C(θ̄, α) that quantifies parameter uncertainty with a user-specified coverage level γ.

## Method

Conformal IO is a three-step pipeline: (1) split data into training and validation sets, (2) obtain a point estimate θ̄ via any existing IO method (e.g., sub-optimality loss or PFYL), and (3) calibrate the smallest angular uncertainty set C(θ̄, α) = {θ : ‖θ‖=1, θᵀθ̄ ≥ cos α} that covers at least γ(N_val+1) validation decisions. The calibration problem (CP) is solved by decomposing it into N_val independent subproblems that maximize the inner product θ_kᵀθ̄ subject to x̂_k being optimal under θ_k, then taking the τ-th largest value (Theorem 1). The learned set is then plugged into a robust forward optimization (RFO) problem min_x max_{θ∈C} f(θ,x) to prescribe decisions. The key theoretical result (Theorem 2) proves the uncertainty set is conservatively valid (coverage ≥ γ) and asymptotically exact via exchangeability and Rademacher complexity arguments, which in turn yields bounded AOG and POG (Theorem 3) independent of the exogenous parameter u.

## Results

On the shortest path problem (d=120), conformal IO improves AOG by 20.1–30.4% and POG by 15.0–23.2% over classic IO across confidence levels γ. On the knapsack problem (d=10), the improvements are larger: AOG by 40.3–57.0% and POG by 13.5–20.1%. In the theoretical Example 1, classic IO's AOG grows unboundedly with u (reaching 35 at u=100), while conformal IO maintains AOG=0 and POG≤0.70 across all u values. Training times are comparable (0.27s vs 0.18s for shortest path; 1.95s vs 2.47s for knapsack), with prediction time increasing from 0.01s (FO) to 0.44–0.63s (RFO).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares conformal IO against two classic IO baselines: (1) IO with the sub-optimality loss (Mohajerin Esfahani et al., 2018) and (2) the PFYL differentiable perturbed optimizer approach (Berthet et al., 2020). Conformal IO consistently outperforms both baselines on both AOG and POG across all tested confidence levels γ and both problem types (shortest path and knapsack). The improvement is orthogonal to the point estimation method used, meaning conformal IO improves regardless of whether the underlying point estimate comes from sub-optimality loss or PFYL. The gains are particularly dramatic for the knapsack problem, where AOG improves by up to 57% over classic IO. In the theoretical Example 1, classic IO's AOG and POG grow unboundedly as u increases, while conformal IO achieves AOG=0 and POG bounded by π/(2√2)≈1.11, with the theoretical bound closely tracking actual performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: stochastic inverse optimization (which estimates parameters from decision data), conformal prediction (which produces prediction sets with finite-sample coverage guarantees), and robust optimization (which hedges against parameter uncertainty). It departs from classic IO, which produces a point estimate and then solves the forward problem, by instead calibrating an angular uncertainty set around the point estimate using conformal prediction principles applied to inverse feasible sets. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from Sun et al. (2023), who calibrate uncertainty sets around ML predictions of the parameters (requiring parameter observations), and from distributionally robust IO (Mohajerin Esfahani et al., 2018), which the authors show does not resolve the core failure mode. The 'estimate, then optimize' framing of Elmachtoub et al. (2023) provides the broader context, but conformal IO is the first to use decision data (rather than parameter observations) to calibrate the uncertainty set and to jointly bound both ground-truth and perceived solution quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: algorithmic recommendations that are objectively optimal but perceived as poor by the decision maker will be rejected, undermining adoption in settings like rideshare routing or clinical planning. The demonstration that classic IO with the standard sub-optimality loss can yield arbitrarily large optimality gaps (both actual and perceived) as problem scale grows is a sobering result that challenges the implicit assumption that good parameter fit implies good decisions. Key limitations include the restriction to objectives linear in the unknown parameters, the computational overhead of solving the robust forward problem at prediction time, and the dependence on point-estimation quality. Open questions include extending to general convex objectives, accelerating RFO solution, and designing point-estimation methods that directly optimize downstream robust optimization performance.
