## Problem

Existing hallucination detection methods for LLMs—such as SelfCheckGPT, INSIDE, and RAG-based verifiers—rely on multiple stochastically sampled model responses or large external reference databases, incurring substantial computational overhead that limits real-time applicability. This paper asks whether hallucinations can be reliably identified within a single LLM response in both white-box and black-box settings without any additional training or inference-time generation overhead.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241077/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a prompt concatenated with a single model response (and optionally external reference passages in RAG settings), fed through an LLM via teacher-forcing in a single forward pass. In black-box settings where the original generating model is inaccessible, an auxiliary open-source LLM (e.g., Llama-2-7B) substitutes. The output is a suite of scalar detection scores—Hidden Score, Attention Score, Perplexity, Logit Entropy, and Windowed Logit Entropy—each computed per layer or per token window, which can be thresholded to yield a binary hallucination label or used as a continuous confidence signal.

## Method

LLM-Check extracts hallucination signals from three complementary sources within a single forward pass: (1) the mean log-determinant of the hidden-state covariance matrix (Hidden Score), computed via SVD of the d×m representation matrix; (2) the mean log-determinant of lower-triangular self-attention kernel maps (Attention Score), which exploits the fact that eigenvalues of a lower-triangular matrix equal its diagonal entries, eliminating the need for explicit eigen-decomposition; and (3) output-token uncertainty metrics including perplexity, top-k logit entropy, and a windowed variant that captures localized hallucinatory spans without dilution from sequence-length averaging. The key insight is that hallucinated content produces measurably larger log-eigenvalues in attention kernels and shifted hidden-state covariance structure compared to truthful text, reflecting the model's internal sensitivity to non-factual information grounded in its pretraining. The entire pipeline is training-free and requires only teacher-forcing, yielding 45×–450× speedups over multi-response baselines while achieving the best AUROC (72.34) and TPR@5%FPR (14.97) on the FAVA-Annotation dataset.

## Results

On the FAVA-Annotation dataset (no external references), the Attention Score (Layer 21) of LLM-Check with Llama-2-7B achieves an AUROC of 72.34, Accuracy of 67.96%, TPR@5%FPR of 14.97%, and F1 of 69.27, substantially outperforming all baselines. On the SelfCheckGPT dataset (multiple responses available), LLM-Check's Attention Score reaches AUC-PR of 80.04 and TPR@5%FPR of 9.41 with Llama-2, versus 72.84 and 4.81 for SelfCheckGPT. On RAGTruth (external references available), the Attention Score achieves an overall black-box AUROC of 58.30 and F1 of 57.18, with performance improving for larger target models (GPT-4: AUROC 63.51). The method achieves speedups of up to 45× and 450× over baselines, with Attention Score computation across all 32 layers taking only 0.22 seconds per example.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLM-Check's Attention Score decisively outperforms all four baselines on the FAVA-Annotation dataset. Against INSIDE (Chen et al., ICLR 2024), the strongest baseline, LLM-Check improves AUROC from 59.03 to 72.34 (+13.3 points) and F1 from 39.66 to 69.27 (+29.6 points) with Llama-2-7B, while also achieving a higher TPR@5%FPR (14.97 vs. 13.17). Against SelfCheckGPT-Prompt (Manakul et al., 2023), LLM-Check improves AUROC from 50.08 to 72.34 and TPR@5%FPR from near-zero to 14.97, and on the SelfCheckGPT dataset itself, LLM-Check's Attention Score achieves AUC-PR of 80.04 vs. SelfCheck's 72.84 and TPR@5%FPR of 9.41 vs. 4.81. Against the FAVA fine-tuned model (Mishra et al., 2024), LLM-Check improves AUROC from 53.29 to 72.34 and F1 from 43.88 to 69.27, without requiring any supervised training. Against Self-Prompt (Kadavath et al., 2022), LLM-Check improves AUROC from 50.30 to 72.34, though Self-Prompt's F1 (66.53) is competitive with LLM-Check's PPL Score (68.33) but well below the Attention Score (69.27). Notably, LLM-Check achieves these gains while operating on a single model response without multiple generations or external databases, and with 45–450× speedups over consistency-based baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant consistency-based paradigm (SelfCheckGPT, INSIDE) that requires multiple stochastically sampled responses or population-level eigen-analysis, by showing that a single forward pass with teacher-forcing on an auxiliary LLM suffices for hallucination detection. It repurposes spectral analysis—specifically the log-determinant of hidden-state covariance matrices and attention kernel diagonal entries—in a single-response, training-free setting, whereas INSIDE applies similar eigen-analysis across multiple responses at the population level. The output-token uncertainty measures (perplexity, windowed logit entropy) extend prior logit-based uncertainty estimation (Kadavath et al., Duan et al.) by introducing windowed variants sensitive to localized hallucinatory spans rather than sequence-length-averaged scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 45x–450x computational speedup makes hallucination detection feasible in real-time deployment pipelines, a practical barrier that consistency-based and retrieval-based methods cannot overcome. However, absolute detection performance remains modest (best AUROC ~72%, TPR@5%FPR ~15–25%), and the method still requires access to at least one proxy open-source LLM, limiting applicability in fully closed-API settings. Open questions include whether the different scoring axes (attention, hidden, logit) can be combined in a principled way that generalizes across hallucination types, and whether the detection signals can be repurposed for active mitigation (e.g., RLHF feedback) rather than post-hoc flagging.
