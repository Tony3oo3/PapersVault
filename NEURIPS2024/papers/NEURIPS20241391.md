## Problem

The s-ID problem asks whether a causal effect P_X(Y|S=1) in a sub-population can be identified from the sub-population's own observational distribution P(V|S=1). Prior work (Abouei et al., 2024) solved this only for causally sufficient settings where all variables are observed (DAGs). This paper addresses the open case where latent variables are present, so the causal graph is an ADMG with bidirected edges, which introduces new identifiability obstacles absent in the DAG setting.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input an augmented ADMG G^S over V∪{S} (where S is an auxiliary selection node with no children), two disjoint subsets X and Y of the observed variables V, and the sub-population distribution P^S(V) = P(V|S=1). It returns either a closed-form expression for P_X^S(Y) written entirely in terms of P^S(V) — involving Q^S[·] terms that are conditional distributions over non-ancestors of S — or FAIL if the effect is not s-ID. The expression is structured as a sum over ancestors of S of a product of Q^S[D_i] terms, one per s-component of the relevant ancestral set.

## Method

The paper extends two classical graphical notions from the ID problem to the sub-population ADMG setting: C-components become s-components (connected components via bidirected edges in G^S[H ∪ Anc(S)] intersected with H ⊆ V_NS), and Hedges become s-Hedges (a superset H ⊆ V_NS that is a single s-component containing Y as a proper subset with H = Anc_{G^S[H]}(Y)). Theorem 5.1 gives a sufficient identifiability condition: an m-separation (X_AS ⊥ Y | X_NS, S) in a modified graph plus the absence of any s-Hedge for the s-components of D = Anc_{G^S[V_NS\X_NS]}(Y_NS). Algorithm 1 is a recursive procedure that first checks the m-separation, then decomposes the target into Q^S[D_i] factors via s-components, and uses a sub-routine sID-Single that iteratively reduces a single s-component T to a smaller one T' (via Lemma 4.5's ratio formula) until it either computes Q^S[C] by marginalization or detects an s-Hedge and returns FAIL. The paper also proves a reduction from s-Recoverability to s-ID (Theorem 6.1), showing that solving s-ID subsumes the earlier s-Recoverability problem.

## Results

The main contribution is theoretical: Theorem 5.1 provides a necessary and sufficient graphical condition for s-ID in ADMGs (with latent variables), and Algorithm 1 is proven sound. In the numerical experiment (Appendix D), the s-ID algorithm estimates P_{X=0}(Y=1|S=1) ≈ 0.641, closely matching the true value of ≈ 0.648. In contrast, naively applying the classical ID algorithm to the sub-population data yields ≈ 0.725, an error of ~0.077. The paper also proves a reduction from s-Recoverability to s-ID (Theorem 6.1), showing that solving s-ID subsumes the s-Recoverability problem.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed s-ID algorithm against the classical ID algorithm (Tian & Pearl, AAAI 2002) on a synthetic SCM with latent variables and a selection variable S. The s-ID algorithm produces an estimate of 0.641 for P_{X=0}(Y=1|S=1), which is within 0.007 of the true value (0.648). The ID algorithm, which ignores the sub-population structure and simply returns P(Y|X), yields 0.725 — an error of 0.077, or roughly 12% relative error. This demonstrates that applying the ID algorithm to sub-population data without accounting for selection bias S leads to substantially incorrect causal effect estimates. The paper further shows (Appendix A) a case where a causal effect is ID-identifiable but not s-ID-identifiable, reinforcing that s-ID is strictly harder than ID and that the two problems cannot be conflated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the s-ID problem introduced by Abouei et al. (2024), which was restricted to causally sufficient settings (DAGs), to the presence of latent variables (ADMGs). It builds directly on the classical ID framework of Pearl (1995) and Tian & Pearl (2002), specifically extending the graphical notions of C-components and Hedges to their s-ID counterparts (s-components and s-Hedges). The paper also connects to the s-Recoverability problem of Bareinboim, Tian & Pearl (2014) by showing a reduction from s-Recoverability to s-ID, unifying two previously separate lines of work on selection bias in causal inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that naively applying the standard ID algorithm to sub-population data yields incorrect causal effect estimates, as demonstrated empirically (ID gives 0.725 vs. true value 0.648). The s-Hedge concept introduces a more complex obstruction to identifiability than the classical Hedge, meaning that even effects identifiable in the full population can become non-identifiable in a sub-population when latent variables are present. The authors conjecture but do not prove completeness of their algorithm, leaving open whether the sufficient condition in Theorem 5.1 is also necessary. The reduction from s-Recoverability to s-ID suggests that a single algorithmic framework could address both sub-population identification and population recovery problems.
