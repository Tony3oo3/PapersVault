## Problem

Computing exact Shapley values for dataset valuation requires evaluating 2^I coalitions, which is intractable for realistic numbers of players. Existing Monte Carlo approximations are generic and do not exploit the specific structure of the dataset valuation utility function, leaving them expensive whenever marginal contributions require model retraining. This paper asks whether the structural properties of the utility function in dataset valuation can yield a fundamentally more efficient Shapley value estimator.

## Input / Output

The method takes I player datasets D_i (each of size n_i, drawn from player-specific distributions p_i) and a utility function u: 2^I → R that scores how well a coalition solves a given ML task (e.g., hold-out accuracy or negative MSE). It outputs a per-player Shapley value approximation ψ_i requiring only I utility evaluations. In the structured use-cases where u(S) = w(q(S)) for a scalar q, the computation further reduces to evaluating w at I deterministic real values, eliminating the stochastic sampling entirely.

## Method

DU-Shapley is built on the observation that in three theoretical use-cases (non-parametric regression, homogeneous learning, and heterogeneous linear regression with local differential privacy), the coalition utility decomposes as u(S) = w(q(S)) where q(S) is a scalar. Theorem 2 proves that q(S)/q(I) converges almost surely to U([0,1]) as the number of players I grows, meaning the random coalition size inside the Shapley expectation becomes effectively uniform. DU-Shapley (Definition 3) replaces the random coalition with I deterministic samples of increasing size drawn uniformly without replacement from the pool of other players' data, reducing the computation from 2^I to I utility evaluations. Non-asymptotic guarantees (Theorem 5, Corollary 6) bound the approximation error in terms of the empirical variance and range of dataset sizes, showing the bias vanishes as I → ∞ and degrades gracefully when dataset sizes are heterogeneous.

## Results

On the worst-case Shapley value approximation task (I=10 players), DU-Shapley achieves an averaged MSE of 2×10⁻³ on Adult, 3×10⁻³ on Breast Cancer, 5×10⁻² on Bank, and 1×10⁻² on Cal-Housing, consistently outperforming MC-Shapley (1×10⁻², 3×10⁻², 9×10⁻², 5×10⁻²) and MC-anti-Shapley (8×10⁻³, 1×10⁻², 8×10⁻², 3×10⁻²). On the OpenDataVal dataset valuation tasks with I=100 players, DU-Shapley achieves F1-scores of 0.30 (CIFAR-10 NLD 15%), 0.55 (CIFAR-10 DR 15%), and 0.14 (CIFAR-10 DA 15%), matching or exceeding DataShapley and KNN-Shapley. In synthetic experiments, DU-Shapley maintains lower MSE than all MC-based methods even when those methods are granted 10× the sampling budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DU-Shapley outperforms both MC-Shapley and MC-anti-Shapley [32] on all six real-world datasets at both I=10 and I=20 players, with MSE reductions ranging from ~2× (Year Prediction, I=20) to ~4× (Make Regression, I=10). Against DataShapley [10] and KNN-Shapley [15] on the OpenDataVal benchmark (I=100 players), DU-Shapley achieves comparable or slightly better F1-scores on noisy label detection (e.g., 0.30 vs. 0.25 and 0.28 on CIFAR-10 at 15% noise) and dataset removal (0.55 vs. 0.59 and 0.57 on CIFAR-10 at 15%), while matching or exceeding them on dataset addition. In the complexity comparison (Figure 2), both DataShapley [16] and IGTB [46] require more iterations than DU-Shapley's I² budget to reach the same accumulated bias. On synthetic data (Figure 4), DU-Shapley maintains lower MSE than MC-Shapley, MC-anti-Shapley, Owen-Shapley [34], and Orthogonal-Shapley even when those methods are allocated 10× the sampling budget. Finally, SVARM [22] cannot guarantee an error equal to DU-Shapley's bias with high probability after I² iterations (Figure 3), further confirming DU-Shapley's efficiency advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Shapley value framework from cooperative game theory (Shapley 1952) as applied to dataset valuation, but departs fundamentally from the generic Monte Carlo approximation strategies (permutation sampling, antithetic sampling, Owen's multilinear extension) that dominate the literature. The key structural insight is that in dataset valuation, the utility function depends on a scalar quantity (e.g., total data points or a weighted sum), which allows replacing the 2^I coalition evaluations with only I deterministic evaluations of increasing size. This contrasts with data valuation (Data Shapley, KNN-Shapley), where each data point is distinct and such scalar reduction is not possible, and with SVARM, which avoids marginal contributions but still requires I² iterations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential reduction from 2^I to I utility evaluations is practically significant for dataset valuation in federated or multi-party ML settings where retraining is expensive. The non-asymptotic bounds (Theorem 5, Corollary 6) give practitioners concrete guidance on when the approximation is reliable—specifically, when dataset sizes across players are not too heterogeneous. A key limitation is that the method may degrade when some players have vastly larger datasets than others, and the convergence guarantees require bounded heterogeneity. Open questions include extending the scalar-structure exploitation to settings where the utility depends on richer coalition properties, and understanding how DU-Shapley behaves with very large I in practical federated learning deployments.
