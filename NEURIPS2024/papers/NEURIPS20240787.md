## Problem

Most neural network pruning research focuses on designing better scoring criteria to decide which weights to remove, but largely overlooks whether a trained network is inherently sparsifiable at all. Standard training does not encourage sparsifiability, so networks tend to use all parameters to fit the data and resist effective pruning regardless of the chosen criterion. This paper addresses the gap by modifying the training process itself to produce networks that are naturally amenable to aggressive sparsification.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard supervised training setup (dataset plus architecture) and trains the network using the Laplace-approximated marginal likelihood as an additional differentiable objective on top of the usual loss. It produces a sparsified model via either unstructured (per-weight) or structured (per-unit/filter) masking, along with a compacted architecture for structured pruning. A key practical detail is that the OPD pruning scores are a free byproduct of the marginal likelihood computation—no extra forward or backward passes are needed—making it cheaper than criteria like SNIP or GraSP that require additional gradient accumulation.

## Method

SpaM optimizes thousands of prior hyperparameters (regularization strengths at scalar, layer-wise, unit-wise, or parameter-wise granularity) by gradient ascent on the Laplace-approximated marginal likelihood, implementing an automatic Bayesian Occam's razor that drives irrelevant parameters toward small magnitudes during training. The Laplace approximation uses either a diagonal or KFAC Hessian structure; the paper derives a new proposition (Prop. 3.1) for efficiently projecting diagonal priors into the KFAC eigenbasis without computing Kronecker products. After training, the pre-computed posterior precision P_θ = H_θ + diag(δ) is reused to define the OPD criterion S(θ_p) = P_pp · θ_p², which scores each weight by the product of posterior certainty and magnitude. For structured pruning, scores are aggregated over units and uniform per-layer sparsity is applied, followed by weight transfer into a smaller compacted model. The approach is criterion-agnostic—SpaM-trained networks improve under any pruning rule—but OPD consistently outperforms alternatives at high sparsities (90–99%) without requiring fine-tuning.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet, SpaM+OPD retains 89.98% accuracy at 95% sparsity (20× fewer parameters), nearly matching the unpruned model (~91%), while MAP+OPD drops to 37.52% and MAP+GraSP to 26.46% at the same sparsity. At 90% sparsity, SpaM+OPD achieves 90.68% accuracy versus 64.08% for MAP+OPD. The advantage is most pronounced at high sparsities: at 99% sparsity, SpaM+OPD still reaches 66.28% while all MAP-based criteria fall below 18%. SpaM also improves uncertainty calibration (NLL, ECE, Brier score) over MAP training across all criteria.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpaM consistently improves every pruning criterion over MAP training, with the largest gains at high sparsity. Against GraSP [37], SpaM lifts 95%-sparsity accuracy from 26.46% (MAP) to 82.18% (SpaM), a 55-point improvement, though OPD still edges out GraSP by ~8 points. Against magnitude pruning [7], SpaM raises 95% accuracy from 32.88% to 81.80%, again with OPD leading by ~8 points. Against SNIP [36], SpaM improves 95% accuracy from 13.88% to 17.06%, but SNIP remains the weakest criterion even under SpaM, far below OPD (89.98%). Against SynFlow [50] in the structured pruning setting (Figure 5), SpaM-OPD dominates the Pareto frontier, achieving 10×–20× FLOPS and memory savings with minimal accuracy loss, while SynFlow degrades more sharply at high sparsities. The OPD criterion itself, being a free byproduct of the Laplace posterior precision, outperforms all four baselines at 90%+ sparsity without requiring additional forward passes or gradient accumulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Laplace-approximated marginal likelihood framework for Bayesian neural networks (Immer et al., ICML 2021; Daxberger et al., NeurIPS 2021; Ritter et al., ICLR 2018), repurposing it from model selection to the problem of making networks inherently more sparsifiable during training. The proposed OPD pruning criterion is a direct extension of the classic Optimal Brain Damage (LeCun et al., 1990), replacing the Hessian with the posterior precision that already includes the learned prior. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant pruning literature—which focuses on designing better post-hoc scoring functions—by arguing that the training objective itself should encourage sparsifiability, an angle the authors identify as largely overlooked. The new unit-wise and parameter-wise priors for KFAC also extend the Laplace-approximation toolkit beyond the scalar and layer-wise priors previously supported.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: SpaM-OPD achieves 10×–20× reductions in FLOPS and memory with minimal accuracy loss, and the OPD criterion is essentially free since it reuses the posterior precision already computed during marginal likelihood training. This positions the method as a viable path for deploying large models on resource-constrained hardware, a growing concern as AI models outpace consumer hardware. Key limitations include the inherited constraints of the Laplace approximation (single-mode, potential numerical instability with low-precision weights) and the additional training cost over standard MAP. Open questions include whether the approach scales to billion-parameter LLMs (the GPT-2 experiment uses only 124M parameters and two optimization steps), whether the sparsifiability benefit persists without fine-tuning at extreme sparsities, and how the method interacts with other compression techniques like quantization.
