## Problem

While Nash equilibrium computation in adversarial team Markov games (ATMGs) was shown to be tractable under full model knowledge [65], no learning algorithm with sample complexity guarantees existed for the model-free, bandit-feedback setting. The underlying min-max optimization is nonconvex-nonconcave, making saddle-point computation computationally intractable in full generality, and the coupled feasibility constraints (the adversary's visitation measure depends on the team's policy) further complicate standard min-max techniques.

## Input / Output

Each agent receives only bandit feedback: trajectory roll-outs from which they observe their own state sequence and individual reward, but never the actions of other agents (including the adversary). The game structure (state space, per-player action spaces, discount factor γ, initial distribution ρ) is assumed known. The output is a joint stationary policy (x*, y*) constituting an ε-approximate Nash equilibrium, with iteration and sample complexity polynomial in 1/ε, |S|, Σ|Aᵢ|+|B|, and 1/(1−γ), avoiding the curse of multi-agents (no dependence on ∏|Aᵢ|).

## Method

The key insight is reformulating the adversary's value function in terms of the state-action visitation measure λ(y;x) and adding a quadratic regularizer −(ν/2)‖λ‖², which makes the max function Φ^ν(x) differentiable via Danskin's theorem and eliminates the need to solve a linear program. This transforms the problem into nonconvex-strongly-concave optimization with coupled constraints (Λ(x) depends on x), for which the paper proves Φ^ν has a (1/2, ℓ_{1/2})-Hölder continuous gradient. The ISPNG algorithm is a nested-loop scheme: the adversary runs VIS-REG-PG (a policy gradient method on the regularized value function using trajectory-based gradient estimators), while each team member independently performs a REINFORCE-based projected gradient step on Φ^ν. Convergence is established by extending the inexact-oracle framework of Devolder et al. [35] to nonconvex, constrained, weakly-smooth settings with stochastic inexact gradients, where the regularization coefficient ν simultaneously controls the Hölder constant and bounds the gradient inexactness from unobserved adversary actions.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 3.3) establishes that Algorithm 1 (ISPNG) computes an ε-approximate Nash equilibrium in adversarial team Markov games with iteration and sample complexity polynomial in 1/ε, |S|, Σᵢ|Aᵢ| + |B|, 1/(1−γ), and the mismatch coefficient D_m. A key technical contribution (Theorem 3.1) shows that stochastic projected gradient descent with an inexact gradient oracle converges to an ε-FOSP in O(ε^{−(1+p)/p}) iterations for functions with (ℓ_p, p)-Hölder continuous gradient. The general min-max result (Theorem 4.2) achieves an ε-approximate saddle-point for nonconvex-hidden-strongly-concave functions in O(1/(ν²ε³)) calls to a maximization oracle, with the inner loop requiring Õ(1/(ν³ε²)) iterations. Notably, the complexity scales with Σᵢ|Aᵢ| (sum of action spaces) rather than Πᵢ|Aᵢ| (product), thereby beating the curse of multi-agents.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental baselines or empirical comparisons. The primary theoretical comparison is with the prior equilibrium computation algorithm of Kalogiannis et al. (ICLR 2023, [65]), which required full knowledge of the reward and transition functions and provided no sample complexity guarantees; the present work resolves both caveats by operating under bandit feedback with polynomial sample complexity. The paper also circumvents the general computational intractability of nonconvex-nonconcave min-max optimization [33] by exploiting the hidden strongly-concave structure of the adversary's value function via a regularized reformulation. Prior to this work, learning algorithms with convergence guarantees for Nash equilibria were known only for the special cases of two-player zero-sum Markov games [27, 103] and Markov potential games [68, 114], but not for the more general ATMG setting that unifies both. The extension of inexact-oracle techniques from Devolder et al. [35] (originally for smooth convex optimization) to the nonconvex, constrained, Hölder-continuous setting is a key methodological advance that enables the main convergence guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Kalogiannis et al. [65], who provided the first efficient equilibrium computation algorithm for adversarial team Markov games (ATMGs) but required full knowledge of the reward and transition functions with no sample complexity guarantees. The learning algorithm here resolves that gap by working under bandit feedback, drawing on the inexact-oracle framework of Devolder, Glineur, and Nesterov [35] (extended here to nonconvex and constrained settings) and the policy gradient machinery from Daskalakis et al. [27] and Leonardos et al. [68] for zero-sum and potential Markov games respectively. The key conceptual departure is the nonconvex-hidden-concave reformulation via the adversary's state-action visitation measure, which converts the intractable nonconvex-nonconcave saddle-point problem into a nonconvex-strongly-concave one with coupled constraints, a structural trick not previously exploited in the MARL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first learning algorithm with polynomial sample and iteration complexity for Nash equilibria in ATMGs, a game class that unifies both two-player zero-sum and Markov potential games, thereby closing a notable gap in the MARL theory landscape. The Hölder-continuity convergence result for stochastic projected gradient descent on weakly-smooth nonconvex functions is a reusable optimization tool beyond this specific application. Key limitations include the nested-loop structure (weaker than fully independent learning), the unclear tightness of the Hölder exponent (it remains open whether Φ^ν is actually Lipschitz-smooth, which would yield better rates), and the absence of any empirical validation. Open questions include whether two-timescale schemes can eliminate the inner loop and whether variance reduction can improve the sample complexity.
