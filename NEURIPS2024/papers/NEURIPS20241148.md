## Problem

Online experimentation with cross-unit interference (e.g., e-commerce discounts affecting competing goods) is a common practical challenge, yet the adaptive/online learning setting remains largely unaddressed compared to the well-studied offline causal inference literature. The core difficulty is combinatorial: with N units and A actions, the joint action space is A^N, making naive MAB regret scale as Õ(√(A^N T)). Existing online work (Jia et al. 2024) restricts to known grid-like networks with decaying interference strength and measures regret only against the best constant policy, leaving a significant gap for general sparse interference patterns and stronger comparators.

## Input / Output

The learner receives N units, A actions (assumed a power of 2, with redundant encoding otherwise), a time horizon T, and optionally the directed interference graph G (known case). At each round it simultaneously assigns one action to every unit and observes a noisy scalar reward per unit (semi-bandit feedback), where each unit's reward depends on the treatments of at most s neighboring units. The output is a per-unit treatment assignment for the exploitation phase, derived from estimated per-unit Fourier coefficient vectors θ̂_n ∈ R^{A^N} that are A^s-sparse under the interference model.

## Method

The key technical insight is to encode each action a ∈ [A] as a Boolean vector v(a) ∈ {−1,1}^{log₂(A)} and represent each unit's reward r_n as a linear function in the discrete Fourier basis χ_S, yielding r_n(a) = ⟨θ_n, χ(a)⟩. Under sparse network interference (Assumption 2), the Fourier coefficient vector θ_n is provably A^s-sparse, with non-zero entries indexed only by subsets of the neighbor block B(n). For known interference, Algorithm 1 performs uniform exploration for E rounds, then runs per-unit OLS regression on the A^s relevant Fourier coordinates, aggregates the estimates, and greedily plays the argmax action for the remaining T−E rounds, achieving regret Õ((A^s T)^{2/3}). For unknown interference, Algorithm 2 replaces OLS with Lasso regression against the full A^N-dimensional Fourier basis to adapt to the unknown sparsity pattern, incurring an additional N^{1/3} factor in regret. The analysis relies on incoherence of the random Fourier design matrix and standard sparse recovery guarantees.

## Results

The paper establishes high-probability regret bounds for two explore-then-commit algorithms: Õ((A^s T)^(2/3)) for known interference (Algorithm 1) and Õ(N^(1/3)(A^s T)^(2/3)) for unknown interference (Algorithm 2), both significantly improving over the naive MAB rate of Õ(√(A^N T)). In simulations with A=2, s=4, and N up to 10, both proposed algorithms substantially outperform UCB, with UCB exhibiting exponential regret growth as N increases while the proposed methods show only polynomial scaling. Algorithm 1 (known graph) achieves lower regret than Algorithm 2 (unknown graph) due to its reduced ambient regression dimension, and it terminates its exploration phase earlier since it does not require additional samples for sparsity recovery.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole experimental baseline is UCB (Auer et al., 2002), which treats the entire N-unit network as a single bandit with A^N arms. In the simulations (Figure 2), UCB's cumulative regret grows exponentially with N, while both Network MAB (Known) and Network MAB (Unknown) exhibit only mild polynomial growth, confirming the theoretical advantage of exploiting sparsity. The paper could not include a direct empirical comparison to Jia et al. [2024] due to the absence of a public implementation, but notes that Jia et al. measure regret against the best constant action (a strictly weaker comparator) and assume a grid-like network with decaying interference strength, whereas this paper compares against the optimal per-unit action a* and allows arbitrary sparse interference. Theoretically, the proposed methods also outperform a global-estimation variant by a factor of N^(2/3), since per-unit regression avoids the additional variance from averaging across heterogeneous units.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the explore-then-commit framework for sparse linear bandits (Hao et al., 2020) to the setting of network interference by using discrete Fourier analysis to construct a sparse linear representation of unit-specific rewards. It generalizes Jia et al. (2024), who studied MABs with interference only under a known grid-like network with decaying interference strength and measured regret against the best constant action, by allowing arbitrary sparse neighborhood interference, handling unknown network structure via Lasso, and comparing regret to the combinatorially optimal action. The key technical departure is the Fourier-analytic sparsity argument: sparse network interference (Assumption 2) directly implies A^s-sparse Fourier coefficients, enabling standard linear regression tools to apply.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first regret guarantees for MABs with arbitrary sparse network interference under both known and unknown network settings, bridging online learning and discrete Fourier analysis in a way that could inform other structured bandit problems. However, the T^(2/3) regret rate is suboptimal (T^(1/2) would be ideal), and the authors acknowledge this as a key open direction. Extensions to contextual bandits, reinforcement learning, and establishing matching lower bounds remain open, as does understanding whether the Fourier-based feature construction is the most efficient way to exploit network sparsity.
