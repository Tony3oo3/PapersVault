## Problem

Robust ERM (adversarial training) suffers from large generalization gaps and overfitting that are absent in standard ERM, where overparameterized models interpolate training data and generalize seamlessly. The paper asks: what type of implicit regularization should ideally be induced for a given perturbation set to improve robust generalization, and how does the implicit bias of the optimization algorithm or architecture affect this? It identifies a 'price of implicit bias' where the default ℓ₂ bias of gradient descent can actively harm robust generalization when misaligned with the threat model.

## Input / Output

For the theoretical and synthetic experiments, the input is a vector x ∈ ℝᵈ with binary label y ∈ {±1}, and the threat model is an ℓ_p ball of radius ε around each training point; the model must classify correctly for all perturbations x′ with ‖x′−x‖_p ≤ ε. For the neural network experiments, inputs are 28×28 grayscale MNIST digits with ℓ_∞ perturbations of magnitude ε (e.g., 0.2), and the output is a binary or multi-class label. The key structural detail is that the effective 'input' during robust training is the worst-case perturbation within the threat set, making the loss a bilevel optimization problem.

## Method

The paper proves that steepest descent with respect to an ℓ_r norm on the worst-case exponential loss in robust ERM converges (in direction) to the minimum ℓ_r norm separator that robustly classifies all training points (Theorem 3.3), generalizing prior results for gradient descent (r=2). It then shows that the optimal choice of r depends not only on the dual perturbation norm p* but also on the sparsity of the data-generating process and the perturbation magnitude ε, with ℓ₁ regularization being preferable for sparse teachers under ℓ_∞ perturbations. A second mechanism is identified through architecture: gradient descent on diagonal neural networks f(x;u) = ⟨u₊²−u₋², x⟩ (a reparameterized linear model) converges to a minimum ℓ₁ norm solution in prediction space (Proposition 3.8), effectively providing the 'correct' implicit bias without changing the algorithm. Experiments on synthetic Gaussian data and MNIST (with 1-hidden-layer ReLU nets and CNNs) confirm that the gap between algorithms with different implicit biases grows with perturbation magnitude, and that sign descent (steepest descent w.r.t. ℓ_∞) can outperform gradient descent in robust ERM for homogeneous networks.

## Results

On synthetic linear classification with ℓ∞ perturbations, the robust generalization gap between gradient descent (GD) and coordinate descent (CD) exceeds 20% for Dense,Dense data with few training examples, while for sparse-teacher data (k_W=4, k_X=512) CD and diagonal-network GD consistently outperform GD and the gap widens under robust ERM. On MNIST (digits 2 vs 7) with 1-hidden-layer ReLU nets and ε=0.2, sign descent (SD) outperforms GD by 4.11% mean robust test accuracy over 3 seeds, while both reach 100% robust train accuracy. For CNNs on full MNIST, the advantage of the better-aligned algorithm grows with ε and shrinks with dataset size m, confirming that the 'price of implicit bias' scales with problem difficulty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three optimization strategies for robust ERM: gradient descent (GD, inducing ℓ₂ bias), coordinate descent (CD, inducing ℓ₁ bias), and gradient descent on diagonal neural networks (diag-net-GD, also inducing ℓ₁ bias via Proposition 3.8). In standard ERM (ε=0), all three methods generalize similarly on Dense,Dense data, but under robust ERM the gap widens dramatically—CD and diag-net-GD achieve substantially smaller robust generalization gaps than GD, especially for sparse-teacher distributions where the dimension-dependent penalty of ℓ₂ solutions becomes pronounced. On MNIST ReLU networks, SD (steepest descent w.r.t. ℓ∞) beats GD by 4.11% robust test accuracy at ε=0.2, and also avoids the robust overfitting observed with GD. Interestingly, for CNNs the situation reverses: GD outperforms SD, demonstrating that the 'correct' implicit bias depends on the architecture, not just the algorithm. The paper also notes that at the maximum permissible perturbation ε*, all algorithms converge to the same max-margin separator (Lemma C.7), so the price of implicit bias vanishes in that limit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed threads: (1) margin-based generalization bounds for adversarially robust classification (Yin et al. 2019, Awasthi et al. 2020), which advocated ℓ_{p*} regularization to control the worst-case Rademacher complexity term, and (2) the implicit bias of optimization in ERM (Gunasekar et al. 2018a, Soudry et al. 2018), which explains why gradient descent generalizes without explicit regularization. The key departure is showing that the implicit bias that *helps* in standard ERM can *hurt* in robust ERM when misaligned with the threat model. Theorem 3.3 generalizes both Gunasekar et al.'s steepest-descent analysis and Li et al.'s (2020) gradient-descent-in-robust-ERM result to arbitrary ℓ_r steepest descent and arbitrary ℓ_p perturbations, while the diagonal-network analysis (building on Lyu and Zhu 2022 and Woodworth et al. 2020) reveals that reparameterization alone can flip the induced bias from ℓ_2 to ℓ_1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, mechanistic explanation for the large robust generalization gap observed in adversarial training: the default ℓ_2 bias of gradient descent is suboptimal (or actively harmful) for ℓ_∞ perturbations in sparse-data regimes, and this misalignment grows with perturbation magnitude. This suggests that choosing the right optimization algorithm (coordinate descent, sign descent) or architecture (diagonal networks) is a practical lever for improving robust generalization without explicit regularization. However, the theoretical results are confined to linear models, the neural network experiments are limited to MNIST with small networks, and the CNN results (where GD outperforms SD) complicate the clean linear-model narrative. Open questions include extending the analysis to non-linear models, understanding the connection to robust overfitting dynamics, and whether algorithmic changes can close the robustness gap on ImageNet-scale problems.
