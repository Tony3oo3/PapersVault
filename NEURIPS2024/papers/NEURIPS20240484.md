## Problem

The classical Coase theorem guarantees welfare-efficient outcomes through bargaining when property rights are well-defined, but it assumes players have perfect knowledge of their own and others' utility functions. This paper asks whether the theorem survives when both players must learn their preferences sequentially in a multi-armed bandit setting, and shows that without property rights the social welfare regret grows linearly (welfare breakdown), while with property rights a suitable bargaining policy restores sub-linear social welfare regret.

## Input / Output

The downstream player's algorithm BELGIC takes as input a K-armed bandit instance, a time horizon T, the upstream player's black-box no-regret policy characterized by high-probability regret constants C and κ, and hyperparameters α, β satisfying β/α < 1−κ. Each round, the downstream player outputs a triple (incentivized arm, transfer amount, own arm) rather than a single arm, and the upstream player's policy consumes the incentive as an additional input to its arm selection. The final output is a sub-linear social welfare regret bound of O(K log(T) T^{(κ+1)/2}) when the upstream player runs UCB.

## Method

BELGIC operates in two phases. In the first phase, the downstream player performs a batched binary search on each of the K arms to estimate the optimal transfer τ*_a = max_{a'} v^up(a') − v^up(a) to precision 1/T^β, using the upstream player's deviation frequency within each batch as the binary signal (Lemma 2), which is valid under the high-probability batched regret assumption H2. In the second phase, a standard bandit subroutine (e.g., UCB) is run in black-box fashion on a shifted bandit instance where each arm's reward is offset by the estimated transfer, so the downstream player simultaneously selects her own arm and the arm to incentivize. The key theoretical insight (Lemma 1) is that the social welfare regret is upper-bounded by the sum of the two players' individual regrets, meaning that individual no-regret behavior under the property-rights game automatically yields welfare efficiency—recovering the Coase theorem under uncertainty.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 3 + Corollary 1) shows that when the upstream player runs UCB and the downstream player runs BELGIC (with α=3/4, β=1/4), the social welfare regret is O(K log(T) T^{(κ+1)/2}), which is sub-linear and hence welfare efficient. In contrast, Theorem 2 proves that without property rights, the social welfare regret is Ω(T) (linear) whenever there is a misalignment between the upstream player's preferred arm and the social optimum. Empirically, with T=5×10⁶, K=10 arms, and UCB as the bandit subroutine, BELGIC drives the upstream player's action frequency toward the social optimum (q₁*=8) rather than the competitive optimum (q₁'=5), as shown in Figure 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare BELGIC against other incentive-design or bandit algorithms in a head-to-head numerical benchmark. Instead, the primary comparison is between two regimes: (1) no property rights, where both players independently run UCB, and (2) property rights with BELGIC, where the downstream player estimates optimal transfers via batched binary search and then runs a shifted bandit subroutine. In the no-property-rights regime, the social welfare regret grows linearly (Ω(T)), confirming the breakdown of welfare efficiency predicted by Theorem 2. In the BELGIC regime, the social welfare regret is sub-linear (O(K log(T) T^{(κ+1)/2})), recovering the Coase theorem under uncertainty. The empirical experiment (Figure 1) visually confirms this: without property rights, the upstream player's action frequencies converge to the competitive optimum, while with BELGIC they converge to the social optimum. The paper also notes that the downstream player's regret bound depends on the upstream player's regret exponent κ, meaning the downstream player 'never learns faster than the upstream player'—a structural limitation rather than a comparison against a specific baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the principal-agent bandit framework of Dogan et al. (2023a,b) and Scheid et al. (2024) by introducing the Coasean externality structure, where the downstream player's reward depends on the upstream player's action, and by allowing the upstream player to use any black-box no-regret algorithm rather than a specified one. The core economic result builds directly on Coase's (1960/2013) theorem on property rights and bargaining, which had previously been studied only under perfect information. The BELGIC algorithm's binary-search subroutine for estimating optimal transfers draws on batched bandit techniques (Perchet et al., 2016) and incentivized exploration literature (Mansour et al., 2016; Simchowitz and Slivkins, 2023), but applies them in the novel context of online Coasean bargaining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper removes the perfect-information assumption from the Coase theorem, providing a rigorous foundation for why property rights and bargaining restore welfare efficiency even when both parties are learning their preferences over time. The key structural result (Lemma 1) that individual no-regret behavior under property rights automatically yields social welfare efficiency is a clean and general insight. However, the setting is limited to two players with discrete actions and specific high-probability regret bounds (H2), and the authors explicitly note that extensions to multi-agent settings raise many open questions. The practical impact is currently theoretical, as the framework requires knowledge of the upstream player's regret constants and assumes a specific Stackelberg structure.
