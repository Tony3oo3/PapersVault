## Problem

Conformal prediction constructs prediction sets via a deterministic threshold on non-conformity scores, but it is unclear whether such threshold-based sets can always maximize the average accuracy of human experts using a decision support system. This paper shows that conformal prediction sets are in general suboptimal, that finding the truly optimal prediction sets is NP-hard and hard to approximate to any factor less than the label set size, and that a simple greedy algorithm can provably match or exceed conformal prediction performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a feature vector x, the calibrated softmax scores f(x) ∈ [0,1]^L from a classifier, and a confusion matrix C parameterizing a mixture of multinomial logit models (MNLs) that captures how the expert distributes predictions across labels given the true label. It outputs a prediction set S(x) ⊆ Y — a subset of the L label values — from which the expert is constrained to select their final prediction. The set is not a single label but a variable-size subset whose composition is optimized per instance.

## Method

The paper first proves via a concrete counterexample that conformal prediction (which selects labels by ranking non-conformity scores against a single threshold) cannot recover the optimal prediction set under many data distributions. It then establishes NP-hardness via a reduction from k-clique and shows the problem is hard to approximate to any factor less than |Y|. The proposed greedy algorithm estimates the expert's accuracy under a candidate set S as ĝ(S|x) = Σ_{y∈S} f_y(x) · C_yy / Σ_{y'∈S} C_{y'y}, then runs L rounds: at round k it starts from an empty set and greedily adds labels from the top-k classifier-ranked labels that maximize marginal gain in ĝ, tracking the best set across all rounds. A key proposition guarantees that for any non-conformity score that is non-increasing in f_y(x), the greedy output achieves at least as high estimated accuracy as any conformal prediction set. The overall complexity is O(L⁴), and experiments on synthetic tasks and ImageNet16H show the greedy algorithm consistently outperforms four conformal baselines (NAIVE, APS, RAPS, SAPS), with gains growing as expert noise and the number of labels increase.

## Results

On synthetic multiclass classification tasks (L=10 labels), the greedy algorithm consistently achieves the highest average expert accuracy across all combinations of classifier accuracy (P(Y'=Y) ∈ {0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9}) and expert noise (γ ∈ {0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0}), e.g., 0.824 vs. 0.804 (best conformal) at γ=0.3, P(Y'=Y)=0.7. On the real ImageNet16H dataset, the greedy algorithm achieves the highest average accuracy at all noise levels except ω=80 where it ties with NAIVE (0.957), and the advantage grows with noise: 0.874 vs. 0.860 (NAIVE) at ω=125. Brute force search achieves identical performance to the greedy algorithm in all settings, confirming near-optimality. The greedy algorithm also degrades more gracefully as expert noise increases compared to conformal prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed greedy algorithm against four conformal prediction baselines: NAIVE (Vovk et al. 2005, s(x,y)=1−f_y(x)), APS (Romano et al. 2020, adaptive prediction sets), RAPS (Angelopoulos et al. 2021, rank-adaptive prediction sets), and SAPS (Huang et al. 2024, label-ranking-based conformal prediction). The greedy algorithm wins against all four baselines in every experimental setting on both synthetic and real data. On synthetic tasks, the advantage ranges from ~1–2 percentage points at low noise (γ=0.3) to ~5–6 percentage points at high noise (γ=1.0), e.g., 0.782 vs. 0.734 (RAPS) at γ=1.0, P(Y'=Y)=0.7. On ImageNet16H, the greedy algorithm achieves 0.874 vs. 0.860 (NAIVE), 0.852 (APS), 0.849 (RAPS), and 0.855 (SAPS) at the highest noise level (ω=125), with the gap widening as noise increases. At the lowest noise (ω=80), the greedy algorithm ties with NAIVE (both 0.957) but still outperforms APS, RAPS, and SAPS. Notably, the four conformal baselines perform very similarly to each other, suggesting that the choice of non-conformity score matters far less than the fundamental limitation of threshold-based set construction that the greedy algorithm overcomes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the decision support framework of Straitouri et al. [18, 19], which used conformal prediction to construct prediction sets that help human experts achieve higher accuracy. The key conceptual departure is reframing the problem from 'construct sets with valid coverage guarantees' to 'find the set that maximizes expert accuracy,' revealing that conformal prediction's threshold rule is provably suboptimal for this objective. The paper also draws structural parallels to assortment optimization under customization (via the mixture of MNLs expert model) and learning under algorithmic triage, while arguing that the unknown ground-truth label makes the problem fundamentally harder than its assortment-optimization analogue.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NP-hardness and inapproximability results (hard to approximate to any factor less than |Y|) establish fundamental limits on what is achievable for optimal prediction set construction, while the greedy algorithm provides a practical O(L⁴) solution with a provable guarantee of matching or exceeding conformal prediction. Open questions include developing model-free algorithms (the current approach assumes a mixture-of-MNLs expert model), incorporating expert prediction distributions into non-conformity scores to close the gap without model assumptions, and extending the framework to account for fairness in high-stakes domains. The finding that coverage is a poor proxy for expert accuracy under prediction sets has broader implications for how decision support systems should be evaluated.
