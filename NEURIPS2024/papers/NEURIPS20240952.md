## Problem

AI systems that personalize to individual users must learn human values quickly and safely, avoiding harmful actions during the learning process. While representational alignment (learning human-like internal representations) is known to improve generalization and robustness, its role in enabling safe exploration and value alignment during reinforcement learning has not been studied. This paper identifies representational alignment as a previously overlooked factor that determines how safely and efficiently an agent can learn a human value function.

## Input / Output

The agent receives 50 textual action descriptions (atomic actions adapted from the ETHICS Justice category) and a frozen pairwise similarity matrix (kernel) derived from a language model's embeddings or similarity judgments. At each timestep, the agent selects one action from a randomly drawn subset of 10, receives a scalar reward sampled from N(m_x, 1) where m_x is the human value score, and updates its value function. The personalization phase restricts the agent to 25 of 50 actions over 1000 timesteps; the generalization phase evaluates on the remaining 25 unseen actions without parameter updates. Performance is measured by mean reward per timestep and count of immoral actions (score < 50).

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical analysis showing that in Gaussian process regression, prediction error grows monotonically as the Spearman correlation between teacher and student kernels decreases, with misalignment in the cross-covariance matrix K* (between personalization and generalization actions) having a strictly larger effect than misalignment in the training covariance K. Empirically, the agent's representation space is defined entirely by a pairwise similarity matrix provided as a kernel, and the agent learns only the mapping from that fixed representation to a value function using Thompson sampling in a multi-armed bandit setting. This design isolates the effect of representational alignment by freezing the kernel while varying its correlation with the human similarity matrix. The framework is validated across 16 embedding models, 4 GPT variants, and 9 additional LLMs, demonstrating that higher representational alignment yields safer exploration (fewer immoral actions) and better generalization across 10 distinct human values including morality, fairness, honesty, and compassion.

## Results

In simulated experiments across 2,300+ runs, Spearman correlations between representational alignment and mean reward range from 0.647 (Gaussian Process Regression) to 0.750 (Support Vector Regression), while correlations with immoral actions taken range from −0.696 to −0.798 (all p < 0.0001). In human-data experiments across 20 language models and 10 human values, representational alignment (Spearman ρ_S) correlates positively with mean reward (e.g., ρ_S = 0.631 for morality generalization, 0.77 for cross-alignment) and negatively with bad actions (e.g., ρ_S = −0.691 for morality generalization). The strongest generalization-phase correlations appear for morality (ρ_S = 0.77), integrity (ρ_S = 0.771), and social status (ρ_S = 0.732) using cross-alignment measures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Thompson sampling, a standard Bayesian multi-armed bandit method that does not use any representation space. The paper finds a critical threshold: kernel-based agents with representational alignment above roughly 0.3–0.4 outperform Thompson sampling on mean reward and immoral actions, while agents below this threshold perform worse than the baseline (i.e., leveraging a misaligned representation is actively harmful). In the human-data experiments, the best-aligned model (GPT-4, ρ_S = 0.396) achieves substantially higher mean morality reward and fewer immoral actions than the worst-aligned models (Doc2Vec, multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1, both ρ_S = 0.063). In the text-based RL experiment with 9 LLMs, a ceiling effect limits the range of performance (most LLMs take fewer than 3 immoral actions on average), attenuating correlations to ρ_S = 0.1 for mean reward (p = 0.013) and ρ_S = −0.16 for immoral actions (p < 0.0001), though effects remain statistically significant. The control experiment confirms that a length-based kernel outperforms the human kernel for a trivial length-based reward but fails catastrophically on human value tasks (e.g., 594 bad actions vs. 38 for the human kernel on morality), validating that the benefit is specific to value-aligned representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of representational alignment research (Sucholutsky & Griffiths 2023; Marjieh et al. 2022; Muttenthaler et al. 2022) and value alignment/safe exploration in reinforcement learning (Garcia & Fernandez 2012; Achiam & Amodei 2019). While prior work has shown that human-aligned representations improve generalization and robustness in classification tasks, and Rane et al. (2023) argued concept alignment is a prerequisite for value alignment, this paper is the first to directly study how representational alignment affects safe exploration of human values in a reinforcement learning setting where the agent has autonomy to act. It repurposes classical kernel methods (kernel regression, SVR, Gaussian process regression) as a controlled vehicle for precisely measuring and manipulating representational alignment, rather than relying on deep learning architectures where representations are learned end-to-end.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical prediction (error grows monotonically as representational alignment decreases, with cross-phase misalignment in K* having a larger effect than within-phase misalignment in K) and validates it across 10 human values, offering a principled argument for using representational alignment as a criterion when selecting model architectures, training data, or hyperparameters for value-aligned systems. Key limitations include the restricted action space (50 actions from one ETHICS category), English-speaking US participants, kernel-based agents rather than deep learning, and the 'difficulty' value being an exception. Open questions include whether the effect holds for individual and cultural variation in values, whether it transfers to deep learning architectures, and how it interacts with the action selection strategies LLMs actually use in text-based RL.
