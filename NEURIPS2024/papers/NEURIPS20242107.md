## Problem

Predictive combinatorial optimization (CO) requires jointly learning predictions of unknown problem coefficients and solving the resulting optimization, yet existing PtO and PnO methods have been evaluated in fragmented, non-comparable settings with no unified categorization of design choices. No benchmark covers the full spectrum of optimization types (ILP, QP, submodular, top-k) with industrially scaled real-world data, making it unclear which PnO design choices are effective for which scenarios.

## Input / Output

Each problem instance provides a tabular feature vector (e.g., 9-dim weather/calendar features for energy scheduling, 1433-dim bag-of-words for bipartite matching, or 41-dim encrypted user features for combinatorial advertising) that a shared two-layer MLP maps to predicted optimization coefficients. The predicted coefficients are fed into a problem-specific solver (Gurobi, CVXPY, OR-Tools, or a submodular heuristic) to produce discrete or continuous decision variables. Evaluation uses relative regret against the ground-truth optimum when coefficients are available, or an uplift metric (treatment minus control conversion probability) for the combinatorial advertising problem where ground-truth coefficients are fundamentally unobservable.

## Method

The paper builds a modular framework that unifies 11 PtO/PnO methods across 8 CO problems and multiple solvers, categorizing methods along two axes: how discrete variables are handled (discrete-category with on-the-fly gradient interpolation vs. continuous-category with KKT-based automatic differentiation) and how the loss is constructed (statistical-category using solution-ranking losses like NCE/LTR vs. surrogate-category learning a differentiable proxy objective like LODL). The key empirical finding is that PnO outperforms PtO on 7 of 8 benchmarks, but no single category dominates: SPO is best for linear problems with labeled data, listwise-LTR excels on non-linear tasks with solution caches, and discrete-category methods (Blackbox, Identity) are the only viable option when neither labels nor extra optimization samples exist. The paper also introduces a new industrial combinatorial advertising dataset (2933-variable ILP with uncertain conversion rates across 2^C channel combinations) and conducts sensitivity analyses showing PnO methods degrade under tighter constraints, larger variable sizes, and distribution shifts in optimization parameters.

## Results

Across 7 standard benchmarks, PnO methods outperform the PtO (Two-stage) baseline on 7 out of 8 problems. The most dramatic gains appear on Budget Allocation, where SPO reduces regret from 20.33% (Two-stage) to 5.56%, and on Knapsack (Energy), where listwise-LTR achieves 8.08% regret versus 8.75% for Two-stage. On Portfolio Optimization, LODL attains the lowest regret (0.160) compared to 0.243 for Two-stage. The sole exception is Cubic Top-K, where Two-stage (0.110%) slightly edges out the best PnO method (LODL at 0.172%), likely due to the simplicity of the synthetic prediction task. On the new Combinatorial Advertising dataset (evaluated via uplift), Identity achieves the best PnO result (0.135) versus 0.069 for Two-stage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Two-stage (PtO) baseline [Bertsimas & Kallus, 2020] is outperformed by at least one PnO method on 7 of 8 problems, with the largest margin on Budget Allocation (SPO: 5.56% vs. 20.33% regret). SPO [Mandi et al., 2020] is the strongest PnO method on linear problems (Knapsack, Scheduling, Budget Allocation), consistently achieving the lowest or near-lowest regret with moderate training time. listwise-LTR [Mandi et al., 2022] is the most consistently competitive PnO method across problem types, winning or tying on Knapsack (Gen/Energy) and remaining within 1% of the best on Scheduling and Budget Allocation. LODL [Shah et al., 2022] achieves the best regret on Portfolio (0.160 vs. 0.243) and is competitive on Knapsack (Gen) and TopK, but underperforms on Budget Allocation (25.70%) likely due to difficulty learning a surrogate for the non-linear submodular objective. Discrete-category methods (DFL, Blackbox, Identity) [Pogančić et al., 2019; Sahoo et al., 2023; Shah et al., 2022] generally underperform on non-linear tasks (e.g., Blackbox reaches 35.7% regret on Knapsack-Energy vs. 8.4% for SPO) but are the only PnO methods applicable to the Combinatorial Advertising dataset, where Identity (uplift 0.135) and Blackbox (0.134) both substantially outperform Two-stage (0.069). CPLayer [Agrawal et al., 2019] is restricted to linear/convex problems and shows competitive regret on Knapsack (Gen: 24.77%) but incurs O(N³) backward complexity, making it impractical for larger instances. NCE [Mulamba et al., 2021] and pointwise-LTR [Mandi et al., 2022] show moderate performance, with pointwise-LTR degrading notably on Budget Allocation (69.66%) and generalization tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the predictive combinatorial optimization (predict-then-optimize) paradigm initiated by Bertsimas & Kallus [7] and the decision-focused learning (predict-and-optimize) line of work from Wilder et al. [72], Mandi et al. [44, 46], and Shah et al. [60]. Rather than proposing a new method, it unifies 11 existing PtO/PnO approaches—spanning gradient interpolation (Blackbox, Identity, DFL), implicit differentiation (CPLayer, SPO), statistical ranking (NCE, LTR), and surrogate modeling (LODL)—into a single modular framework, extending beyond the linear/convex-only scope of prior implementations like PyEPO [64]. The paper also introduces a new industrial combinatorial advertising dataset, filling a gap where prior benchmarks relied on synthetic or small-scale real data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that while PnO outperforms PtO on 7 of 8 problems, no single PnO design choice dominates, and PnO methods degrade under tighter constraints, larger variable sizes, and distribution shifts—highlighting that the field still lacks a universal, stable training recipe. The finding that discrete-category methods (Blackbox, Identity) often underperform the simple two-stage baseline, and that PtO pre-training can rescue them, offers practical guidance but also signals that gradient interpolation alone is insufficient for complex objectives. Open questions include whether PnO can generalize across optimization parameters without retraining, how to handle problems where ground-truth coefficients are unavailable (as in the advertising setting), and whether surrogate or statistical approaches can reduce their heavy sample-collection overhead.
