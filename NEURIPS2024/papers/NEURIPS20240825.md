## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning benchmarks suffer from data contamination, saturation, and construct validity issues that make it difficult to distinguish genuine multi-step reasoning from memorisation. LINGOLY addresses this by adopting Linguistic Olympiad puzzles in low-resource and extinct languages, where the task is both necessary (cannot be solved without reasoning) and sufficient (all required information is provided in-context), while the rarity of these languages in training data minimises memorisation.

## Input / Output

Each query presents a full puzzle sheet as text — a preamble with language background, a context of example translations or word lists in the target language, and a specific question (sometimes with subquestions) — and the model must produce a short text answer (typically a translation of one to three words or a matched pair). Scoring is two-part: exact match against an exhaustive answer key (normalising unicode differences), and Δ_NC, the improvement over a no-context baseline where the context is removed, which penalises answers derivable from memorised language knowledge or leaked mark schemes. The benchmark spans 1,133 questions across 6 formats (Rosetta, Pattern, Match-up, Monolingual, Computational, Text), 5 difficulty levels, and 90+ language varieties.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed from UK Linguistics Olympiad puzzle sheets, manually parsed into a standardised JSON format with preamble, context, questions, and exhaustive answer keys, after filtering out image-dependent, non-Latin-script, and free-response items. The key evaluation mechanism is the no-context baseline: each question is scored both with the full puzzle context and with the context stripped, and Δ_NC = S(r_Full) − S(r_NC) quantifies how much the model actually uses the provided examples rather than relying on pre-trained language knowledge. Twelve state-of-the-art LLMs (both open-weight and API-based) are evaluated with zero-shot prompting in JSON mode, with chain-of-thought prompting found to have minimal impact. The design ensures that correct answers require multi-step deductive and analogical reasoning — extracting morphological rules, phonological patterns, or syntactic structures from a small set of examples and applying them to novel translations — rather than pattern matching or world knowledge.

## Results

Across 12 state-of-the-art LLMs evaluated on 1,133 Linguistic Olympiad puzzles, the average exact match score is only 20.8%, and the average improvement over the no-context baseline (Δ_NC) is just 12.0%. The top-scoring model, Claude Opus, achieves 46.3% exact match and 28.8% Δ_NC, while the best open model (Mixtral 8x7B) reaches only 14.2% exact match and 6.4% Δ_NC. On the hardest (Round 2) problems, even the top model achieves only 38.7% accuracy, a 24.7% improvement over the no-context baseline, indicating that multi-step reasoning in low-resource linguistic domains remains a significant challenge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All closed-source models (Claude Opus, GPT-4o, GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Command R+) outperform all open-source models (Llama 3 8B/70B, Mixtral 8x7B, Aya 23 35B, Gemma 7B, Llama 2 70B) on both exact match and Δ_NC metrics. Claude Opus leads by a wide margin, achieving 46.3% exact match versus 14.2% for the best open model (Mixtral 8x7B), a gap of over 32 percentage points. The Δ_NC gap is even more pronounced: 28.8% for Claude Opus versus 6.4% for Mixtral, suggesting open models rely more heavily on memorised language knowledge rather than in-context reasoning. Performance degrades consistently with human difficulty level across all models, with the largest drops between Foundation and Intermediate levels. Higher-resource languages yield better scores for exact match (significant positive regression, p<0.05), but this effect weakens or disappears under the Δ_NC metric, particularly for open models, indicating that apparent advantages on high-resource languages partly reflect memorisation rather than genuine reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on linguistic reasoning benchmarks for LLMs, most directly the Rosetta Stone paired-translation tasks of Sahin et al. (2020) and Chi et al. (2024), and the low-resource-language approach of Tanzer et al. (2023/2024) that uses scarce languages as a natural anti-contamination mechanism. It departs from these prior efforts in three key ways: (i) it draws on real, human-authored Olympiad puzzles rather than synthetic examples, (ii) it broadens the task from a single Rosetta format to six distinct puzzle types (including Match-up, Pattern, Computational, Monolingual, and Text), and (iii) it introduces a no-context baseline (Δ_NC) that explicitly penalises memorisation by comparing full-prompt performance against a context-stripped prompt. The construct-validity framing (necessity + sufficiency of reasoning) positions the benchmark within the broader debate over whether autoregressive models genuinely reason or merely recall.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LINGOLY provides a contamination-resistant, multi-format benchmark that exposes a clear ceiling on current LLMs' multi-step linguistic reasoning: even the best model (Claude Opus) reaches only 46.3% exact match and 28.8% Δ_NC, with scores dropping sharply on harder problems. The error analysis reveals that many failures stem not from linguistic knowledge gaps but from instruction-following breakdowns (e.g., copying context words, matching letters alphabetically), suggesting that auxiliary-task demands disproportionately limit performance. Open questions include whether the exact-match scoring understates partial reasoning, whether the benchmark's text-only, Latin-script restriction generalises to multimodal or non-Latin settings, and whether future models trained on more multilingual data will erode the low-resource anti-contamination property. As a publicly released benchmark with code and a Hugging Face dataset, it is likely to become a standard reference for evaluating out-of-domain reasoning in LLMs.
