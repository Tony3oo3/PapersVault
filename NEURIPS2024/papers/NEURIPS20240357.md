## Problem

Prior work on distribution testing with predictions assumed very strong prediction models (perfect predictors, (1±ε)-approximate, or c-approximate per-element guarantees), which are often unrealistic and fail under noisy predictions. This paper is the first to study the more natural and weaker assumption that the prediction p̂ is close to the true distribution p in total variation distance, and asks whether such a prediction can reduce sample complexity for uniformity, identity, and closeness testing while remaining robust to prediction errors of unknown magnitude.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives sample access to the unknown distribution p, the full description of a predicted distribution p̂ over [n], and either a known distribution q (identity/uniformity) or sample access to q (closeness), along with parameters α (suggested accuracy level), ε (farness threshold), δ (confidence), and n (domain size). The output is one of three values: accept, reject, or 'inaccurate information'—a new output that signals p̂ is not α-close to p, allowing the outer search loop to retry with a larger α. The search component iteratively doubles the sample budget across rounds, selecting α_i so the tester operates within the current budget, until a conclusive accept/reject is returned.

## Method

The approach decomposes into a search component (iteratively guessing α and doubling sample budget) and a test component (the augmented tester). For closeness testing, the key technique is augmented flattening: bucket sizes m_i are set to ⌊n·p̂_i⌋ + f_i + 1 (combining prediction-based and sample-based frequencies), which reduces the expected ℓ₂²-norm of the flattened p to O(α/s_f + 4/n), yielding Θ(n^{2/3}·α^{1/3}/ε^{4/3} + √n/ε²) samples. For identity/uniformity testing, the algorithm uses the Scheffé set S = {x : p̂(x) < q(x)} as a witness: estimating p(S) to accuracy (d−α)/4 distinguishes whether p ≠ q (reject) or p̂ is not α-close to p (inaccurate information), achieving Θ(min(1/(d−α)², √n/ε²)) samples when d > α. Matching lower bounds are established via multivariate coupling (extending Le Cam's method to three distributions) for identity/uniformity and via reduction plus wishful-thinking arguments for closeness.

## Results

For augmented closeness testing over [n], the paper achieves Θ(n^(2/3)·α^(1/3)/ε^(4/3) + √n/ε²) samples, improving over the standard Θ(n^(2/3)/ε^(4/3) + √n/ε²) bound by a factor of α^(1/3) whenever α < 1. For augmented identity/uniformity testing, the sample complexity drops to Θ(min(1/(d−α)², √n/ε²)) when the prediction–reference distance d exceeds the suggested accuracy α. Empirically, the algorithm achieves >20× sample complexity reduction on theoretically hard instances and up to 40% reduction on real network traffic data, while remaining robust to noisy predictions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prior approaches. Against the standard closeness tester of Chan et al. [30] (no predictions), the augmented algorithm strictly improves sample complexity by a factor of α^(1/3) in the dominant n^(2/3) term whenever α = o(1), while never exceeding the standard bound when α = Θ(1). Against CRS'15 (Canonne, Ron, Servedio [28]), which requires a strong point-wise (1±ε/2)-approximation guarantee for every domain element, the paper's method only assumes a weaker TV-distance bound between p and p̂. Empirically, CRS'15 fails under noisy predictions, whereas the paper's algorithm remains robust and still achieves >20× sample reduction on hard instances and up to 40% reduction on network traffic data. The paper also notes that the stronger prediction models in [28,29,67] provably yield O(√n/ε²) samples for all three problems, circumventing the closeness lower bound, but at the cost of unrealistic assumptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical distribution testing literature (uniformity, identity, and closeness testing) into a learning-augmented setting where a predicted distribution p̂ is available. It builds directly on the flattening technique of Diakonikolas and Kane [42] and the optimal closeness tester of Chan et al. [30], adapting both to exploit prediction information. The key departure from prior augmented-testing work (Canonne & Rubinfeld [29], Canonne et al. [28], Eden et al. [48]) is the use of total variation distance as the prediction quality measure, which is strictly weaker than the per-element multiplicative guarantees those papers required. This makes the first paper to achieve optimal sample complexity under the most natural and least restrictive prediction model for all three core testing problems simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result establishes that even a coarse, aggregate prediction (measured in TV distance) can yield substantial sample complexity reductions—up to 20× on hard instances and 40% on real network traffic data—while guaranteeing the algorithm never performs worse than the standard no-prediction baseline. This resolves a natural open question about whether weaker prediction models suffice, and provides a template (search + augmented test) applicable to other distribution properties. Open questions include extending the analysis to other distance metrics (Lp, KL-divergence, Hellinger), to other distributional properties beyond the three studied, and to interactive or streaming settings. The empirical gap between worst-case guarantees and actual performance on structured data suggests further algorithmic refinements may be possible.
