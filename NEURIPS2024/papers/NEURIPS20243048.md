## Problem

While simplicity biases in neural networks have been well-characterized for feed-forward architectures, it remained unclear whether transformers trained via self-supervised objectives (MLM or next-token prediction) also learn data distributions in a sequential, low-to-high complexity order. Prior work on Pythia showed only unigram-then-bigram learning, and estimating higher-order token interactions on large corpora is prohibitively expensive due to the curse of dimensionality, leaving the full picture of distributional simplicity bias in NLP unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tokenized text sequences from standard NLP corpora (WikiText-103 with vocabulary size 500 and sequence length 64; TinyStories with vocabulary size 10000 and sequence length 128) and produces two types of outputs: (1) synthetic 'clone' datasets that approximate the original distribution but with many-body token interactions truncated to a specified maximum degree, and (2) test loss curves of a standard transformer trained on the original data but evaluated on these clones, which reveal the temporal ordering of interaction learning. The value matrix in the factored-attention generative models is rank-reduced (rank 400) to handle the large vocabulary, and clones are generated by evolving test-set sequences for 20 Metropolis-Hastings sweeps.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a generative architecture of stacked factored-attention layers with quadratic (x²) activation and skip connections, where the depth n rigorously controls the maximum interaction order captured (2^(n-1)+1), as proven analytically by matching the model's conditional distributions to those of a corresponding many-body Boltzmann distribution. These models are trained via MLM on the target corpus and then sampled using a Metropolis-Hastings scheme (following Goyal et al.) to produce clones with truncated interaction degrees. A standard BERT or GPT model is then trained on the original data while being tested on clones of varying interaction order; the resulting test-loss plateaus on low-degree clones (saturating early) versus continued improvement on high-degree clones provide direct evidence of sequential learning. A gradient-flow analysis for two-layer factored attention with d=1 further explains the mechanism: with zero initialization, the last layer activates first to capture two-body interactions, and higher layers become unstable (and thus activate) only when the data contains correlations beyond the order they can represent.

## Results

On TinyStories, a four-layer BERT encoder trained via MLM shows test loss on 3-body interaction clones saturating after ~3×10⁴ steps, on 9-body clones saturating after ~3×10⁴ steps, while 33-body and BERT-sampled clones continue decreasing throughout training (up to 10⁵ steps). On WikiText-103, low-degree interaction clones (≤3-body) saturate after ~60 SGD epochs while high-degree clones keep improving. In the controlled synthetic 4-body experiment, one, two, and three layers of factored attention with x² activation recover the best 2-body, 3-body, and 4-body approximations respectively, with distinct loss plateaus separating each stage. A two-layer GPT-Neo trained on next-token prediction shows the same sequential pattern, with low-order clone loss saturating within the first 500 training steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between multi-layer factored attention generative models (2, 4, and 6 layers, capturing up to 3-, 9-, and 33-body interactions) and a standard four-layer BERT encoder, all trained on TinyStories via MLM. The factored attention models achieve systematically lower test loss as depth increases, with the 6-layer model approaching BERT's final loss, confirming that deeper factored attention captures more of the true data distribution. When BERT is trained on the original TinyStories and tested on clones generated from both factored attention and BERT itself, the test loss on low-degree clones (3-body, 9-body) plateaus while high-degree clones (33-body, BERT-sampled) continue to improve, demonstrating that BERT sequentially learns higher-order interactions. The factored attention clones are validated as statistically consistent: a standard transformer trained on TinyStories shows nearly identical test loss on 20-sweep and 70-sweep clones from both factored attention and BERT sources, confirming the clones faithfully represent the underlying distribution at their respective interaction orders.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributional simplicity bias framework (Refinetti et al. [13], Bardone & Goldt [14]) from feed-forward networks to transformers in NLP, going beyond Belrose et al. [15] who showed only unigram→bigram sequential learning in Pythia. The core architectural tool is factored attention from Rende et al. [17], which the authors augment with quadratic activations and skip connections so that network depth rigorously controls the maximum interaction order (2^(n-1)+1). For sampling the resulting bidirectional generative models, they adopt the Metropolis-Hastings scheme of Goyal et al. [21], which is necessary because standard Gibbs sampling of MLM conditionals can be inconsistent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clone-based methodology provides a principled, reusable framework for dissecting which interaction orders in a dataset drive transformer learning dynamics, and the finding that this sequential structure holds for both BERT (MLM) and GPT-Neo (next-token prediction) suggests it is a general property of transformer training rather than an artifact of a specific objective. Key limitations temper the impact: the sampling procedure may leave residual correlations, the theoretical analysis is restricted to d=1 and does not yield quantitative timescales or sample complexities, and experiments use small models (4-layer BERT, 2-layer GPT-Neo) on two datasets. Extending the clone framework to other modalities (e.g., protein sequences) and developing a quantitative theory of the sequential learning dynamics are natural next steps.
