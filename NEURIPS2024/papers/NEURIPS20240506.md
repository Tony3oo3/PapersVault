## Problem

Standard compute scaling laws require training models across many scales within a single family, limiting them to 3–5 data points and making them impractical for studying downstream capabilities, emergent behaviors, and post-training interventions. While hundreds of public models now span a wide range of scales, their heterogeneous training compute efficiencies prevent direct application of compute-based scaling laws across families. The paper asks whether a shared, low-dimensional capability space can unify these models into a single predictive scaling framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240506/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standardized benchmark scores (MMLU, ARC-C, HellaSwag, Winogrande, GSM8K, TruthfulQA, XWinogrande, HumanEval) from ~100 public models, training FLOPs for a single reference family (Llama-2), and a target downstream metric normalized to [0,1]. It outputs a fitted sigmoidal scaling curve as a function of capability-equivalent FLOPs, point predictions for held-out stronger models, and interpretable weight vectors over the base benchmarks. Notably, the framework handles proprietary models (e.g., GPT-4) that have no public compute information, and the V-optimality selection step reduces the required evaluation budget to as few as 12 models while preserving prediction accuracy.

## Method

The core mechanism is a three-stage pipeline: (1) PCA on the benchmark-model matrix extracts K=3 principal capability measures S that explain ~97% of variance and are interpretable as general, reasoning, and programming capabilities; (2) a sigmoid regression E ≈ h·σ(βᵀS + α) is fitted to predict downstream performance from these capabilities, with h constrained to [0.8, 1.0] to handle floor-performance discrepancies; (3) the scalar capability P = β*ᵀS + α* is projected onto a shared FLOPs scale via linear regression within the reference family, yielding 'Llama-2-equivalent FLOPs' for all models. The key insight is that model families differ only in their compute-to-capability conversion efficiency (θ_f, ν_f in Eq. 4), while all downstream metrics derive from the same capability space, enabling cross-family generalization. For practical deployment, a V-optimality criterion from optimal experimental design selects the minimal model subset that minimizes expected prediction error Tr(SᵀS(S_MᵀS_M)⁻¹).

## Results

The top 3 principal components of the benchmark-model matrix explain ~97% of variance, with PC-1 alone capturing nearly 80% of variation in LM capabilities. Within each model family, PC measures correlate linearly with log-training FLOPs (R² > 0.9), validating their use as surrogate scale measures. Observational scaling laws fitted on models with ≤ Llama-2-7B FLOPs (47 models) accurately extrapolate to 30 stronger models, significantly outperforming training FLOPs and model size baselines in MSE across emergent capabilities, agentic benchmarks, and post-training interventions. Using V-optimality selection, only 12 of 47 models are needed to maintain near-full-set prediction accuracy for post-training scaling analyses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares observational scaling laws (using PCA-derived PC measures) against two compute-based baselines: training FLOPs and model size. On all three validation tasks (emergent BigBench tasks, AgentBench/AgentBoard, and GSM8K with CoT/SC), the PC-measure-based observational scaling achieves substantially lower test MSE than both FLOPs and model-size baselines, with the gap attributed to the incomparability of training FLOPs across heterogeneous model families. For agentic capabilities, the observational law predicts GPT-4 and Claude-2 performance from models with >10% lower benchmark scores, a prediction that FLOPs-based scaling cannot make since proprietary models lack public compute information. For post-training interventions, the fitted sigmoidal curves show CoT has a more pronounced scaling trend than Self-Consistency, and the weight visualization reveals CoT benefits are driven primarily by MMLU and HumanEval capabilities. The V-optimality model selection (Sec. 5) significantly outperforms random selection in prediction error, converging to full-set accuracy with as few as 12 models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes standard compute scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020) and their sigmoidal extensions for downstream performance (Finnveden, 2020; Owen, 2024) from single-model-family, training-FLOPs-based formulations to a cross-family 'observational' framework built from ~100 publicly available models. The key conceptual departure is replacing training compute as the scaling axis with a low-dimensional PCA-derived capability space extracted from standard benchmarks, which the authors show is log-linearly related to compute within each family but shared across families. The model-subset selection in Sec. 5 draws directly on V-optimality from optimal experimental design (Pukelsheim, 2006), and the low-rank benchmark structure connects to prior psychometric analyses of LLM leaderboards (Ilić, 2023; Burnell et al., 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: practitioners can now build high-resolution scaling predictions for emergent behaviors, agentic capabilities, and post-training interventions (CoT, Self-Consistency) using only 10–20 publicly available models rather than training across scales, and the preregistered predictions validated on Llama-3.1-405B and Qwen2-72B demonstrate genuine extrapolative power. Key limitations include applicability primarily to post-training (not pretraining) scaling, sensitivity to benchmark contamination, and inability to capture within-family heterogeneity in compute efficiency. Open questions include whether the capability space remains low-dimensional as model architectures diverge further (MoE, hybrid Mamba-Transformer) and whether the framework extends to fine-tuning or test-time compute scaling regimes.
