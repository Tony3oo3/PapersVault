## Problem

Fragment-based molecule generation methods are largely constrained by the existing fragment library, as they can only reassemble or locally modify known fragments, severely limiting exploration of chemical space. The paper addresses the challenge of discovering novel, high-quality fragments that go beyond the initial vocabulary while still effectively exploiting existing chemical knowledge for target property optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244210/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a molecule dataset (ZINC250k) with a target property function, decomposes molecules into arms (one attachment point) and linkers (two attachment points) via an arm-linker-arm slicing algorithm, and scores each fragment by its average contribution to the target property. During generation, two hard fragments are retrieved as explicit input to the language model, while K=10 soft fragments from the complementary vocabulary are injected as implicit guidance. The output is a set of optimized molecules in SAFE representation (dot-connected fragment sequences), along with a dynamically updated fragment vocabulary and molecule population that evolve over iterative generation cycles.

## Method

f-RAG augments the frozen SAFE-GPT molecular language model with a lightweight fragment injection module (2.64% of total parameters) that uses cross-attention to fuse soft fragment embeddings into the input sequence at an early layer (L=1). Hard fragments are explicitly included as input context to force their presence in the generated molecule, while soft fragments—retrieved from the complementary vocabulary (linkers when hard fragments are arms, and vice versa)—guide the model to propose novel fragments through the injection module. The module is trained self-supervised to predict the most similar fragment to a held-out fragment given its K nearest neighbors as soft fragments, making the training target-property-agnostic. A post-hoc genetic algorithm (crossover and mutation on molecular graphs) further modifies generated fragments, and both the fragment vocabulary and molecule population are dynamically updated after each generation cycle, enabling iterative exploration beyond the initial chemical space.

## Results

On the PMO benchmark (23 tasks), f-RAG achieves a sum AUC top-10 of 16.928, outperforming the next-best method SELFIES-REINVENT (16.213) and winning 12 out of 23 individual tasks. In terms of the multi-metric trade-off, f-RAG attains the best average diversity (0.532), the best average SA score (2.026, lower is better), and the second-best novelty (0.800) among all compared methods. On the docking score optimization task with QED/SA/novelty constraints, f-RAG outperforms all 16 baselines across all five protein targets (e.g., −12.945 kcal/mol on parp1 vs. −12.891 for GEAM, the closest competitor).

## Baselines & Comparisons

f-RAG achieves the highest sum AUC top-10 (16.928) on the PMO benchmark, beating SELFIES-REINVENT (16.213), GP BO (14.708), STONED (14.196), and SMILES GA (12.054). Among the fragment-based and GA-based baselines, Mol GA achieves the highest novelty (0.845) but at the cost of the worst SA score (4.605) and lower optimization performance; f-RAG trades a small novelty gap (0.800 vs. 0.845) for substantially better synthesizability (2.026 vs. 4.605) and optimization. On the docking score task, f-RAG outperforms the closest fragment-based competitor GEAM on all five targets (e.g., parp1: −12.945 vs. −12.891; 5ht1b: −12.670 vs. −12.374) and also beats retrieval-based RetMol by a large margin (e.g., fa7: −9.899 vs. −5.448). In the additional MPO comparison (Table 7), f-RAG significantly outperforms GEAM on all seven multi-property optimization tasks (e.g., Amlodipine: 0.704 vs. 0.626; Sitagliptin: 0.458 vs. 0.417), confirming consistent gains across task types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends fragment-based drug discovery (FBDD) by introducing retrieval-augmented generation at the fragment level, departing from prior fragment-based methods (RationaleRL, MARS, FREED, GEAM) that only reassemble or locally modify existing fragments. It builds on SAFE-GPT as a frozen backbone molecular language model, adapts the cross-attention retrieval mechanism from RetMol (Wang et al., 2023) from whole-molecule retrieval to fine-grained fragment-level retrieval with two distinct roles (hard and soft), and incorporates genetic operations from Graph GA (Jensen, 2019) for post-hoc fragment modification. The key conceptual departure is the dual retrieval strategy: hard fragments are explicitly included in the output molecule, while soft fragments are injected via a lightweight trainable module to implicitly guide generation of novel fragments, enabling exploration beyond the initial vocabulary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

f-RAG addresses a fundamental limitation in fragment-based generation—the inability to discover genuinely new fragments—by dynamically updating the fragment vocabulary during generation, allowing the model to propose fragments that outperform the best in the training set. The practical significance is high: only 2.64% of parameters (the fragment injection module) require training, making the approach efficient and broadly applicable across target properties without retraining. Limitations include reliance on the backbone model's generation quality and the arm-linker-arm decomposition, which may not capture all chemically meaningful fragment boundaries. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to 3D structure-based generation, larger fragment vocabularies, or multi-target optimization settings.
