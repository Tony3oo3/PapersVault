## Problem

State-of-the-art human-machine teaming (HMT) methods trained via imitation or reinforcement learning produce brittle, individualized agents that decouple from human teammates rather than collaborating synergistically, and their black-box representations prevent humans from understanding or adapting the AI's behavior. The paper identifies that no existing framework enables end-users to iteratively reprogram an interpretable AI teammate policy across repeated teaming episodes to develop effective collaboration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242072/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent reasons over a 13-dimensional binary state vector encoding objects held, pot statuses, and counter objects (semantic features, not pixels), and outputs macro-actions (e.g., 'get an onion', 'place ingredient on counter') that are executed via A* path planning with dynamic replanning at each timestep. The learned policy is a decision tree with categorical probability distributions at leaf nodes, constrained to a maximum depth of four (16 leaves) for user interpretability. Users interact with the policy through a GUI that supports adding/removing decision nodes, changing split features, and modifying leaf actions and their probabilities.

## Method

The core architecture is the Interpretable Discrete Control Tree (IDCT), a differentiable decision tree trained end-to-end via PPO where each decision node uses a sigmoid split on a single input dimension with Boolean outcomes enforced through differentiable crispification (straight-through trick), and leaf nodes hold sparse categorical distributions over macro-actions regularized by an L1 sparsity penalty. A post-hoc contextual pruning algorithm reduces large trained trees (up to 256 leaves) to small equivalent representations (2–3 leaves) by detecting impossible subspaces via variable range bounds and redundant splits via ancestor-node hierarchy, enabling ease-of-training without sacrificing interpretability. The human-led policy modification scheme creates a feedback loop: after each teaming episode, users visualize the tree and interactively reprogram decision nodes, split features, and leaf actions via a GUI, enabling iterative mixed-initiative team development. Training uses agent-agent collaborative training in PantheonRL, pairing the IDCT agent with a synthetic human teammate, and the full system is evaluated in a 50-participant between-subjects study across two Overcooked-AI domains.

## Results

In the 50-subject user study, Fictitious Co-Play (IV1-C5, black-box NN) achieves the highest maximum team reward in both domains, significantly outperforming all tree-based conditions in Forced Coordination (p<0.001 to p<0.05) and Optional Collaboration (p<0.001). However, Human-Led Policy Modification (IV1-C1, white-box with interactive editing) significantly outperforms the other white-box conditions (IV1-C2, C3, C4) in Optional Collaboration (p<0.05), and both IV1-C1 and IV1-C2 show significant team development (improvement across 4 iterations, p<0.01) in Optional Collaboration, while no condition improves in Forced Coordination. All learning-based approaches underperform a simple collaborative heuristic (408 cumulative score vs. 295.02 for the best NN policy and 171.46 for the IDCT in Optional Collaboration). Subjectively, users rate IV1-C1 and IV1-C5 as more fluent and positive than IV1-C2 and IV1-C4 (p<0.01, p<0.05), with no significant subjective difference between IV1-C1 and IV1-C5 despite the latter's objective performance advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Carroll et al. [5] (HA-PPO, NeurIPS 2019), the paper demonstrates a fundamental gap: their publicly available agents score 0 under a human-preferred collaborative strategy (freezing ~80% of the episode) and only 40 under their own individualized strategy, showing rigidity and an inability to adapt to human-preferred coordination. The paper's IDCT + interactive modification paradigm directly addresses this by enabling users to reprogram the agent's policy after each episode. Against Strouse et al. [40] (Fictitious Co-Play, NeurIPS 2021), the paper's black-box NN baseline (IV1-C5) wins on objective team reward in both domains (significantly better than all four IDCT-based conditions), confirming that population-based training produces stronger initial policies. However, Fictitious Co-Play shows no team development across iterations, whereas the paper's Human-Led Policy Modification (IV1-C1) and AI-Led Policy Modification (IV1-C2) both show significant improvement over 4 iterations in Optional Collaboration (p<0.01), suggesting a developmental advantage of interactive white-box approaches. Subjectively, IV1-C1 and IV1-C5 are rated equivalently on fluency and positivity, indicating that the interpretability and interactivity of IV1-C1 compensates for its lower objective score. The critical negative result is that all learning-based methods (both the paper's and the baselines') underperform a simple collaborative heuristic by 30–58%, highlighting that current RL approaches converge to individualized coordination rather than true collaboration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on differentiable decision trees (Suárez & Lutsko, 1999) and differentiable crispification (Paleja et al., 2022) to create the Interpretable Discrete Control Tree (IDCT), a white-box architecture trainable via PPO for multi-agent sequential decision-making. It departs from the dominant HMT paradigm of producing a single, out-of-the-box collaborative policy (Carroll et al., 2019; Strouse et al., 2021) by introducing an iterative, mixed-initiative team development loop where users can inspect and directly modify the agent's tree-based policy between episodes. The contextual pruning algorithm, inspired by neural network pruning and the lottery ticket hypothesis, addresses the tension between training large expressive trees and maintaining human-readable model size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The critical negative result—that all learning-based HMT approaches underperform a simple collaborative heuristic—challenges the field's assumption that RL-trained agents automatically produce effective teaming behavior, revealing that population-based training can bias agents toward individualized coordination. The finding that interpretability alone (viewing the tree) yields no objective performance benefit, but interpretability combined with interactive modification enables measurable team development (the Norming stage in Tuckman's model), provides concrete design guidance for future HMT systems. Open questions include whether white-box collaborative agents can close the performance gap with black-box methods, how to design learning objectives that incentivize true collaboration over individual coordination, and how to make policy-modification interfaces accessible to users without engineering backgrounds.
