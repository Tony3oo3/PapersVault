## Problem

Structured state space models (SSMs) such as S4 and Mamba commonly use complex parameterizations, yet the theoretical reasons for their benefit over real parameterizations remain unexplained. Empirical evidence in the literature is mixed—some tasks show clear advantages for complex parameters while others do not—leaving the extent and mechanism of this benefit as an open question.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243679/cropped_page30_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The SSMs under analysis receive a real scalar series (u(t)) as input and produce a real scalar series (y(t)) as output, with the output obtained by taking the real part of a complex state-space recursion. The theoretical framework identifies each SSM with its impulse response (a real scalar series), and approximation quality is measured by L1 distance between truncated impulse responses up to time t. Experiments additionally use CIFAR-10 images (32×32) fed through an S4 architecture for image classification, and synthetic copy/induction-head sequences for Mamba-based task accuracy.

## Method

The paper establishes two formal separations between real and complex diagonal SSMs. First, an expressiveness gap: a 1-dimensional complex SSM can realize oscillatory mappings (via a complex eigenvalue with non-trivial phase) that a real SSM cannot approximate up to time t unless its dimension is Ω(t), while the reverse direction requires only n_C ≥ n_R. Second, a practical learnability gap: even in the overparameterized regime where both SSMs can express a target mapping, the real SSM requires either exponential dimension or exponentially large parameter magnitudes (proven via forward-difference bounds on decaying exponentials), whereas the complex SSM achieves exact matching with dimension and parameter norms at most linear in t (via DFT-based assignment of roots of unity to the diagonal). Experiments corroborate these results on synthetic copy, random, and oscillatory mappings, on CIFAR-10 via S4, and on Mamba with selectivity ablations showing that selectivity can partially offset the real-parameterization disadvantage.

## Results

In the theoretically analyzed setting (t=32), the real SSM achieves normalized approximation errors of 0.77 (copy), 0.54 (random), and 0.81 (oscillatory), indicating near-complete failure to learn the target mappings. In stark contrast, the complex SSM achieves errors of 1.6×10⁻⁵ (copy), 6.3×10⁻⁵ (random), and 1.6×10⁻⁴ (oscillatory) at t=32, scaling gracefully to t=256 with errors still below 10⁻³. On the real-world CIFAR-10 task (Long Range Arena benchmark), the S4 architecture with complex parameterization achieves 89.10% test accuracy, substantially outperforming the best real-parameterization result of 78.27% (a gap of ~11 points).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between real and complex parameterizations of diagonal SSMs. On CIFAR-10 (Long Range Arena), the complex-parameterized S4 achieves 89.10% test accuracy versus 78.27% for the best real-parameterized S4 across three seeds, a gap of approximately 11 percentage points. In the theoretically analyzed setting, the real SSM (dimension 1024) fails to learn any of the three target mappings (copy, random, oscillatory) with any of three optimizers (Adam, AdamW, RAdam), while the complex SSM (dimension t) achieves errors below 10⁻³ even at t=256. With selectivity (Mamba architecture), the picture is more nuanced: on the copy task, complex parameterization wins decisively (93.05% vs. 80.17%), but on the induction-head task, real parameterization slightly outperforms complex (97.35–98.3% vs. 93.93–97.64%). Ablation experiments reveal that this reversal on induction-head is driven by selectivity of both B and C: when either is made non-selective, complex parameterization regains a clear advantage (e.g., 91.93–96.77% vs. 61.82–71.28% when C is non-selective).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-task result of Orvieto et al. [42]—which showed that a copy/delay task requires exponential parameters for real SSMs but moderate parameters for complex SSMs—into a general theoretical framework covering arbitrary LTI mappings, random (generic) mappings, and oscillatory mappings. It positions itself against the mixed empirical evidence in the SSM literature (Gu et al. [22, 41] showing complex is essential vs. Ma et al. [37] and Gu & Dao [20] showing real can be comparable) by providing the first formal expressiveness and learnability separations between real and complex diagonal SSMs. The analysis draws on classical tools from linear systems theory (Vandermonde matrices, discrete Fourier transforms) and introduces forward-difference-based lower bounds as a new analytical technique for SSM parameterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper resolves a long-standing open question about why SSMs like S4 and Mamba use complex parameterizations, providing the first provable justification that goes beyond empirical observation. The exponentiality result (Theorem 2) has direct practical implications: it explains why real SSMs fail to learn certain mappings even when overparameterized, and suggests that complex parameterizations are not merely a convenience but a necessity for practical training. Key open questions remain: how prevalent is the expressiveness separation, whether formal learnability guarantees can be established for complex SSMs, and how selectivity (a dominant architectural feature in modern SSMs) interacts with the real/complex distinction—the paper's ablation suggests selectivity may be the key factor behind the mixed empirical evidence, but a formal theory accounting for it is still needed.
