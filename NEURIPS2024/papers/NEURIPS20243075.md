## Problem

Deep Thinking (DT) and Deep Thinking with Recall (DT-R) networks can learn iterative algorithms that extrapolate to larger problem instances, but they suffer from severe training instability (overflow, vanishing, NaN losses), require massive overparameterization to train reliably, and offer no guarantee that the learned iterative procedure converges to a solution. The root cause is the absence of any mechanism to control the growth of the intermediate scratchpad representation across iterations.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a spatial grid or matrix (1-channel binary string for prefix sums, 3-channel maze with start/end markers, 12-channel chess board, or 3-channel distance matrix for TSP) and produces a same-sized output grid (solution string, path image, move map, or binary tour-edge matrix). The input is fed to the recurrent block at every iteration via a recall connection, and the number of iterations M can be increased at inference time to solve larger instances than those seen during training. For TSP, the output is constrained to be a valid permutation matrix via a Gumbel-Sinkhorn layer, with a loss term penalizing fractional edges.

## Method

DT-L engineers the recurrent function G to be a contraction mapping with Lipschitz constant K < 1 by applying spectral normalization to each convolutional kernel (dividing weights by spectral norm + ε), guaranteeing convergence to a unique fixed point via the Banach fixed-point theorem. To keep K close to 1 (allowing slow accumulation of global information through local convolutions), residual skip connections are replaced with per-channel parametric linear interpolations (1−γ)·id + γ·B, and the recall connection uses two separate convolutions combined by element-wise addition rather than concatenation, so the constant input offset does not violate the Lipschitz bound. Additional stabilizing modifications include ELU activations, batch normalization outside the recurrent block, and a bias term in the recall convolution. For TSP, the scratchpad is augmented with channels from which an orthogonal matrix is extracted via differentiable SVD and applied to a sub-vector, giving the network the ability to reason about how permuting the tour changes the edge set.

## Results

On the 512-bit prefix sums extrapolation task, DT-L (w=32) achieves >90% accuracy in 28 out of 30 training runs, compared to only 2 out of 30 for DT-R, demonstrating dramatically improved training reliability. On the TSP benchmark (a novel evaluation where DT fails entirely), DT-L achieves mean tour lengths of 3.99±0.006 (symmetric n=15) and 6.02±0.007 (symmetric n=30), substantially outperforming random tours (7.5 and 15.0) but remaining below greedy nearest-neighbor heuristics (2.85/3.50 for NN, 2.31/2.72 for BNN). The models use w=32 channels, representing up to three orders of magnitude fewer parameters than the w=400 DT-R models in prior work, while achieving comparable or better extrapolation performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is DT-R (Bansal et al., NeurIPS 2022), the most recent and strongest prior Deep Thinking variant. On the 512-bit prefix sums extrapolation task, DT-L decisively outperforms DT-R: 28/30 DT-L runs exceed 90% accuracy versus only 2/30 for DT-R, and the mean accuracy curves show DT-L maintaining high accuracy across all iteration counts while DT-R runs frequently degrade or fail. On the mazes problem (17×17 → 33×33 extrapolation), DT-L similarly outperforms DT-R with more consistent per-run accuracy. On chess puzzles, DT-L matches DT-R performance, both hitting a similar apparent ceiling around 80% accuracy on harder problems. On the TSP benchmark (where DT and DT-R fail to learn), DT-L produces tours roughly 47% shorter than random tours but still 25–40% longer than the greedy nearest-neighbor (NN) and best-starting-point (BNN) heuristics, which the authors note are among the best known algorithms for asymmetric non-Euclidean TSP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Deep Thinking (DT) and Deep Thinking with Recall (DT-R) architectures by Schwarzschild et al. and Bansal et al., which use recurrent convolutional networks with a scratchpad to learn iterative algorithms that extrapolate from small to large problem instances. The key departure is engineering the recurrent function G to be a contraction mapping via spectral normalization, grounded in the Banach fixed-point theorem to guarantee convergence to a unique solution. Additional architectural changes—parametric residual connections, separate convolutions for recall instead of concatenation, ELU activations, and batch normalization outside the recurrent block—are motivated by the need to maintain the Lipschitz constraint while improving expressiveness and mitigating the dead-cell problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The convergence guarantee is a meaningful theoretical contribution for a class of models that previously had no such assurance, and the practical consequence—enabling models with up to three orders of magnitude fewer parameters while maintaining or improving extrapolation performance—makes the approach more accessible and reliable. The TSP benchmark demonstrates applicability to NP-hard problems where prior DT/DT-R approaches failed entirely, though results remain well below classical heuristics like nearest-neighbor. Open questions include understanding what algorithms the network actually learns, whether the convolutional inductive bias limits performance on problems requiring long-range dependencies, and how the approach scales to more complex or higher-dimensional problem settings.
