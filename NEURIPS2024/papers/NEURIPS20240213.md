## Problem

Existing MedVLP methods are evaluated under inconsistent datasets, preprocessing pipelines, and finetuning protocols, making it impossible to determine whether a newer method genuinely outperforms an earlier one or simply benefits from better tuning. No unified benchmark exists that standardizes these variables across heterogeneous architectures (ResNet, ViT, Swin Transformer) and diverse pretraining objectives (contrastive learning, masked modeling, semantic matching).

## Input / Output

The benchmark pretrains all MedVLP models exclusively on frontal chest X-rays from MIMIC-CXR (370K+ images) and evaluates them on nine public datasets spanning four tasks. For classification and report generation, images are resized to 256×256 and randomly cropped to 224×224; for segmentation, images are resized to 512×512 without further augmentation. Classification is tested at 1%, 10%, and 100% training data to measure data efficiency, while segmentation and report generation use frozen backbones to isolate representation quality from task-specific head capacity.

## Method

BenchX standardizes three axes of comparison: (1) a fixed pretraining set (MIMIC-CXR frontal images only) and nine evaluation datasets across classification, segmentation, report generation, and image-text retrieval; (2) consistent preprocessing and empirically determined training strategies including layer normalization before the classifier, truncated normal initialization, and discriminative learning rates for encoder versus head; (3) unified task adaptation pipelines that accommodate heterogeneous architectures—UperNet with a frozen backbone for segmentation (replacing architecture-specific U-Net) and R2Gen with a frozen encoder for report generation. The framework benchmarks nine state-of-the-art MedVLP methods (ConVIRT, GLoRIA, MedCLIP, MedKLIP, M-FLAG, MGCA, MRM, PTUnifier, REFERS) under identical conditions. A key finding is that minor training adjustments boost ConVIRT's NIH AUROC from 71.7% to 77.0%, making it competitive with or superior to more recent methods like MedCLIP and MedKLIP, while MRM and MGCA-ViT emerge as the most consistently strong performers across tasks.

## Results

Across nine MedVLP methods evaluated on four downstream tasks (multi-label classification, binary classification, segmentation, and report generation), MRM achieves the best overall average rank (2.00) with top scores in multi-label classification AUROC (86.18%) and segmentation mDice (80.66%). MGCA-ViT is a close second (avg. rank 2.38), leading in multi-label classification (86.10% AUROC) and report generation BLEU-4 (17.0). Notably, ConVIRT—one of the earliest MedVLP methods—rises to competitive levels (avg. rank 6.38) when equipped with proper training strategies (layer normalization, truncated normal initialization, discriminative learning rates), while M-FLAG consistently underperforms (avg. rank 10.00) across all tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MRM (masked record modeling) and MGCA-ViT (multigranularity cross-modal alignment) emerge as the two strongest methods, with MRM winning segmentation (80.66 mDice on TBX11K) and multi-label classification (85.3 AUROC on NIH at 100%), while MGCA-ViT leads in image-text retrieval (63.3% Hit@1) and report generation (17.0 BLEU-4). ConVIRT, despite being one of the earliest MedVLP methods, achieves 84.2% AUROC on NIH and 90.9% on VinDr at 100% data—surpassing GLoRIA (83.8/91.3) and MedCLIP-R50 (83.9/89.8) on NIH—when proper training strategies are applied, a finding the authors argue calls for revisiting prior conclusions. MedCLIP-ViT and REFERS occupy the middle tier, with MedCLIP-ViT excelling in binary classification (75.7% F1 on SIIM) but underperforming in retrieval (42.0% Hit@1 vs. 61.9% for ConVIRT). M-FLAG is the clear laggard, scoring 77.73% AUROC on multi-label classification and 72.77% mDice on segmentation—roughly 8–9 points below the leaders—likely due to its frozen language model design limiting representation quality. PTUnifier achieves the highest retrieval Hit@K (78.7% Hit@1) but suffers from low Precision@K (23.4% P@10), indicating its text embeddings do not align well semantically with image embeddings. Overall, no single method dominates all tasks, and the performance gaps between recent methods (MedCLIP, MedKLIP, REFERS) and earlier ones (ConVIRT, GLoRIA) are smaller than previously reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

BenchX carves new ground as the first unified benchmark specifically for Medical Vision-Language Pretraining (MedVLP), addressing gaps left by TorchXRayVision (vision-only, no multimodal evaluation) and ViLMedic (limited MedVLP coverage, no standardized task adaptation across heterogeneous architectures). Rather than proposing a new pretraining method, it repurposes the evaluation infrastructure of nine existing MedVLP methods—spanning contrastive learning (ConVIRT, GLoRIA, MedCLIP, MGCA), knowledge-enhanced alignment (MedKLIP, M-FLAG), masked modeling (MRM, REFERS), and unified prompting (PTUnifier)—under a single protocol with standardized preprocessing, data splits, and task-specific heads (UperNet for segmentation, R2Gen for report generation). The framework's key conceptual departure from prior benchmarks is the unified task adaptation pipeline that handles both CNN-based (ResNet) and ViT-based backbones without favoring either architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ConVIRT, one of the earliest MedVLP methods, becomes competitive with or superior to newer approaches like MedCLIP and MedKLIP when properly tuned challenges the field's narrative of steady methodological progress and suggests that prior comparisons may have been confounded by inconsistent evaluation protocols. This calls for a reevaluation of conclusions drawn in prior MedVLP papers and highlights that finetuning strategies (layer normalization, discriminative learning rates, initialization) can matter as much as the pretraining objective itself. Limitations include restriction to chest X-rays, exclusion of crossmodal tasks like visual question answering, and the acknowledgment that some methods may still be suboptimally configured. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to other medical imaging modalities and whether the rapid integration of LLMs for report generation will shift the evaluation landscape.
