## Problem

Between the polynomial-time algorithm for linear-swap regret (Farina and Pipis, 2023) and the quasipolynomial-time algorithm for full swap regret (Dagan et al., 2024; Peng and Rubinstein, 2024), virtually no efficient algorithms were known for intermediate classes of deviations in extensive-form games. This paper fills that gap by developing parameterized algorithms for k-mediator deviations and degree-k polynomial swap deviations, yielding complexity bounds that smoothly interpolate between the two extremes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243975/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a tree-form decision problem (game tree with N terminal nodes and depth d, assumed binary branching), a sequence of linear utility vectors u^(t) with |⟨u^(t), x⟩| ≤ 1, and a deviation parameter k. Crucially, the learner must output a mixed strategy π^(t) ∈ Δ(X) (a distribution over pure strategies) rather than a point in conv(X); this distinction is essential because operating over behavioral strategies makes even quadratic-deviation regret minimization PPAD-hard. The output guarantee is that the time-average Φ-regret is at most ε after N^{O(k)}/ε² rounds (for k-mediator deviations) or N^{O(kd)³}/ε² rounds (for degree-k polynomial deviations).

## Method

The approach has two key ingredients. First, it replaces the (approximate) fixed-point oracle required by the Gordon et al. [2008] template with an 'expected fixed point': a distribution π over pure strategies satisfying E_{x~π}[φ(x) − x] ≈ 0, which is computed efficiently via a telescoping argument over L iterates of the extended deviation map, avoiding the PPAD-hardness of computing actual fixed points. Second, it characterizes the deviation sets combinatorially: k-mediator deviations are identified with reduced strategies in the interleaving X ⊗ X̄^⊗k (a DAG-form decision problem of size N^{O(k)}), and degree-k polynomial deviations are reduced to low-depth decision trees via the Boolean-analysis result that every degree-k polynomial on {0,1}^N is a depth-2k³ decision tree (Midrijanis, 2004), yielding Φ_poly^k ⊆ Φ_med^{O(kd)³} in the extensive-form setting. Regret minimization over these structured deviation sets then follows from standard no-regret algorithms for tree-form and DAG-form decision problems (e.g., counterfactual regret minimization), with the expected-fixed-point reduction providing the outer loop.

## Results

The paper establishes parameterized regret bounds interpolating between linear-swap (k=1) and full swap regret (k=N). For k-mediator deviations Φ_med^k, the algorithm achieves ε-regret in N^{O(k)}/ε² rounds with N^{O(k)}/ε per-round time (Theorem 3.4). For degree-k polynomial deviations Φ_poly^k, the bound is N^{O(kd)³}/ε² rounds, where d is the game tree depth—polynomial for Bayesian games and quasipolynomial under the standard balancedness assumption d=polylog N. As a byproduct, the paper gives the fastest known algorithm for computing ε-correlated equilibria in normal-form games in the medium-precision regime (1/A^{ω/2−1} ≤ ε ≤ 1/log A), with time O(A log A/ε² · (EO(n,A) + nA²/ε)), obviating the need to solve a linear system per round.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the swap-regret algorithms of Dagan et al. [2024] and Peng & Rubinstein [2024] (complexity N^{Õ(1/ε)}), this paper provides a strictly better bound in the regime ε ≤ poly(1/N) by restricting to structured deviations: N^{O(k)}/ε² for k-mediator deviations is polynomial in N for any fixed k, whereas the swap-regret bound is quasipolynomial. Compared to Farina & Pipis [2023] (linear-swap regret, poly(N)/ε²), the k=1 case recovers their result exactly, while k > 1 gives a smooth interpolation toward full swap regret. In the normal-form CE computation comparison (Table 1), the paper's method dominates Anagnostides et al. [2022] and Daskalakis et al. [2021] in the medium-precision regime by replacing the nA^ω term (requiring fast matrix multiplication) with nA²/ε, and outperforms Huang & Pan [2023] by a factor of A/ε in the leading term. The Dagan/Peng-Rubinstein bound nA·log^{1/ε}(nA) is superior when ε is an absolute constant but degrades to exponential in 1/ε, whereas the paper's bound remains polynomial in 1/ε. The PPAD-hardness result (Theorem 3.3) shows that the mixed-strategy (distribution over pure strategies) formulation is essential—no polynomial-time algorithm exists for behavioral strategies even for quadratic deviations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Gordon et al. [2008] template for Φ-regret minimization by replacing the computationally expensive fixed-point oracle with an efficient 'expected fixed point' computation, thereby circumventing the PPAD-hardness barrier identified via the Hazan and Kale [2007] equivalence between regret minimization and fixed-point computation. It generalizes the k=1 (linear-swap) result of Farina and Pipis [2023] and the mediator interpretation of Zhang et al. [2024] to arbitrary k, while also building on the DAG-form decision problem framework of Zhang et al. [2023] and the Boolean analysis result of Midrijanis [2004] connecting low-degree polynomials to low-depth decision trees. The paper positions itself as filling the complexity gap between the polynomial-time linear-swap regret minimization and the quasipolynomial-time full swap regret minimization of Dagan et al. [2024] and Peng and Rubinstein [2024].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first parameterized complexity landscape for Φ-regret minimization in extensive-form games, showing that for fixed degree k the problem is polynomial (for k-mediator deviations) or quasipolynomial (for low-degree polynomial deviations) under standard balancedness assumptions. The PPAD-hardness result for behavioral strategies reveals that the choice between mixed strategies and behavioral strategies is not merely a representational convenience but a fundamental computational distinction. Key open questions include whether the exponential dependence on game-tree depth d can be eliminated, whether k = Õ(1/ε) mediators suffice to capture full swap regret, and the computational complexity of computing exact Φ-equilibria in the centralized model. The expected fixed point relaxation is a general technique with independent utility, already yielding the fastest known algorithm for ε-correlated equilibria in normal-form games in the moderate-precision regime.
