## Problem

In standard reinforcement learning, agents observe rewards only after acting, yet in many practical settings (transaction prices, traffic estimates, goal locations) partial future reward information is available in advance. No prior work quantifies the multiplicative performance gain from such lookahead information in tabular RL. This paper fills that gap by deriving exact competitive ratios between no-lookahead agents and agents with L-step future reward visibility, for the full spectrum L ∈ [H].
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242659/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a tabular MDP specified by its transition kernel P, expected rewards r, state/action spaces (|S|=S, |A|=A), horizon H, and a lookahead parameter L ∈ [H]. It produces closed-form competitive ratio expressions: for fixed (P,r), the CR is a ratio of two occupancy-weighted sums; for worst-case reward expectations, it reduces to a min-max over Markovian policies involving conditional reaching probabilities; and for worst-case environments, it yields explicit lower bounds of max{1/SAH, 1/((H−L+1)A^L)} matched by near-tight upper bounds from tree-like MDPs. The worst-case reward distributions are characterized as long-shot (Bernoulli with vanishing probability), and the resulting CRs are shown to be inverses of coverability coefficients from offline RL and reward-free exploration.

## Method

The core technique is to identify long-shot reward distributions (a single high reward realized with probability ε → 0) as the worst case, which makes the L-lookahead value decompose into a sum over conditional occupancy measures d_{t_L(h)}^π(s') · d_h^*(s | s_{t_L(h)}=s'), where t_L(h)=max{h−L+1,1} is the timestep at which h-step rewards become visible. The no-lookahead value is expressed as the standard occupancy-weighted sum d^πᵀr. To optimize over worst-case reward expectations, the paper applies the minimax theorem on the convex compact polytope of Markovian occupancy measures, converting the ratio into a min-max over state-action visitation probabilities. For the worst-case environment, a tree-like MDP with a 'delay' action at the root achieves near-tight bounds: the no-lookahead agent can only collect one leaf reward, while the L-lookahead agent waits at the root and enters the tree only when a reward is predicted at a reachable leaf, gaining a factor of (H−L+1) attempts. The resulting CR for full lookahead is the inverse of the coverability coefficient, and for one-step lookahead it is a weaker 'pre-known distribution coverability' notion, establishing a spectrum of concentrability measures across the lookahead range.

## Results

The paper establishes tight competitive ratio (CR) bounds for the full spectrum of lookahead levels L ∈ [H]. For the worst-case environment, CR^L ≥ max{1/(SAH), 1/((H-L+1)A^L)}, with matching upper bounds achieved by tree-like MDPs with a delay action at the root. For one-step lookahead specifically, CR^1 = Θ(1/(HA)), independent of state-space size. The worst-case CR for full lookahead, CR^H(P), is shown to equal the inverse of the coverability coefficient from offline RL and reward-free exploration, linking this seemingly distinct problem to fundamental complexity measures in those areas.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are to known quantities in the RL literature. The full-lookahead CR^H(P) is shown to be exactly the inverse of the coverability coefficient studied by Xie et al. (2022) and Al-Marjani et al. (2023), revealing a surprising structural link between lookahead value and offline/reward-free RL complexity. For one-step lookahead, the CR^1(P) is identified as an intermediate coverability notion between single-policy coverability and the full coverability coefficient. In the chain MDP (prophet inequality) setting, the CR between one-step and full lookahead is the classical 1/2 bound (Hill and Kertz, 1981), while the CR between no-lookahead and one-step is Θ(1/(AH)), showing that the dominant information gain comes from even a single step of lookahead rather than from extending to full lookahead. For grid MDPs, the CR between one-step and full lookahead is O(1/H), which is counter-intuitive since both individual CRs are Θ(1/H); the paper explains this by noting the worst-case environments differ (reward sparsity vs. navigation difficulty).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the long-shot distribution framework of Hill and Kertz (1981) as its central technical tool, and positions itself in contrast to the prophet inequality literature (which measures CR between one-step and full lookahead) and to MPC/receding-horizon control (which uses lookahead for planning efficiency rather than quantifying its value). It also connects to Garg et al. (2013) on online MDP optimization with partial predictions, but departs by assuming known reward distributions for both agents and measuring CR between no-lookahead and L-lookahead for all L simultaneously. The most striking lineage connection is to coverability coefficients in offline RL (Xie et al., 2022) and reward-free exploration (Al-Marjani et al., 2023), which the paper shows are exactly the inverse of the worst-case CR for full lookahead.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first complete characterization of how much value different levels of reward lookahead add in tabular MDPs, covering the full spectrum from one-step to full lookahead. The surprising identification of the CR with the coverability coefficient opens a potential bridge between competitive analysis and learning complexity, though the paper itself does not develop this connection further. Key open questions include: whether analogous results hold for transition lookahead (the paper notes the CR can be exponentially small), how to design practical algorithms that achieve the lookahead value, and whether the refined coverability notion for one-step lookahead (covering all valid state distributions) yields meaningful guarantees in offline RL. The analysis is restricted to tabular, worst-case settings, so extending to function approximation or average-case analysis remains open.
