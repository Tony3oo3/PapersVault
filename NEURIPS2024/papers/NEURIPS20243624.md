## Problem

Standard deep neural networks trained end-to-end (single-grade deep learning, SGDL) exhibit spectral bias: they learn low-frequency components of a target function first and struggle to capture high-frequency features, even when the network has sufficient capacity. This limitation hinders DNNs in tasks requiring accurate representation of high-frequency information, such as image reconstruction, seismic wavefield modeling, and medical imaging. The paper asks whether a high-frequency function can be more effectively learned by decomposing it into compositions of lower-frequency functions, each captured by a shallow network.

## Input / Output

The method is demonstrated across four settings with varying input dimensions: 1D scalar functions on [0,1], 2D points on flower-shaped manifolds, 2D pixel coordinates mapped to 3D RGB values for color image regression, and 784-dimensional flattened MNIST digit vectors mapped to 10-dimensional one-hot class vectors. For the image experiments, the network input is the spatial coordinate of each pixel rather than the image patch itself, and the output is the corresponding RGB triplet. For MNIST, the target function is modified by adding a radial sinusoidal wave component (controlled by amplitude β and frequency κ) to the one-hot label, introducing high-frequency structure that the network must regress.

## Method

The paper employs the multi-grade deep learning (MGDL) model to implement a 'sum-composition' decomposition of a high-frequency target function into compositions of low-frequency shallow neural networks (SNNs). In grade 1, an SNN is trained on the raw data to capture the lowest-frequency component. In each subsequent grade l, a new SNN is trained on the residual from the previous grade, but its input is the composition of all previously learned (frozen) SNNs applied to the original input, so the new SNN only needs to learn a low-frequency correction on top of an increasingly rich feature representation. The final prediction is the sum of outputs from all grades, yielding a sum of nested compositions of SNNs. This mechanism is motivated by the Jacobi-Anger identity, which shows that composing two low-frequency functions (e.g., cos(a·sin(bx))) produces high-frequency harmonics, and is validated empirically by observing that each successive grade of MGDL captures progressively higher-frequency spectral components while each individual SNN remains low-frequency.

## Results

On 1D synthetic data with high-frequency components (frequencies up to 200), MGDL reduces test RSE by a factor of 592–7,058 compared to SGDL across four settings (e.g., TeRSE drops from 1.2×10⁻¹ to 1.7×10⁻⁵ in Setting 1, and from 7.7×10⁻¹ to 1.3×10⁻³ in the hardest Setting 4). On 2D colored images (Cat, Sea, Building), MGDL with 4 grades surpasses SGDL by 2.35–3.93 dB in test PSNR (e.g., 24.18 vs 21.83 dB for Cat). On 784-dimensional MNIST data with added high-frequency radial-wave noise (β=1), MGDL improves TeRSE by approximately 27–29% over SGDL while also exhibiting better generalization (lower validation loss without overfitting).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole experimental baseline is SGDL (single-grade deep learning), i.e., standard end-to-end training of a DNN with the same total depth and width as the MGDL configuration. MGDL wins decisively on every dataset: on 1D synthetic data it reduces TeRSE by 2–5 orders of magnitude while often using less training time; on 2D manifold data it achieves 10–100× lower TeRSE in roughly half the training time; on colored images it gains 2.35–3.93 dB in test PSNR; and on 784-dim MNIST it improves TeRSE by ~27–29% while avoiding the overfitting observed in SGDL (SGDL's validation loss increases after ~1,600 s at β=1, whereas MGDL's continues to decrease). The paper does not run direct head-to-head comparisons against other spectral-bias mitigations mentioned in the introduction (phase-shift DNN, adaptive activation, multiscale DNN, Fourier features), so the quantitative advantage is established only relative to vanilla end-to-end training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the multi-grade deep learning (MGDL) model introduced by Xu [43, 44], repurposing it as a mechanism to decompose high-frequency functions into sum-compositions of low-frequency shallow networks. It positions itself against a family of spectral bias mitigation strategies—including phase-shift DNNs [5], adaptive activation functions [18], multiscale DNNs [6, 23], and Fourier feature mappings [36]—by instead modifying the training procedure (grade-by-grade incremental learning) rather than the input representation or network architecture. The conceptual motivation draws on the Jacobi-Anger identity from classical analysis, which shows that composing two low-frequency functions can produce high-frequency content, providing a mathematical justification for the sum-composition decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Spectral bias is a fundamental limitation that restricts DNNs' applicability to problems involving high-frequency features (medical imaging, wave equations, seismic modeling), and this paper offers a practical, dimension-agnostic training strategy that requires no architectural changes. However, the paper explicitly acknowledges the absence of a theoretical foundation explaining why MGDL overcomes spectral bias, and the empirical validation is limited to four relatively small-scale experiments with no comparison against other spectral bias mitigation methods (Fourier features, phase shift, multiscale). Open questions include whether the approach extends to convolutional or residual architectures, whether it scales to larger datasets, and whether the grade-by-grade decomposition can be made adaptive or data-driven rather than fixed.
