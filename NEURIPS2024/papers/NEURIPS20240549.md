## Problem

Adapter-based continual imitation learning (CiL) methods isolate parameters per task to prevent catastrophic forgetting, but this isolation prevents knowledge sharing across demonstrations, limiting sample efficiency in non-stationary environments. The paper addresses how to incrementally learn shareable, retrievable skills from partial or incomplete demonstrations so that skills learned for one task can be reused to resolve missing sub-goals in another, without requiring comprehensive expert data or rehearsal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240549/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each timestep the method consumes a state tuple (o_t, g_t) consisting of a low-dimensional observation vector (60-dim for Franka Kitchen, 140-dim for Meta-World) and a language embedding of the current sub-goal, which are jointly encoded into a fixed state embedding via a frozen encoder function. The output is a short-horizon action sampled from a DDPM diffusion policy conditioned on the retrieved skill adapter. Because the state encoder is fixed (not learned), retrieval results remain stable across stages despite input distribution shifts in non-stationary settings.

## Method

IsCiL employs a two-level hierarchical policy: a skill retriever π_R that selects the appropriate skill via cosine similarity between the current state embedding and multifaceted skill prototypes (each prototype comprises ~20 KMeans-clustered basis vectors capturing the multi-modal state distribution of a skill), and a skill decoder π_D that is a DDPM diffusion model modulated by a dedicated 4-rank LoRA adapter corresponding to the retrieved skill. New skills are incrementally learned by initializing their adapter from the most frequently retrieved existing adapter (identified by average prototype similarity over the new sub-goal's data), then fine-tuning with an imitation loss; the new prototype is appended to the growing prototype pool. This design enables cross-task skill sharing (a skill learned for sub-goal g in task τ₁ can be retrieved to fill a missing g in task τ₂), supports task-wise selective adaptation by simply swapping goal sequences at inference, and permits efficient task unlearning by deleting the corresponding prototype-adapter pair.

## Results

IsCiL achieves the highest AUC across all six CiL scenarios (Evolving Kitchen and Evolving World × Complete/Semi/Incomplete), reaching 89.8% on Kitchen-Complete, 75.8% on Kitchen-Semi, 74.0% on Kitchen-Incomplete, 84.3% on World-Complete, 68.9% on World-Semi, and 71.2% on World-Incomplete. This corresponds to 84.5%–97.2% of the oracle multi-task baseline. Notably, IsCiL is the only non-oracle method that maintains positive BWT (backward transfer) across all scenarios, indicating that later stages improve rather than degrade earlier task performance. In the unseen-task adaptation setting (Table 2), IsCiL achieves 72.8% AUC-A on Kitchen and 45.3% AUC-A on World, substantially outperforming all baselines on adaptation-only metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IsCiL decisively outperforms all adapter-based baselines on AUC in every scenario. Against TAIL-τ (the strongest non-oracle competitor in Complete settings), IsCiL gains +3.6 AUC on Kitchen-Complete (89.8 vs 86.2) and +44.6 on Kitchen-Semi (75.8 vs 41.2), where TAIL-τ's isolated per-task adapters cannot share knowledge across stages. Against L2M/L2M-g, the gap is even larger: IsCiL achieves 89.8 vs 32.3 AUC on Kitchen-Complete and 84.3 vs 48.6 on World-Complete, as L2M struggles with inaccurate skill retrieval and knowledge overwriting. EWC (Kirkpatrick 2017) performs worst among all methods (AUC 10.4–17.1), as its Fisher-regularization severely hinders learning on new long-horizon tasks. In the unlearning scenario (Table 3), IsCiL achieves 72.7 AUC on Kitchen-Incomplete versus only 33.8 for TAIL-τ CLPU (Liu 2022), a 115% relative improvement, while maintaining near-identical performance to its non-unlearned version. Experience Replay (Chaudhry 2019) matches IsCiL's FWT with ~250 rehearsal samples but never surpasses its AUC, confirming that IsCiL's skill-based knowledge sharing is more sample-efficient than explicit data replay.

## Relation to Prior Work

IsCiL sits at the intersection of adapter-based continual learning (L2M, TAIL) and skill-based imitation learning, but departs from both in a key way: rather than isolating parameters per task (TAIL) or per state region (L2M), it learns shareable skill prototypes with multifaceted KMeans-based bases that enable cross-task knowledge transfer while retaining parameter isolation via LoRA adapters. The hierarchical skill retriever/decoder structure draws on skill-based RL and few-shot imitation, while the diffusion-model base policy and LoRA fine-tuning follow the parameter-efficient adaptation paradigm. Unlike rehearsal-based CiL (ER, progressive networks), IsCiL requires no stored past data and no stage identification at evaluation, positioning it as a rehearsal-free, stage-agnostic alternative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses three practical bottlenecks in deploying lifelong robot agents—data cost, non-stationary environments, and privacy—within a single architecture, and the positive backward transfer (future stages improving past task performance) is a notable property absent in most adapter-based baselines. However, the evaluation is confined to two low-dimensional simulation benchmarks (60-dim Franka-Kitchen, 140-dim Meta-World) with only qualitative LIBERO visualization, leaving open whether the prototype-based retrieval generalizes to high-dimensional sensory inputs (images, point clouds). The dependence on explicit sub-goal sequences and the need to tune adapter rank per environment are practical constraints that limit immediate deployment, and the computational overhead of per-step retrieval, while small in simulation, may scale differently in real-robot settings.
