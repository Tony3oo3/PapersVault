## Problem

While watermarking schemes for LLM-generated text have been proposed, two critical gaps remain: (1) existing detection methods lack rigorous Type I and Type II error guarantees under realistic settings where the next-token distribution varies across positions, and (2) the problem of segmenting a user-modified published text into watermarked and non-watermarked sub-strings has been largely unexplored. This paper addresses both by providing theoretical error control for randomization-based watermark detection and developing a change-point-detection framework for text segmentation.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a published text string (possibly modified by the user via substitutions, insertions, or deletions) and the corresponding watermark key sequence generated by the LLM provider. The text is partitioned into overlapping moving windows of fixed size B (e.g., B=20), and for each window a randomization-based p-value is computed by comparing the observed dependence statistic against T independently resampled key sequences. The output is a sequence of m p-values from which change point locations are identified, yielding a segmentation of the text into contiguous watermarked and non-watermarked sub-strings.

## Method

The approach combines randomization testing with change point detection. For detection, a test statistic (e.g., the inverse-transform correlation metric or the exponential-minimum log-likelihood metric) measures dependence between the watermark key and the text; a p-value is obtained by comparing the observed statistic to T permutations of the key, with theoretical guarantees showing Type I error ≤ α and power → 1 under mild conditions on the NTP distribution. For segmentation, the text is scanned with a sliding window of size B, producing a p-value sequence where non-watermarked segments yield approximately uniform p-values while watermarked segments yield p-values concentrated near zero. The segmentation problem is then cast as detecting distributional change points in this p-value sequence using a SeedBS-NOT algorithm (seeded binary segmentation with narrowest-over-threshold selection), with significance assessed via moving block bootstrap to account for the B-dependence structure in the p-values. The paper establishes a convergence rate of O_p(√(mB log(m/B)) / D) for the estimated change point location, where D is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance between the null and alternative p-value distributions.

## Results

Across three attack settings (insertion, substitution, and combined) on texts generated by GPT-2, OPT-1.3B, and Llama-3-8B using prompts from the C4 dataset, the exponential minimum sampling (EMS) method achieves the highest Rand index for change point detection, with its best performance at threshold ζ=0.005. In the most complex Setting 4 (four change points), EMS and EMSL detect change points closer to the true locations (at indices 101, 201, 301, 401) compared to the inverse transform methods (ITS, ITSL). For the hyperparameter study with Llama-3-8B and T=999, the optimal window size B=30 yields a Rand index of 0.9641, while B=20 (used in main experiments) gives 0.9429. The Rand index remains stable (0.9326–0.9404) across T values from 99 to 999, with computation time scaling linearly from 3.41 to 24.94 hours.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four watermark detection and segmentation methods: ITS (inverse transform sampling with original metric), ITSL (inverse transform with Levenshtein cost), EMS (exponential minimum sampling with original metric), and EMSL (exponential minimum with Levenshtein cost). EMS consistently outperforms all other methods across all three LLMs (GPT-2, OPT-1.3B, Llama-3-8B) and all attack settings, achieving the highest Rand index in every configuration. EMSL is the second-best method, while the two inverse transform methods (ITS, ITSL) perform worst, with their p-value sequences showing more spurious high values that lead to false change point detections. In the false positive analysis (Setting 1, no true change points), the EMS methods produce fewer false discoveries than the ITS methods, confirming that the quality of the underlying p-value sequence drives segmentation accuracy. The Levenshtein-cost variants (ITSL, EMSL) improve upon their base counterparts but do not close the gap to the non-Levenshtein EMS method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate literatures: LLM watermarking (inverse transform sampling à la Kuditipudi et al., exponential minimum/Gumbel sampling à la Aaronson) and nonparametric change point detection (SeedBS, NOT). While prior watermark detection work (Huang et al. 2023, Li et al. 2024) focused on global detection with statistical efficiency guarantees, and change point methods (Kovács et al. 2022, Baranowski et al. 2019) assumed i.i.d. or weakly dependent observations, this paper is the first to formalize the segmentation of partially watermarked text as a change point problem on a B-dependent p-value sequence, providing Type I/II error control and a convergence rate for the estimated change point locations. The moving block bootstrap is repurposed to handle the specific B-dependence structure induced by the sliding-window p-value construction, departing from the independence assumptions in classical change point theory (Carlstein 1988).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that real-world published texts are often only partially watermarked (due to user edits, insertions, substitutions), yet prior detection methods could only answer a global yes/no question. By providing a principled segmentation procedure with statistical guarantees, this work enables detectors to pinpoint exactly which sub-strings are LLM-generated, which is critical for provenance tracking and academic integrity. Key limitations include the strong dependence on p-value quality (a weak watermark detector degrades segmentation), the assumption of a single watermark key sequence, and the restriction to specific watermarking schemes. Open directions flagged by the authors include adaptive watermark generation tied to NTP distributions and extending the framework to texts combining watermarks from multiple LLMs with different schemes.
