## Problem

Direct Alignment Algorithms (DAAs) like DPO, IPO, and SLiC bypass the explicit reward-modeling stage of classical RLHF, yet they still exhibit degradation from over-optimization that has never been formally characterized for this setting. Because DAAs do not train a separate proxy reward, the mechanism and scaling behavior of their over-optimization remain unclear, leaving practitioners without principled guidance on KL budgets or training duration.

## Input / Output

The method consumes preference triplets (prompt x, preferred response y_w, dispreferred response y_l) from datasets such as Reddit TL;DR (92K pairs) and Anthropic-HH, together with a frozen reference policy π_ref initialized via SFT. Training produces an aligned LLM policy π_θ whose implicit reward r_θ(x,y) = β log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) is never explicitly scored; instead, quality is assessed via GPT-4 win-rates over held-out prompts at temperature 1.0 with max length 512. The key tunable is the KL budget β, which trades off reward exploitation against deviation from the reference, and the paper sweeps seven β values across three Pythia scales (1B, 2.8B, 6.9B).

## Method

The paper unifies DPO, IPO, and SLiC under a single DAA objective L_DAA(π_θ; π_ref) = E[g(β log π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x) − β log π_θ(y_l|x)/π_ref(y_l|x))] where g is a convex loss (negative log-sigmoid, quadratic, or hinge), then empirically demonstrates that all three objectives exhibit hump-shaped win-rate curves as a function of achieved KL divergence, with peak performance often reached before 25% of a single epoch. It shows that the classical RLHF scaling law R(d) = d(α − β log d) with d = √D_KL(π‖π_ref) accurately fits DAA win-rates (halving RMSE over a quadratic fit), and that smaller models or tighter KL budgets extrapolate more strongly along simple features like response length. Theoretically, the paper proves (Proposition 1, adapted from Hejna et al.) that the DAA loss is rank-deficient in the prompt-response space, yielding a non-trivial null space that permits infinitely many optimal policies, some of which concentrate probability mass on out-of-distribution responses; this is corroborated by a toy tree-MDP experiment showing DPO, IPO, and SLiC all shift mass onto unseen trajectories. An OOD-bootstrapping interpretation via the soft Q-function formulation further explains why smaller β values produce more optimistic value estimates and worse over-optimization.

## Results

Across DPO, IPO, and SLiC at three model scales (Pythia 1B, 2.8B, 6.9B) and seven KL budgets, all DAAs exhibit hump-shaped GPT-4 win-rate curves versus KL divergence, confirming over-optimization analogous to classical RLHF. Performance peaks after only ~25% of a single training epoch, after which win rates decline while KL divergence continues to increase. The Gao et al. scaling law R(d) = d(α − β log d) fits DAA win rates with roughly half the RMSE of a quadratic fit in KL. IPO consistently achieves lower KL values under the same β constraint and shows the least over-optimization, while the 1B model over-optimizes almost immediately and the 6.9B model with IPO exhibits little to no degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three DAA objectives—DPO [46], IPO [4], and SLiC-HF [68]—under identical training protocols (Pythia models, TL;DR dataset, GPT-4 win-rate evaluation). DPO and SLiC exhibit similar over-optimization dynamics and comparable win-rate–KL trade-offs across all model sizes. IPO, which uses a quadratic fixed-margin loss, is consistently less prone to over-optimization: it achieves lower KL values under the same β constraint and, at 6.9B scale, shows little to no performance degradation even at higher KL budgets. The length-regularization approach from Park et al. [44] shifts the KL–win-rate Pareto frontier but does not eliminate over-optimization and can even exacerbate it in the low-KL regime, where the regularized model underperforms standard DPO. Smaller models (1B) over-optimize more severely than larger ones (6.9B) across all three objectives, and weaker models extrapolate more strongly on the length feature (higher R² in the log-likelihood vs. length regression), suggesting capacity-limited models latch onto simpler spurious correlations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward over-optimization analysis established for classical RLHF by Gao et al. [21] to Direct Alignment Algorithms (DPO, IPO, SLiC), which bypass the explicit reward model entirely. The theoretical mechanism is drawn from Hejna et al. [23], whose rank-deficiency and null-space analysis of preference learning objectives is adapted here to show that DAA losses are non-strictly convex and admit multiple global optima that can concentrate probability mass on out-of-distribution responses. The OOD bootstrapping interpretation in Appendix D builds on the Q-function reformulation of Rafailov et al. [47], connecting DAA training dynamics to the classical offline RL problem of querying value estimates on unseen states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since DAAs (especially DPO) have become the dominant practical approach for LLM alignment, demonstrating that they exhibit the same hump-shaped over-optimization as RLHF—and that this can occur within a single epoch—provides a critical warning for practitioners tuning KL budgets and training duration. The rank-deficiency explanation offers a mechanistic account of *why* DAAs over-optimize (under-constrained optimization with multiple optima placing mass on OOD responses), which is distinct from the OOD reward-querying mechanism in RLHF and points toward potential fixes. Key limitations include evaluation only up to 6.9B parameters, a primary reliance on the TL;DR summarization task, and no multi-seed runs, leaving open whether the scaling-law fit and within-epoch degradation patterns persist at frontier model scales and across more diverse alignment tasks.
