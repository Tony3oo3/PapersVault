## Problem

Identifying the synaptic plasticity rules that govern learning in biological circuits remains an open challenge because synaptic weight dynamics cannot be directly recorded at scale. Prior statistical approaches, such as the logistic-regression-based method of Rajagopalan et al. (2023), are restricted to plasticity rules that depend only on presynaptic activity and reward, and cannot capture recurrent dependencies on current synaptic weights or postsynaptic activity. This paper addresses that gap by providing a general gradient-based framework that can infer arbitrary parameterized plasticity rules, including weight-dependent homeostatic terms, from either neural or behavioral trajectories.

## Input / Output

The method consumes either (a) neural firing-rate trajectories from a recorded circuit, or (b) binary behavioral choices (accept/reject) paired with input stimuli and probabilistic reward signals. Crucially, for behavioral data the training signal contains no direct information about synaptic weights or hidden-layer activity—only the sequence of binary decisions. The output is the parameter vector θ of the plasticity function g_θ (e.g., Taylor coefficients or MLP weights), which fully specifies how each synapse's weight changes as a function of presynaptic activity, postsynaptic activity, current weight, and reward. A fixed readout function f maps the model's internal neural activity to the observable space (a subset of neurons for neural data, or a choice probability for behavioral data).

## Method

The core mechanism is to embed a parameterized plasticity rule g_θ(x_j, y_i, w_ij, r) into a feedforward network whose weights are updated at every timestep, then optimize θ via Adam with backpropagation through time over full length-T trajectories to minimize a trajectory-level loss (MSE for neural data, binary cross-entropy for behavioral data). The plasticity function is parameterized either as a truncated second-order Taylor series in the four biological variables (yielding 81 interpretable coefficients that map directly to candidate terms like x_j·r or w_ij) or as a small multilayer perceptron for greater functional flexibility. Because optimization is performed over entire trajectories rather than before/after weight snapshots, the method can recover rules with long nonlinear temporal dependencies—most importantly, weight-dependent terms that produce active forgetting. The approach is validated by recovering Oja's rule from synthetic neural data and a covariance-based reward rule from simulated fly behavior, then applied to 18 Drosophila two-alternative-choice trajectories where it identifies a statistically significant negative weight-decay coefficient (θ_001 < 0), revealing an active forgetting mechanism absent from prior models.

## Results

On simulated neural activity, the method recovers Oja's rule with θ₁₁₀→1 and θ₀₂₁→−1, maintaining high R² on weight trajectories even at 50% recording sparsity. On simulated behavior, the MLP parameterization achieves R²(weights)=0.85, R²(activity)=0.96, and 64.76% deviance explained for the ground-truth rule Δw=xⱼr. On Drosophila two-alternative choice data (n=18 flies), adding a weight-dependent term (wᵢⱼ) to the plasticity rule significantly improves behavioral fit over the model without it (Wilcoxon signed-rank p=5×10⁻⁵), and the inferred coefficient is negative, indicating active synaptic weight decay (forgetting). Incorporating reward expectation on top of the weight-dependent term further improves fit (p=0.067).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the logistic-regression-based approach of Rajagopalan et al. (2023), which assumes the plasticity rule depends only on presynaptic activity and reward (no weight or postsynaptic dependence). On the Drosophila dataset, the proposed method without a wᵢⱼ term matches the performance reported by Rajagopalan et al., confirming equivalence in the restricted setting. However, when the weight-dependent term is included, the proposed method achieves a significantly better fit to fly behavior (Wilcoxon signed-rank p=5×10⁻⁵), a comparison that Rajagopalan et al.'s logistic regression framework cannot make because it cannot represent recurrent weight dependencies. The negative sign of the inferred wᵢⱼ coefficient reveals an active forgetting mechanism that the baseline method is structurally unable to capture. In simulation, the MLP parameterization consistently outperforms the Taylor series on R²(weights) (e.g., 0.85 vs. 0.78 for the xⱼr rule), while both achieve comparable behavioral fit (% deviance 64.76 vs. 61.91).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on inferring synaptic plasticity rules from data, building on the Taylor series parameterization introduced by Confavreux et al. (2020) for meta-learning plasticity rules. It departs from Rajagopalan et al. (2023), who used logistic regression to infer learning rules in Drosophila but could only capture rules depending on presynaptic activity and reward, by employing backpropagation through time over full trajectories to accommodate arbitrary dependencies including current synaptic weight and postsynaptic activity. Unlike GAN-based approaches (Ramesh et al., 2023) or Bayesian frameworks (Ashwood et al., 2020), the method uses a direct gradient-based loss over observed trajectories, and unlike meta-learning approaches that require specifying a target task, it infers rules purely from recorded data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key biological finding—that Drosophila mushroom body plasticity includes a negative weight-dependent (homeostatic decay) term producing active forgetting—was inaccessible to prior inference methods and provides a mechanistic account of forgetting that aligns with behavioral timescales. The method's generality (handling both neural and behavioral data, arbitrary parameterized rules) positions it as a reusable tool for system identification in neuroscience, though it currently requires known circuit architecture and cannot capture metaplasticity or higher-order temporal dynamics. Open questions include whether the 'sloppiness' in the solution space (multiple rules producing similar dynamics) resolves with more data, and whether the approach scales to mammalian-scale connectomes with unknown initial weights.
