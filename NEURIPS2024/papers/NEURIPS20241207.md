## Problem

Data Point Selection (DPS) aims to filter or re-weight large, noisy, or imbalanced training sets using a small in-domain meta set, but the dominant bi-level optimization (BLO) formulation is computationally and memory expensive, relies on approximate Hessian or reverse-mode differentiation heuristics, and lacks convergence guarantees when applied with minibatches. This limits BLO-based DPS to small models and datasets, making it impractical for the most important use case: curating web-scale data for billion-parameter models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a large out-of-domain training set D_t (which may be noisy, imbalanced, or uncurated) and a small in-domain meta set D_m whose distribution matches the target test task. It produces instance-wise soft weights w ∈ R_+^{N_t} that determine how much each training point contributes to the loss, along with the trained backbone parameters θ. The weights can be individual per-sample scalars or generated by a lightweight weight network that maps data-point embeddings to [0,1], enabling transfer to new unseen samples without retraining. The framework handles both classification losses (cross-entropy on images) and autoregressive generative losses (next-token NLL on text/LLMs).

## Method

BADS reframes DPS as posterior inference in a Bayesian model where the backbone parameters θ and instance weights w are jointly random variables. The prior on θ is a weighted-data-driven distribution (proportional to exp(−Σ w_i · l(z_i^t; θ)) times a base Gaussian), and the likelihood is defined by the meta-set loss, so the joint posterior p(θ, w | D_t, D_m) encodes both 'fit the meta set' and 'select informative training data.' Posterior sampling is performed via Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD), yielding update equations that are standard gradient descent on the log-posterior plus additive Gaussian noise—comparable in per-step cost to plain SGD and avoiding Hessian computations or multi-step unrolling. A sparsity-inducing Gaussian prior on the weight sum (centered at ⌊N_t·β⌋) controls the effective selection ratio, and a running-average trick keeps the weight-sum constraint computable on minibatches without loading all N_t weights in memory. The weight update naturally performs loss alignment: once the backbone reaches a good regime on the meta set, training points with low loss under that backbone receive higher weights, effectively selecting data whose loss landscape is consistent with the in-domain distribution.

## Results

On the LLM instruction fine-tuning task (OpenLLaMA 3B), BADS achieves the best MMLU score of 26.59, outperforming the next-best baselines (Random_Select and CDS at 25.62) by nearly 1 point, and leads on ARCe (67.00) and HellaSwag (52.91) as well. On CIFAR-10 with 80% symmetric label noise, BADS exceeds BLO by 15% and non-DPS approaches by 20% in test accuracy. On WebNLG (efficient learning), BADS achieves a 2 BLEU advantage over the second-best method (Duplicate_Meta) and surpasses all other DPS baselines by more than 5 BLEU. On MNIST (data balancing with 4,975 vs. 25 class imbalance), BADS outperforms BLO by a noticeable margin while both exceed 90% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BADS consistently outperforms the BLO baselines (Ren et al. [41] and Grangier et al. [19]) across all three proof-of-concept scenarios: on MNIST it leads by a noticeable accuracy margin, on CIFAR-10 with 80% noise it exceeds BLO by 15% in test accuracy, and on WebNLG it surpasses BLO by over 10 BLEU in the controlled domain-adaptation setup. Against CDS (Wang et al. [49]), BADS wins in every scenario; CDS underperforms even non-DPS baselines on MNIST and overfits on CIFAR after 15,000 steps, while on WebNLG it incorrectly weights the less relevant domain higher. ClassAct (Evans et al. [13]) is outperformed by BADS on all tasks, with ClassAct showing particularly poor behavior on CIFAR where it assigns higher weights to noisy examples in later training phases. On the LLM IFT task, BADS leads on MMLU (26.59 vs. 25.62 for the best non-BADS method) and ARCe (67.00 vs. 67.34 for ClassAct, a near-tie), but AskLLM-O (Sachdeva et al. [42]) edges out BADS on ARC-challenge (35.15 vs. 34.39) and HellaSwag (53.71 vs. 52.91), though AskLLM-O requires ~4 hours of additional LLM inference time per run and 3.5× more GPU memory. In terms of efficiency, BADS matches non-DPS training time (61s vs. 61s per 100 steps on WebNLG) while BLO takes 113s and ClassAct takes 270s.

## Relation to Prior Work

BADS departs from the dominant bi-level optimization (BLO) formulation for data point selection (Ren et al., Grangier et al., Shu et al.) by recasting the problem as joint posterior inference over network parameters and instance-wise weights, sampled via Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (Welling & Teh). The convergence guarantee is adapted from Zou et al.'s non-log-concave SGLD analysis. Conceptually, the paper also connects to gradient-alignment methods (Xia et al., Killamsetty et al.) and domain-adaptive transfer learning, but unifies them under a single Bayesian generative model where the weighted train data acts as a prior on the backbone and the meta data provides the likelihood.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a simple gradient-plus-noise update (comparable in cost to vanilla SGD) can replace expensive Hessian-based or reverse-mode BLO solvers while maintaining convergence guarantees, and that this scales to 3B-parameter LLMs for automated instruction fine-tuning data curation — a setting where no BLO method has been shown to be feasible. Key open questions include principled hyperparameter selection (the sparsity level β and impact constants ρ require manual tuning), reducing the GPU memory overhead relative to non-DPS training, and whether the approach extends to larger models or more diverse IFT data compositions. The framework's ability to handle balancing, denoising, and domain adaptation within one model is a useful unification, though each scenario still demands scenario-specific hyperparameter choices.
