## Problem

Steering vectors (SVs) have shown promise for adjusting language model behaviour at inference time, but prior work has evaluated them only in-distribution and at the aggregate level, leaving their per-sample reliability and out-of-distribution generalisation properties unknown. This paper rigorously investigates both: how consistently SVs produce the intended behavioural change across individual inputs, and how well they transfer when system or user prompts change.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244416/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on multiple-choice contrastive prompts where the model must select between two labelled options (A/B or Yes/No). A steering vector is extracted from the residual stream at a fixed layer (13 for Llama, 21 for Qwen) at the option-token position, then applied by adding λ·v to activations during inference. Effectiveness is measured via logit-difference propensity (logit of positive option minus logit of negative option) across a sweep of multipliers λ ∈ {−1.5, −1.0, −0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5}, summarised as a steerability score (slope of a least-squares fit).

## Method

The paper uses Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) to extract steering vectors as the mean difference between positive and negative option activations, then systematically evaluates their reliability and generalisation across 40 datasets (from MWE, TruthfulQA, and sycophancy) on two instruction-tuned models (Llama-2-7b-Chat and Qwen-1.5-14b-Chat). For in-distribution reliability, the authors introduce a per-sample steerability metric and discover a novel 'steerability bias'—a dataset-dependent preference for steering towards specific token positions (A vs. B, Yes vs. No) that explains a large fraction of per-sample variance and causes nearly 50% of inputs to be anti-steerable on several datasets. For out-of-distribution generalisation, they construct systematic distribution shifts by injecting persona instructions into system or user messages, and show that SV generalisation is primarily a dataset-level property (highly correlated across models) and is predicted by the similarity of unsteered model propensity between source and target prompt settings.

## Results

Across 40 datasets (MWE, TruthfulQA, Sycophancy) evaluated on Llama-2-7b-Chat and Qwen-1.5-14b-Chat, steerability exhibits high per-sample variance, with several datasets showing ~50% of inputs being anti-steerable (steered in the opposite direction). In-distribution and out-of-distribution steerability are strongly correlated (Spearman ρ=0.891 for Llama, ρ=0.694 for Qwen), but OOD steerability is on average lower than ID. Steerability is primarily a dataset-level property, with cross-model correlation of ρ=0.769 (ID) and ρ=0.586 (OOD) between Llama and Qwen despite different architectures and sizes. Propensity similarity between source and target prompt settings weakly predicts SV generalisation (ρ=−0.26 for Llama, ρ=−0.46 for Qwen), meaning SVs transfer less well when the model's unsteered behaviour differs across settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no traditional baseline methods to beat or lose to. The key comparative analyses are: (1) cross-model comparison showing steerability is highly correlated between Llama-2-7b-Chat and Qwen-1.5-14b-Chat (ρ=0.769 ID, ρ=0.586 OOD), indicating SV effectiveness is a dataset property rather than a model property; (2) ID vs. OOD comparison showing steerability degrades out-of-distribution, with the degradation more pronounced for Qwen (weaker ID-OOD correlation of ρ=0.694 vs. 0.891 for Llama); and (3) a comparison of steerability variance across models (ρ=0.465 ID, ρ=0.491 OOD), confirming that the high per-sample variance and anti-steerability are also partially dataset-driven. The paper builds directly on the CAA method of Rimsky et al. [30] and the activation-addition framework of Turner et al. [33], extending their in-distribution, aggregate-level evaluation to per-sample reliability and systematic OOD generalisation testing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) framework of Rimsky et al. [30] and the broader steering vectors / activation engineering line (Turner et al. [33], Zou et al. [43], Liu et al. [18]) by shifting the evaluation lens from aggregate in-distribution performance to per-sample reliability and systematic out-of-distribution generalization. While prior work demonstrated that CAA can shift model behaviour on a handful of alignment-relevant traits, this paper scales the evaluation to 40 MWE datasets and two architecturally distinct models (Llama-2-7b-Chat, Qwen-1.5-14b-Chat), and introduces the logit-difference propensity metric and a steerability slope metric to quantify per-example effects. It also connects the empirical findings to the Linear Representation Hypothesis [25], arguing that OOD generalisation of steering vectors constitutes a stronger test of global linearity than the in-distribution validations reported in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that steering vectors, a technique widely adopted for inference-time alignment, can produce the opposite of the intended behaviour on nearly half of inputs for several concepts, and that a previously unrecognised 'steerability bias' (dependence on which token or position encodes the positive option) explains a large share of this variance. These findings caution against deploying SVs as a reliable alignment mechanism without per-sample validation, and they identify concrete open problems: disentangling spurious steerability biases from genuine concept directions, understanding why steerability is largely a dataset-level rather than model-level property, and improving OOD generalisation when the target behaviour differs from the model's default propensity. The work also provides a reusable evaluation protocol (propensity curves, steerability slopes, relative steerability) that future SV research can adopt to report error bars and generalisation behaviour.
