## Problem

Despite growing empirical evidence that LLMs can improve their outputs through self-correction, no theoretical explanation exists for how this capability emerges from the transformer architecture. Prior in-context learning (ICL) theories are confined to linear regression with linear attention, which cannot capture the ranking and preference-comparison operations central to alignment. This paper asks whether realistic transformers—with softmax attention, multi-head attention, and feed-forward networks—can provably perform gradient descent on alignment objectives in-context.

## Input / Output

The theoretical setup takes N in-context triplets (x, y_i, r_i) where x is a shared query vector, y_i are response vectors (potentially noisy), and r_i are scalar reward scores indicating response quality; the model outputs a refined response y_N with lower squared error (higher reward). In the practical CaC strategy, the input is a text query followed by an initial generated response and a self-generated critic (verbal or binary), and the output is a single refined text response. The reward quality is not assumed perfect—noisy critics are explicitly modeled, and the theory shows performance degrades gracefully with critic noise.

## Method

The paper proves that a transformer with N−1 stacked blocks (each containing 3-head softmax attention + FFN) can implement one step of gradient descent on the N-ary Plackett-Luce alignment loss in-context, with the special case N=2 (Bradley-Terry) requiring only a single 2-head attention layer. The construction assigns distinct roles to each architectural component: softmax attention selects the top-rewarded token for ranking, multi-head attention discriminates tokens (one head pushes the top response away, another pulls others toward the weighted mean), the FFN with ReLU performs conditional masking of already-processed tokens, and stacking blocks enables sequential ranking of N examples. The practical instantiation, Checking-as-Context (CaC), uses a simple generate→critic→regenerate loop at inference time without parameter updates, and is validated on social bias (BBQ) and jailbreak defense (AdvBench) tasks, where self-correction reduces attack success rates from 95% to 1–2%.

## Results

On AdvBench jailbreak defense, CaC (Checking-as-Context) reduces the attack success rate (ASR) on Vicuna-7b from 95% to 1% (GCG-individual), 90% to 0% (GCG-transfer), and 91% to 29% (AutoDAN), while achieving 0% ASR across all three attack types on Llama2-7b-chat. On the BBQ social bias benchmark, CaC improves alignment scores on 8 out of 9 social dimensions for both Vicuna-7b and Llama2-7b-chat, with a statistically significant positive correlation (p<0.05) between self-checking accuracy and correction gain. Synthetic experiments confirm that a 20-layer GPT-2 with 3-head softmax attention matches or slightly exceeds gradient descent on the Plackett-Luce alignment objective with N=14 in-context examples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Self-reminder [80], CaC achieves a dramatic improvement on Vicuna-7b, reducing GCG-individual ASR from 94% to 1% and GCG-transfer from 59% to 0%, while also substantially outperforming on AutoDAN (29% vs. 88%). Against RAIN [40], CaC similarly dominates on Vicuna (1% vs. 72% GCG-id; 0% vs. 55% GCG-tr), though RAIN was not evaluated on AutoDAN. Against ICD [78], CaC wins on GCG-individual (1% vs. 4%) and GCG-transfer (0% vs. 17%) for Vicuna, but ICD retains a large advantage on AutoDAN (86% vs. 29%), which the authors attribute to AutoDAN's 'ignore all previous instructions' prompt invalidating the in-context self-checking history. On Llama2-7b-chat, all methods including CaC achieve 0% ASR, indicating the model's stronger inherent alignment makes the defense less critical. The paper also notes that CaC-self-instruct (the variant reported in Table 1) specifically addresses the AutoDAN vulnerability by instructing the model to refine its answer without repeating the original query.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL-as-optimization line of work (Von Oswald et al., ICML 2023; Ahn et al., 2023) from linear regression to ranking-based alignment objectives (Bradley-Terry, Plackett-Luce), showing that a realistic multi-layer transformer with softmax attention, multi-head structure, FFN, and stacked blocks can implement gradient descent of these non-convex objectives in-context. The key departure from prior ICL theory is that linear regression only requires single-head linear attention, whereas alignment requires all the 'realistic' transformer components, making the theory much more aligned with actual architectures. On the empirical side, the paper connects to the self-correction literature (Madaan et al., Shinn et al., Ganguli et al.) and alignment literature (Ouyang et al., Rafailov et al., Song et al.), providing a unified theoretical lens that explains why critic quality governs self-correction performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical result establishing that transformers can optimize alignment objectives in-context, providing a principled explanation for why and when self-correction works. The identification of distinct roles for softmax attention (ranking), multi-head attention (token discrimination), FFN (conditional token editing), and depth (multi-example comparison) offers concrete architectural insights for LLM design. Open questions include whether the N-1 layer requirement for N examples is tight, whether the existence proofs can be strengthened to convergence guarantees, and whether the theory extends beyond the simplified linear-regression-with-rewards setup to full language tasks. The CaC method, while simple, demonstrates that intrinsic self-correction can be practically effective for safety-critical applications without external feedback.
