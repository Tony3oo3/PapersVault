## Problem

Existing LM-based table understanding methods require feeding entire tables into prompts, making them infeasible for tables with millions of cells due to context-length limits, the lost-in-the-middle effect, and prohibitive compute costs. Prior retrieval approaches (row-column retrieval) still require encoding all rows and columns, which is intractable at scale. No framework existed for efficient, scalable LM-based table QA on real-world tables with hundreds of thousands to millions of cells.

## Input / Output

The method takes a large-scale table (up to 10 million cells, with tens to hundreds of columns and tens of thousands of rows) and a natural language question as input. Rather than passing the table directly to the LM, TableRAG constructs a compact prompt containing only retrieved column schemas (names, data types, example values) and relevant cell values, which is then fed to a ReAct-based program-aided solver that writes and executes code to query the full table. The output is a single answer evaluated by exact match, with the solver having programmatic access to the original table for computation.

## Method

TableRAG is a retrieval-augmented generation framework that decouples table encoding from LM reasoning through three stages: (1) Tabular Query Expansion, where the LM generates separate queries targeting column names and cell values; (2) Schema Retrieval, which encodes only column names via a dense encoder and retrieves top-K relevant columns with their data types and example values; and (3) Cell Retrieval, which encodes distinct column-value pairs (capped by a frequency-based budget B) and retrieves top-K relevant cells. The key insight is that encoding individual cells rather than entire rows/columns reduces encoding cost to O(min(D,B)) where D is the number of distinct values, while reasoning cost is O(K) and independent of table size. A ReAct-based program-aided solver then uses the retrieved schema and cell information to write and execute code against the full table, enabling precise computation without the LM needing to process raw table data.

## Results

On the million-token ArcadeQA and BirdQA benchmarks, TableRAG achieves the highest exact-match accuracy across all three LMs tested. With GPT-3.5-turbo, TableRAG scores 49.2% on ArcadeQA and 45.5% on BirdQA, outperforming the next-best baseline (ReadSchema) by 6.1 and 5.2 points respectively. With Gemini-1.0-Pro, the gap widens substantially: TableRAG reaches 42.3% and 44.2% versus 30.8% and 31.8% for ReadSchema. On the small-scale WikiTableQA benchmark, TableRAG also sets a new state-of-the-art at 57.03% exact-match accuracy, surpassing Binder (56.74%) and Dater (52.81%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RowColRetrieval (the TAP4LM [Sui et al.] approach), TableRAG wins on every LM–dataset combination: e.g., with GPT-3.5-turbo it gains +11.5 points on ArcadeQA (49.2 vs. 37.7) and +5.9 on BirdQA (45.5 vs. 39.6), and with Mistral-Nemo it gains +16.2 on ArcadeQA (46.2 vs. 30.0). On the small-scale WikiTableQA benchmark, TableRAG (57.03%) narrowly beats Binder [Cheng et al.] (56.74%) by 0.29 points, and more decisively outperforms Dater [Ye et al.] (52.81%) by 4.22 points, Text-to-SQL [Rajkumar et al.] (52.90%) by 4.13 points, and TaBERT [Yin et al.] (52.30%) by 4.73 points. Notably, ReadTable (feeding the full table) collapses to 4.6–9.1% on the million-token benchmarks due to context-length overflow, while ReadSchema and RandRowSampling remain competitive on smaller tables but fall 5–12 points behind TableRAG on the large-scale settings, confirming that targeted schema-cell retrieval is essential at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

TableRAG extends the RAG paradigm to tabular data by decomposing the retrieval problem into two granularities: schema-level (column names, types, example values) and cell-level (distinct column-value pairs), departing from prior row-column retrieval methods (ITR, TAP4LM) that encode entire rows and columns at O(NM) cost. The program-aided solver builds directly on ReAct, while the query expansion mechanism draws from Query2Doc-style LLM-generated sub-queries. The key conceptual departure is encoding individual cells rather than full rows/columns, reducing encoding complexity to O(D) where D is the number of distinct values, and introducing a frequency-aware budget that caps encoding at the B most common pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to make million-token table understanding tractable for LMs, and the two new real-world benchmarks (ArcadeQA, BirdQA) fill a critical evaluation gap—prior TableQA datasets contain only small tables within standard context windows. The finding that ReadSchema substantially outperforms ReadTable on large tables (43.1 vs 4.6 on ArcadeQA) provides strong empirical evidence that context-length degradation, not information scarcity, is the primary bottleneck. Open questions include handling tables where all cell values are distinct (worst-case O(NM) encoding), extending beyond QA/verification to broader table tasks, and whether the frequency-based truncation loses critical rare-value information in adversarial or long-tail settings.
