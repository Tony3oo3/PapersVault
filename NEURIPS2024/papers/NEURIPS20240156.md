## Problem

Fine-tuning large language models for alignment is resource-intensive, and existing test-time search methods either reduce autoregressive decoding to a bandit problem (limiting steerability) or require value functions trained from scratch (as costly as fine-tuning the large model itself). This paper asks whether a large model can be aligned at test time using only off-the-shelf small tuned/untuned model pairs as steering signals, without any additional training of the large model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240156/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt plus three models: a frozen large language model (π_base) to steer, a small tuned model (π*), and a small untuned model (π_ref), and produces a single aligned text response. The small and large models need not share vocabulary, and the large model can be black-box (requiring only sampling access via API), which makes the method applicable to closed-source models like gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct. For black-box models, a longer chunk length (e.g., L=100) is used to amortize the cost of repeated context embedding since stateless APIs do not retain activation caches.

## Method

The core theoretical insight is that the log-probability difference between small tuned and untuned models, log π*(y|x) − log π_ref(y|x), simultaneously serves as a dense per-token reward function and a value function under the token-level MDP formulation of alignment, converting the otherwise sparse sequence-level preference reward into a per-token dense signal whose cumulative sum approximates long-term value. The practical algorithm, Chunk-level Beam Search (CBS), maintains W hypotheses and at each step samples K continuation chunks of length L from the frozen large model, then prunes to the top-W successors by partial return (log π* − log π_ref). The finite chunk length enables mid-generation pruning of bad states (unlike Best-of-N with infinite chunk length), while sampling from π_base implicitly enforces the KL constraint without requiring token-level log-probability access or shared vocabulary. When the small models are weaker than the large model, the method instantiates weak-to-strong generalization, enhancing the strong model with only weak test-time guidance.

## Results

On AlpacaEval 2.0 (instruction following), weak-to-strong search with Zephyr guidance improves length-controlled win rates (LC WR) against gpt-4-turbo by 3.5–4.3 points across all large models: Llama-3-70B-Instruct goes from 34.42% to 37.92%, Llama-3-8B-Instruct from 22.92% to 27.17%, Llama-2-70b-chat from 16.18% to 19.10%, and black-box gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct from 16.00% to 20.07%. The guiding small model (zephyr-7b-beta) itself achieves only 13.20% LC WR, yet it consistently enhances much stronger models. On controlled-sentiment generation and summarization (using 124M gpt2 as guidance), weak-to-strong search with fixed hyperparameters (W,K,L=4,4,5) outperforms BoN(16) and matches or exceeds EFT across gpt2-large, gpt2-xl, Llama-2-7b, and Llama-3-8B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Weak-to-strong search (CBS) consistently outperforms BoN (Best-of-N Sampling) [16] across all models and both guidance settings: e.g., 37.92% vs. 36.60% LC WR for Llama-3-70B-Instruct and 20.07% vs. 19.59% for gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct under Zephyr guidance. Against Emulated Fine-Tuning (EFT) [34, 12, 11, 35], weak-to-strong search wins on all same-vocabulary models (e.g., 13.65% vs. 9.05% for Llama-2-7b-chat) and is the only applicable method for cross-vocabulary and black-box models where EFT is inapplicable. Vanilla beam search without external guidance [20] (Table 2) shows negligible or no improvement over regular decoding (e.g., 34.42% vs. 34.91% for Llama-3-70B-Instruct), confirming that the small-model guidance signal is essential. A key distinction is that EFT and BoN mainly interpolate between the weak and strong model distributions, while weak-to-strong search achieves genuine weak-to-strong generalization—pushing strong models beyond their base performance using only weak test-time signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Rafailov et al.'s token-level MDP formulation for alignment [20] and the DPO duality between language models and reward functions [4], extending them into a practical test-time search algorithm. It departs from prior search-based alignment methods in two key ways: unlike BoN sampling [16,17] which treats decoding as a bandit problem, and unlike MCTS-based approaches [13,18] that require a value function trained from scratch, it uses the log-probability difference between off-the-shelf small tuned/untuned model pairs as both dense reward and value. It also differs from emulated fine-tuning (EFT) [34,12,11,35] by operating at the chunk level via beam search rather than modifying token-level distributions, thereby removing the shared-vocabulary requirement and enabling application to black-box models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practical, training-free path to further aligning large (including black-box) LLMs by reusing small model pairs as steering signals, which is valuable given the cost of fine-tuning frontier models. The consistent weak-to-strong generalization—where ~10%-win-rate small models push 34%-win-rate large models higher—suggests that test-time search can extract alignment signal that interpolation-based methods (BoN, EFT) cannot. Open questions include failure modes of the approach, whether it extends to reasoning and coding tasks with ground-truth answers, whether pre-trained (non-SFTed) models work as the untuned reference, and how the dense reward parametrization might benefit RL fine-tuning (preliminarily explored in Appendix C.3).
