## Problem

Previous work on knowledge storage in Transformers has largely treated knowledge as localized in isolated components—MLPs as key-value memories or individual attention heads—following a restorative-theory paradigm that identifies where knowledge lives but ignores how components cooperate to express it. This paper argues that such component-level localization is an oversimplification, since different types of knowledge often occupy the same regions, and instead asks: what is the collaborative subgraph of attention heads, MLPs, and embeddings that a Transformer actually activates to recall and articulate a specific piece of factual knowledge?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained Transformer (GPT-2 medium/large or TinyLLaMA) and a factual prompt in subject-relation form (e.g., 'The official language of France is ___') as input, and produces a knowledge circuit: a sparse subgraph of the model's computation DAG containing the critical attention heads, MLPs, and edges whose joint activation is necessary to predict the target entity. Circuits are evaluated for completeness by running them standalone (all non-circuit edges zeroed) on held-out test splits using Hit@10, and their internal information flow is inspected by layer-normalizing each node's output and projecting through the unembedding matrix (logit lens) to track the target entity's rank and probability across layers.

## Method

The paper formalizes the Transformer as a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are input embeddings, per-layer attention heads A_{l,j}, per-layer MLPs M_l, and the output node, with edges encoding residual-stream dependencies. Circuit discovery proceeds via automated causal mediation (ACDC): edges are traversed in topological order, each is zero-ablated, and the resulting change in MatchNLL loss for the target token is compared against a threshold τ; edges below threshold are pruned iteratively until a minimal circuit C_k = ⟨N_k, E_k⟩ remains. Once identified, the circuit's internal dynamics are analyzed by mapping each node's layer-normalized output through the unembedding matrix, revealing that knowledge is aggregated in early-to-middle layers (where relation heads attend to the relation token and mover heads extract subject information to the final position) and then amplified in later layers. The discovered circuits are then used as an interpretive lens for three downstream analyses: (1) knowledge editing, where ROME is shown to inject edited information at the edited layer and rely on downstream mover heads to propagate it, while FT-M directly writes the target token into the edited MLP with dominant influence; (2) factual hallucination, where the circuit lacks an effective mover head or the mover selects the wrong candidate; and (3) in-context learning, where new induction-head-like attention heads appear in the circuit that attend to the demonstration context.

## Results

On GPT-2 Medium, the discovered knowledge circuits (using <10% of the full computation graph edges) maintain an average Hit@10 of 0.73 on the validation set versus 0.98 for the full model, and achieve 0.47 on the test set versus 0.44 for the original model — meaning the circuit actually *outperforms* the full model on unseen knowledge. Random circuits of the same size score 0.00 on the test set, confirming the discovered subgraphs are non-trivial. Notable test-set improvements include Country Language (0.16→0.75), Person Native Language (0.50→0.76), and Landmark-Country (0.16→0.36), suggesting the circuit strips out noise from irrelevant components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary validation is against random circuits of identical size: the discovered circuits achieve 0.47 average Hit@10 on the test set while random circuits score 0.00, confirming the ACDC-based discovery identifies genuinely critical edges. Compared to the full model, the circuit matches or exceeds original performance on 7 of 14 knowledge types in the test set (e.g., Country Language 0.75 vs. 0.16; Person Native Language 0.76 vs. 0.50), suggesting the full model's predictions are sometimes degraded by noise from non-circuit components. For knowledge editing, the paper does not frame ROME and FT-M as competitive baselines but uses the circuit lens to dissect their mechanisms: ROME writes the edited fact at the target MLP layer and relies on downstream mover heads to propagate it (rank of 'Intel' peaks ~3 layers after the edit), whereas FT-M directly saturates the edited component's output logits (>10 for the target token), which causes overfitting to unrelated queries (e.g., 'Windows server is created by?' still yields 'Intel'). In the in-context learning setting, ablating the newly emerged induction heads drops performance far more than ablating random heads (e.g., adj_comparative: 62.24→32.55 vs. 58.18), validating that the circuit-identified heads are causally responsible for ICL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the circuit discovery framework established by Conmy et al. (ACDC, NeurIPS 2023) and the residual-stream formalism of Elhage et al. (2021) from task-specific circuits (Indirect Object Identification, Color Object Identification) to knowledge-specific circuits for factual recall. It departs from the dominant 'knowledge neuron' paradigm (Geva et al., Dai et al., Meng et al.), which localizes knowledge in isolated MLP components, by instead analyzing the collaborative information flow between attention heads (mover heads, relation heads) and MLPs across layers. The paper also applies circuit analysis to knowledge editing methods (ROME, FT-M) and to interpreting hallucination and in-context learning, bridging mechanistic interpretability with practical model manipulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The knowledge circuit framework offers a unified lens for understanding why current editing methods produce side effects and fail on multi-hop reasoning: ROME injects information at a single layer while FT-M saturates the residual stream, and both can be traced through the circuit's mover heads. Open questions include whether findings generalize beyond GPT-2/TinyLLaMA to larger models, whether neuron-level granularity within MLPs would reveal finer structure, and why specific heads are activated for particular knowledge types. The computational cost of circuit discovery (1–2 days per knowledge type on A800) limits scalability, and the paper acknowledges that the logit-lens analysis may misrepresent early-layer behavior.
