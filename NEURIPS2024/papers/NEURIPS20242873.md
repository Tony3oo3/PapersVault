## Problem

Graph Neural Networks are the dominant approach for graph-structured data but operate as black boxes, requiring post-hoc explanations that lack correctness guarantees and may fail to expose hidden biases. No existing GNN architecture is interpretable by design in the sense of Generalized Additive Models, where the entire model logic can be visualized and understood directly from its learned components.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a graph G with N nodes, each carrying a d-dimensional feature vector, plus the graph's edge topology (used to compute shortest-path distances between all node pairs). It outputs a scalar or C-dimensional prediction per node or per graph (via sum-pooling), passed through a sigmoid, identity, or softmax activation. Uniquely, the entire model is fully specified by just d+1 univariate functions—one distance function ρ: [0,1]→R and d feature shape functions f_k: R→R—so the complete model logic is captured in a small set of plottable curves rather than millions of opaque weights.

## Method

GNAN extends Generalized Additive Models to graph data by computing each node's k-th representation entry as a weighted sum over all other nodes: the shape function f_k applied to the neighbor's k-th feature, scaled by a distance function ρ(1/(1+dist(j,i))) and normalized by the count of nodes at that distance. This single-shot aggregation over all nodes (rather than iterative message passing) ensures complete information flow while the ρ function modulates influence by graph distance. For graph-level predictions, node representations are sum-pooled before the final activation. The key architectural constraint is that no cross-products between features or between features and topology are allowed—each feature's contribution is independent—making the model a glass-box whose behavior is fully described by its d+1 univariate shape functions. These functions are implemented as small MLPs with ReLU activations trained end-to-end via backpropagation, and the paper demonstrates that this limited-capacity design matches or exceeds standard message-passing GNNs on 9 of 13 benchmark tasks.

## Results

GNAN achieves the best or second-best performance in 9 out of 13 evaluated tasks. On the three long-range molecular regression tasks (μ, α, α_HOMO from QM9), GNAN outperforms all baselines, achieving MAE of 2.55, 4.28, and 1.40 respectively. On node classification, GNAN leads on Tolokers (84.5% ROC-AUC) and ogb-arxiv (74.1% accuracy), while on graph classification it is second-best on Mutagenicity (72.2%) and PTC (64.9%). The paper highlights that GNAN's competitive accuracy is notable given its restricted additive architecture, which avoids iterative message-passing and cross-feature interactions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GraphConv, GNAN outperforms on 11 of 13 tasks, with the largest margins on the long-range regression tasks (e.g., μ: 2.55 vs. 2.91 MAE) and Cornell (85.7 vs. 65.9 accuracy). Against GraphSAGE, GNAN wins on 8 tasks including all three QM9 regression tasks and ogb-arxiv, but loses on PubMed (86.9 vs. 88.4) and PTC (64.9 vs. 67.1). Against GIN, GNAN is superior on 9 tasks, most notably the long-range tasks and Tolokers, but trails on Citeseer (75.8 vs. 77.1) and NCI1 (76.9 vs. 80.0). Against GATv2, GNAN wins on 7 tasks (all QM9, Tolokers, ogb-arxiv, Mutagen, PTC) but is behind on Cora (81.1 vs. 83.1) and NCI1 (76.9 vs. 80.4). Against GTransformer, GNAN dominates on the three long-range regression tasks (2.55 vs. 2.90, 4.28 vs. 4.30, 1.40 vs. 1.41 MAE) and Tolokers, but GTransformer leads on Proteins (73.9 vs. 73.2), Mutagen (73.1 vs. 72.2), and NCI1 (80.5 vs. 76.9). Against FSGNN, the closest competitor on node tasks, GNAN wins on 8 tasks including all QM9 regression tasks and ogb-arxiv, while FSGNN edges ahead on Cornell (86.0 vs. 85.7) and Cora (83.0 vs. 81.1). Overall, GNAN's strongest advantage is on long-range tasks where its global distance-based aggregation (rather than iterative local message-passing) provides a structural benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

GNAN extends the Generalized Additive Model (GAM) family—where predictions are sums of per-feature shape functions—to graph-structured data by introducing a learned distance function ρ that modulates how each node's features influence others based on graph topology. This builds directly on Neural Additive Models (Agarwal et al., 2021), which replaced splines with neural networks for shape functions, and on the disentanglement principle from FSGNN (Maurya et al., 2021) that separates feature processing from structural aggregation. Unlike message-passing GNNs (GCN, GAT, GIN) that iteratively refine node representations through neighborhood aggregation, GNAN performs a single distance-weighted aggregation, and unlike post-hoc GNN explainability methods (GNNExplainer, etc.), it is interpretable by design with exact visualizations of its learned functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GNAN addresses a critical gap: the EU AI Act and similar regulations increasingly mandate transparency for high-risk AI systems, yet most GNNs remain black-box models requiring post-hoc explanations without correctness guarantees. The paper demonstrates that this interpretability comes without significant accuracy loss, supporting the argument that many real-world graph problems do not require the full capacity of expressive GNNs. Open questions include whether the additive structure (no cross-feature interactions) becomes a bottleneck on more complex tasks, how the model scales to very large graphs given its O(N²) distance computation, and whether the interpretability benefits hold when extending to link prediction or heterogeneous graphs. The paper also notes that multi-class interpretability remains challenging and that spline-based activations could improve smoothness of learned shape functions.
