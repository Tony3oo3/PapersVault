## Problem

Graph-enhanced collaborative filtering methods (e.g., LightGCN) apply message passing to user-item bipartite graphs and empirically improve recommendation performance, but the underlying mechanism remains unverified. Prior work assumed message passing helps CF the same way it helps classic graph tasks (e.g., node classification), where high-degree nodes benefit most. This paper shows that assumption is incorrect: the source of improvement and the degree-dependent pattern in CF fundamentally differ from those in general GNN settings.

## Input / Output

TAG-CF takes as input a set of pre-trained user and item embedding matrices (U ∈ R^{|U|×d}, I ∈ R^{|I|×d}) produced by any CF method (e.g., MF trained with BPR or DirectAU) along with the user-item bipartite interaction graph. It outputs augmented user/item representations by performing a single neighbor aggregation at inference time, which are then used to compute user-item inner-product similarity scores for ranking. No retraining or parameter updates are required; the only tunable elements are two normalization exponents (m, n) selected from a small candidate set on validation data.

## Method

The paper first unrolls the LightGCN similarity formula to decompose message passing's contribution into (i) additional neighbor representations during the forward pass and (ii) accompanying gradient updates during back-propagation, then shows via ablation that forward-pass gains dominate (removing neighbor info causes ~25% degradation vs. ~2–4% from removing gradients). It further connects BPR and DirectAU objectives to Laplacian matrix learning, proving (Theorem 1) that these supervision signals inadvertently perform message passing in the backward step, which explains why high-degree users gain less from explicit MP. Building on these findings, TAG-CF applies a single message-passing aggregation at test time: u_i* = u_i + Σ_{i_j∈N(u_i)} |N(u_i)|^m |N(i_j)|^n · i_j, with m=n=−1/2 recovering one-layer LightGCN. A variant TAG-CF+ restricts aggregation to low-degree nodes only (selected via validation), further reducing cost while preserving or improving gains, since high-degree nodes have already benefited from the inadvertent backward-pass message passing during training.

## Results

TAG-CF consistently improves matrix factorization methods without graphs by up to 39.2% NDCG@20 on cold (low-degree) users and 31.7% on all users (MovieLens-1M, MF+TAG-CF: 29.20 vs. 20.98 low-degree; 29.65 vs. 22.51 overall). The additional computational overhead is negligible: TAG-CF adds only ~0.05×10³ seconds (less than 0.5% of total training time) across all datasets. Compared to trending graph-enhanced CF methods (SGL, SimGCL, LightGCL), TAG-CF achieves an average rank of 1.2 on NDCG@20 and 1.7 on Recall@20 while being the fastest method (rank 1.0 on running time), delivering comparable or better performance with less than 1% of their total training time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LightGCN (the de facto graph-based CF backbone), TAG-CF applied to MF achieves comparable or superior NDCG@20 on most datasets without any training-time message passing: on MovieLens-1M, MF+TAG-CF (29.65) substantially outperforms LightGCN (26.64) overall, and on Anime MF+TAG-CF (27.25) approaches LightGCN (27.97). Against NGCF, TAG-CF consistently outperforms on all six datasets for both low-degree and overall NDCG@20. Against ENMF, TAG-CF provides an average 9.1% NDCG@20 improvement on low-degree users and 7.1% overall, with the largest gain on MovieLens-1M (22.8% low-degree). Against UltraGCN (which already uses graph-based regularization during training), TAG-CF still yields further gains of 5–11% NDCG@20, demonstrating that test-time aggregation complements existing graph-enhanced training signals. Among the self-supervised graph CF methods in Table 4, TAG-CF outperforms SGL and LightGCL on all four datasets for NDCG@20, while SimGCL edges ahead on Anime (30.48 vs. 27.25) but TAG-CF wins on Yelp (6.36 vs. 5.99), Gowalla (9.88 vs. 10.32 is close), and Book (8.13 vs. 7.02); TAG-CF's average rank is 1.2 (NDCG) and 1.7 (Recall) versus SimGCL's 1.7 and 1.5, while TAG-CF is the fastest method (rank 1.0 on running time vs. 2.2–3.8 for the others).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LightGCN [20] and NGCF [60], which established message passing over user-item bipartite graphs as the dominant paradigm for graph-enhanced collaborative filtering, and on matrix factorization objectives (BPR [45], DirectAU [57]) that underpin non-graph CF. It departs from the standard GNN narrative by connecting CF supervision signals to Laplacian matrix learning [82, 10, 9], showing that BPR and DirectAU inadvertently perform message passing during back-propagation even without explicit graph structure. This theoretical bridge allows the authors to explain why the degree-bias behavior in CF (MP helps low-degree nodes more) is the inverse of what is observed in classic GNN tasks like node classification [54, 23], and to motivate a test-time-only aggregation strategy (TAG-CF) that circumvents the iterative training-time message passing used by LightGCN, NGCF, and their self-supervised extensions (SGL, SimGCL, LightGCL).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal decomposition of why message passing helps CF, separating forward-pass neighbor information from backward-pass gradient updates and showing the former dominates (~2% gain from gradients vs. ~25% from neighbor info). This reframing has practical consequences: TAG-CF achieves comparable or better performance than full training-time graph CF methods with less than 1% of their training time, making graph knowledge accessible to resource-constrained or large-scale deployment settings where iterative MP is prohibitive. Open questions include whether multi-hop test-time aggregation or learned normalization (beyond the hand-tuned m, n hyperparameters) could close the remaining gap to deeper GNNs, and whether the low-degree-bias finding generalizes to sequential or multi-behavior recommendation settings. A limitation is that TAG-CF's single-layer aggregation may underperform on very dense graphs where multi-hop information is critical.
