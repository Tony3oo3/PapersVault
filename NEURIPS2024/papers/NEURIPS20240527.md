## Problem

In many real-world applications (e.g., CRISPR gene perturbations), interventions are noisy and produce data from a mixture of intended and unintended interventional distributions rather than a single clean interventional distribution. Prior causal discovery methods either assume segregated interventional data, require knowledge of the causal graph, or lack identifiability guarantees for mixture components. This paper addresses the fundamental question of whether individual interventional distributions can be uniquely recovered from such a mixture without knowing the underlying causal graph.

## Input / Output

The method consumes iid samples from a single pooled dataset containing a fixed but unknown number of components (observational plus atomic interventional distributions from a Linear-SEM with Gaussian noise), along with a separate observational dataset and a user-specified cutoff ratio (default 0.07) for component selection. It outputs the estimated parameters (mean vector, covariance matrix, mixing weight) for each mixture component, the identified intervention target for each component, and a causal graph from the interventional Markov Equivalence Class (I-MEC). The number of mixture components is not given a priori and is estimated via a log-likelihood thresholding heuristic over candidate component counts from 2 to n+1.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is proving that under an 'Effective Intervention' assumption (at least one of mean shift γ, edge perturbation ‖c‖, or variance change δ is non-zero), the Frobenius-norm separation between any pair of interventional/observational covariance matrices and mean vectors is lower-bounded by a positive function of these intervention parameters (Lemma 5.1). This separation guarantees a positive radius of identifiability, enabling application of Belkin-Sinha's polynomial-time algorithm for Gaussian mixture recovery with sample complexity inversely proportional to the intervention magnitude. Practically, the two-step Mixture-UTIGSP pipeline first fits Gaussian mixture models via EM (scikit-learn) for each candidate component count and selects the optimal k using a relative log-likelihood change threshold, then passes the disentangled component parameters to UT-IGSP, which greedily searches over permutations to identify unknown intervention targets and output a graph from the I-MEC. The key insight is that the separation structure induced by the linear SEM's triangular adjacency matrix (via Sherman-Morrison rank-1 updates) ensures pairwise distinguishability of components even when intervention targets are unknown.

## Results

On the Protein Signaling Dataset (11 nodes, 6 mixture components), Mixture-UTIGSP achieves an average SHD of 16.75 versus 17.75 for Oracle UT-IGSP, and an average Jaccard Similarity of 0.03 versus 0.05 for the oracle. At the lowest cutoff ratio (0.01), Mixture-UTIGSP recovers 4 of 6 true components and achieves SHD=15, actually outperforming the oracle (SHD=19). In simulation experiments across 4–8 node graphs, parameter estimation error and SHD both decrease monotonically with sample size (from 2^10 to 2^17 samples), and the SHD of Mixture-UTIGSP closely matches that of Oracle UT-IGSP at all sample sizes, indicating that small parameter estimation errors do not degrade causal graph recovery.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Oracle UT-IGSP (Squires et al., UAI 2020), which is run with ground-truth disentangled interventional distributions rather than the mixed data. On the Protein Signaling Dataset, Mixture-UTIGSP achieves a lower average SHD (16.75 vs. 17.75) than the oracle, meaning the end-to-end pipeline from mixed data recovers a causal graph at least as close to the ground truth as when perfectly separated distributions are provided. However, Mixture-UTIGSP lags slightly on Jaccard Similarity for intervention target identification (0.03 vs. 0.05 average), and the gap widens at higher cutoff ratios where fewer components are recovered (1/6 vs. 4/6). In the simulation study, the SHD curves for Mixture-UTIGSP and Oracle UT-IGSP are nearly indistinguishable across all sample sizes and graph sizes, confirming that the mixture-disentangling step introduces negligible additional error in causal graph recovery. The authors note that even the oracle struggles with target identification (max JS = 1.0, oracle achieves only 0.05), suggesting that intervention target recovery is inherently difficult in this regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two previously separate threads: (1) the identifiability theory for mixtures of Gaussians from Belkin and Sinha [2], which provides the algorithmic backbone for disentangling mixture components, and (2) the UT-IGSP algorithm from Squires et al. [26] for causal discovery with unknown intervention targets. Prior causal discovery work with interventions (Squires et al. [26], Jaber et al. [10]) assumes interventional datasets are already segregated, while Kumar and Sinha [14] studied mixture identification but required knowledge of the topological order. This paper is the first to show that the full causal discovery pipeline—from mixed, unsegregated interventional data to I-MEC recovery—is possible in the Linear-SEM with Gaussian noise setting, by proving that interventions induce sufficient parameter separation to make the Gaussian mixture identifiable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is compelling: real-world interventions (e.g., CRISPR gene perturbations) suffer from off-target effects, producing data from mixtures of intended and unintended interventional distributions. The theoretical result that mixed data achieves the same identifiability (I-MEC) as perfectly segregated data is a clean guarantee that noise in intervention targeting does not fundamentally limit causal discovery. However, the scope is narrow—restricted to Linear-SEMs with additive Gaussian noise, causally sufficient models, and a fixed number of components—and the empirical component selection relies on a heuristic threshold. Open questions include extending to non-Gaussian noise, non-linear SEMs, latent confounders, and developing principled (non-heuristic) methods for selecting the number of mixture components.
