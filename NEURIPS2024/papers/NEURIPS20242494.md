## Problem

Higher-order cumulants (HOCs) are known to be critical for neural network performance on real data, yet the fundamental statistical and computational limits of learning from HOCs remain poorly understood. In particular, it was unclear how many samples are needed to detect non-Gaussianity encoded purely in fourth-order and higher cumulants, and whether neural networks can match the best achievable sample complexity or are outperformed by simpler methods like random features.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242494/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Inputs are d-dimensional vectors x ∈ R^d drawn from one of two classes: an isotropic Gaussian N(0, I_d) or a spiked cumulant distribution where a Rademacher spike direction u carries non-Gaussianity in the fourth-order cumulant while the covariance is whitened to the identity. Because both classes share the same mean and covariance, no linear or PCA-based method can separate them; the only signal resides in HOCs. The output is a binary class prediction, evaluated via early-stopping test accuracy, with the key metric being the sample complexity exponent θ (where n_class ∝ d^θ) at which each method transitions from random guessing to successful classification.

## Method

The paper combines three analytical and empirical tools. First, it derives an exact closed-form expression for the likelihood ratio norm (Theorem 2) in the spiked cumulant model, proving that statistical distinguishability requires only n ∝ d samples (θ = 1). Second, it computes low-degree likelihood ratio (LDLR) bounds (Theorem 5) showing that polynomial-time algorithms require n ∝ d² samples (θ = 2), establishing a wide statistical-to-computational gap. Third, it empirically trains two-layer ReLU networks (m = 5d hidden neurons, SGD with weight decay) and random-feature ridge regression on both the spiked Wishart and spiked cumulant models, demonstrating that neural networks achieve the quadratic computational threshold while random features fail even at quadratic sample complexity on the cumulant task. A final experiment on a non-linear Gaussian process image model shows that localisation of the fourth-order cumulant's CP-factors (measured by inverse participation ratio) precedes the neural network's learning transition, further confirming that feature learning from HOCs is the operative mechanism.

## Results

The paper establishes a statistical-to-computational gap in the spiked cumulant model: the likelihood ratio norm (Theorem 2) shows strong statistical distinguishability requires n ≍ d samples (θ = 1), while the low-degree likelihood ratio (Theorem 5) shows polynomial-time algorithms require n ≍ d² samples (θ = 2). Numerically, two-layer ReLU neural networks trained with SGD match the computational threshold, solving the spiked cumulant classification task at quadratic sample complexity (n_class ≍ d²), while random features fail to exceed random guessing even at quadratic sample complexity. On the NLGP image model, feature-learning networks (α = 1) learn the task, whereas the laziest network (α = 100) does not learn even with n = 10³d samples at d = 20.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is random features (RF), a fixed-feature-map method that approximates kernel methods. On the spiked Wishart task, neural networks achieve strong distinguishability at linear sample complexity (n_class ≍ d), while random features are limited to random guessing in this regime and only succeed at quadratic complexity (n_class ≍ d²), a gap confirmed by replica theory predictions. On the spiked cumulant task, the separation is even more dramatic: neural networks solve the task at quadratic sample complexity (n_class ≍ d²), matching the LDLR computational lower bound, whereas random features fail to learn even at quadratic complexity because they are restricted to a quadratic approximation of the target function, which is a fourth-order polynomial. On the NLGP image model, interpolating between feature-learning and lazy regimes via the α-renormalisation trick shows that fully-trained networks (α = 1) learn efficiently while the laziest network (α = 100), closest to random features, does not learn even with n = 10³d samples, confirming a large advantage of learned feature maps over fixed ones for extracting higher-order correlation structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spiked model framework from random matrix theory (spiked Wishart, spiked tensors) to the regime where the signal resides exclusively in higher-order cumulants, using the whitened data model from Wang & Lu's online ICA work [38]. On the analytical side, it builds on the low-degree likelihood ratio (LDLR) method of Hopkins [8] and the statistical-to-computational gap literature on Non-Gaussian Component Analysis (Diakonikolas et al. [11], Dudeja & Hsu [12]), providing an exact closed-form LR norm (Theorem 2) and an alternative direct proof of LDLR bounds. On the empirical side, it connects to the neural tangent kernel / random features separation literature [2, 4, 57–58] and the NLGP image model of Ingrosso & Goldt [13], using fundamental hypothesis-testing limits as benchmarks for neural network sample complexity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous quantification of how the statistical-to-computational gap for higher-order cumulants translates into a concrete separation between neural networks and random features: NNs match the quadratic computational bound while RF fails even at that bound, revealing that the feature-learning advantage of NNs is specifically amplified when the signal is in 4th-order (or higher) correlations rather than the covariance. Key open questions include extending the framework to non-Gaussian null hypotheses, understanding the SGD dynamics that allow NNs to extract HOC information efficiently, and whether the observed gap persists in more realistic, non-low-rank cumulant structures. The small-scale empirical validation (d ≤ 200) limits direct practical conclusions, but the theoretical framework is clean and the NLGP experiment suggests the phenomenon is not an artifact of the simplified spiked model.
