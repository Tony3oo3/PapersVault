## Problem

Existing SR methods uniformly sample training patches and apply L1/L2 losses equally to all weights, ignoring that smooth flat patches vastly outnumber textured ones in natural images (48.8% vs 16.6% in DIV2K). This dual imbalance—data distribution and model optimization—causes models to overfit smooth regions while underfitting complex textures, wasting computational resources where they are not needed. The paper formalizes this as an imbalanced distribution transfer learning problem from a statistical probability perspective.

## Input / Output

The method takes 128×128 low-resolution RGB image patches as input and produces corresponding high-resolution reconstructions at ×2 or ×4 scale factors. At inference, each patch is dynamically routed to one of M subnets (varying in width and depth) based on its gradient vector complexity, so the computational cost per patch adapts to content difficulty. The framework is plug-and-play, requiring no changes to the base network architecture (SRResNet or RCAN) or training data.

## Method

WBSR combines three components: (1) Hierarchical Equalization Sampling (HES) computes per-patch gradient vectors online to classify samples into K difficulty classes, then assigns higher sampling probability to rare hard patches via an exponential decay factor δ, performed in two phases (sample-level for stable initialization, then class-level for bias correction); (2) Balanced Diversity Loss (BDLoss) is derived from a distribution transformation theorem (Theorem 1) that relates the imbalanced training distribution p_train(y|x) to the desired balanced distribution p_bal(y|x), implemented via a Gaussian Mixture Model to obtain a closed-form loss that upweights texture regions while downweighting smooth ones; (3) Gradient Projection Dynamic Inference projects each test patch's gradient vector onto a subnet selection map, routing it to the appropriate subnet (or the optimal subnet under a computational budget C_t) without any additional classifier parameters. The supernet is trained with a divide-and-conquer strategy where subnets of varying widths/depths share weights, achieving ~34% average FLOPs reduction while matching or exceeding baseline PSNR.

## Results

On ×4 SR with RCAN, WBSR achieves 27.75 dB PSNR on B100 and 27.94 dB on Test4K while using only 57–83% of the baseline FLOPs, matching or exceeding the full RCAN (27.76/27.89 dB at 100% FLOPs). On the unseen B100 and Urban100 datasets, WBSR maintains near-baseline PSNR (e.g., 32.34 vs. 32.40 dB for ×2 RCAN) whereas ClassSR and ARM degrade by 0.5–1.0 dB. Across all four test sets and both scale factors, WBSR reduces average computational cost to ~62% of the static baseline while achieving comparable or superior PSNR, with the largest gains on texture-rich content (up to +0.13 dB over the full baseline in ablation).

## Baselines & Comparisons

WBSR consistently outperforms both ClassSR and ARM on PSNR across all four test datasets and both scale factors, while also using fewer FLOPs. For example, on ×4 RCAN at Test2K, WBSR reaches 26.45 dB at 57% FLOPs versus 26.39 dB at 65% (ClassSR) and 26.39 dB at 82% (ARM). On the unseen B100 dataset (×4 RCAN), WBSR achieves 27.75 dB—nearly matching the full RCAN baseline (27.76 dB)—while ClassSR drops to 26.70 dB and ARM to 26.74 dB, a gap of over 1 dB. WBSR also avoids the extra parameters of ClassSR (30.10 M vs. 15.59 M for RCAN) and the validation-set lookup table overhead of ARM. In the sampling-strategy comparison (Table 3), HES outperforms BSPA by ~0.1 dB PSNR on both B100 and Urban100, and the full WBSR† (static inference) further adds 0.08 dB over HES alone, confirming the complementary benefit of BDLoss.

## Relation to Prior Work

WBSR draws from two distinct lineages: (1) class-imbalanced learning from classification (resampling, reweighting, domain-adaptation-style distribution correction), which it repurposes for SR by defining 'classes' via patch gradient complexity rather than semantic labels, and (2) dynamic SR networks (ClassSR, ARM, PathRestore) that route patches to sub-networks of varying capacity. The key departure is that WBSR addresses the root cause of imbalance at training time (HES + BDLoss) rather than adding a classifier or lookup table at inference, and its BDLoss derivation builds on the GMM-based Balanced MSE [35] from imbalanced regression. The distribution-transformation theorem formalizes why uniform L1/L2 training on imbalanced patches systematically underfits rare texture regions, providing a probabilistic justification absent from prior dynamic-SR work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of WBSR (no architecture change, no extra parameters, no additional training data) makes it immediately applicable to any existing CNN-based SR pipeline, and the ~34% average FLOPs reduction with comparable-or-better PSNR is practically significant for resource-constrained deployment. However, the evaluation is limited to two CNN backbones (SRResNet, RCAN) at ×2 and ×4 scales; it remains open whether the framework transfers to transformer-based SR (e.g., SwinIR) or to video SR where temporal consistency adds constraints. The GMM approximation in BDLoss introduces hyperparameters (number of components L, regularization λ) whose sensitivity is not thoroughly explored, and the dynamic supernet still requires training nine subnets, which may not scale trivially to much larger architectures.
