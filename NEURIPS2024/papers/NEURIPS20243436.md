## Problem

Time-series anomaly detection research has been undermined by three interrelated issues: benchmark datasets with mislabeling, run-to-failure bias, and unrealistic anomaly ratios; evaluation measures (notably Point Adjustment) that favor noisy predictions to the extent that random scores can outperform state-of-the-art detectors; and inconsistent benchmarking practices where methods are compared on different datasets without unified preprocessing or proper hyperparameter tuning. These flaws collectively create an 'illusion of progress' where apparent improvements may stem from methodological artifacts rather than genuine algorithmic advances.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 1070 curated time series (870 univariate, 200 multivariate) spanning 40 datasets with highly variable lengths (from ~1,000 to ~631,000 points), dimensions (1 to 248 channels), and anomaly characteristics (point vs. sequence, single vs. multiple, anomaly ratios from 0.2% to 18.6%). Each of the 40 detection algorithms produces a continuous anomaly score per time point, which is then evaluated using 10 point-wise and range-wise measures; the benchmark's primary output is a ranked comparison of methods via VUS-PR, supplemented by Friedman/Nemenyi statistical tests and fine-grained breakdowns by anomaly type (point vs. sequence, single vs. multiple) and modality (univariate vs. multivariate).

## Method

TSB-AD introduces a three-step dataset construction pipeline that iteratively collects 46 candidate datasets, removes time series exhibiting common flaws (mislabeling, run-to-failure bias, infeasible anomaly ratios) through manual inspection by four annotators, and verifies label quality via an algorithmic test that checks whether at least one detector can identify each labeled anomaly within a bounded false-positive/false-negative margin. For evaluation, the paper systematically analyzes 10 measures along three axes—bias toward random/noisy scores, indiscrimination between distinct prediction scenarios, and sensitivity to temporal lags—identifying VUS-PR as the most robust, accurate, and fair metric. The benchmark evaluates 40 algorithms (25 statistical, 10 neural network, 5 foundation model) under a unified setup with z-normalization preprocessing and exhaustive hyperparameter tuning (450+ model variants) on a 15% per-dataset tuning split, ensuring fair comparison without the single-synthetic-dataset bias of prior studies.

## Results

TSB-AD evaluates 40 detection algorithms (25 statistical, 10 neural network, 5 foundation model) across 1,070 curated time series from 40 datasets, using 10 evaluation measures. The key finding is that Sub-PCA (a statistical method) dominates univariate anomaly detection rankings, while CNN and OmniAnomaly rank 2nd and 3rd on multivariate data. For point-based anomalies, foundation models TimesFM and Chronos rank 1st and 3rd respectively, but for sequence-based anomalies, statistical methods Sub-KNN and POLY outperform all others. VUS-PR is identified as the most reliable evaluation measure, while PA-F1 is shown to be severely biased (a random score achieves rank 26 out of 32 detectors under PA-F1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 40 algorithms, statistical methods (Sub-PCA, KShapeAD, Sub-KNN, POLY) consistently outperform neural network and foundation model approaches on univariate data, challenging the conventional assumption that deep learning architectures are superior. CNN [65] and OmniAnomaly [97] are the top neural network methods, ranking 2nd and 3rd on multivariate TSB-AD-M, contradicting prior benchmark [93] where KMeansAD [110] was found superior. Foundation models show a clear split: TimesFM [23] and Chronos [8] excel at point-based anomalies (ranking 1st and 3rd), but struggle with sequence anomalies due to their one-step-ahead predictive mechanism; MOMENT [38] (fine-tuned) outperforms its zero-shot variant and begins to show effectiveness in multi-anomaly scenarios. OFA [114], which fine-tunes GPT-2 for time series, yields unsatisfactory results, indicating a significant gap in LLM-based anomaly detection. Simpler architectures (CNN, LSTM) generally outperform complex transformer-based designs (TranAD [102], AnomalyTransformer [108], TimesNet [105]), consistent with findings in [92]. Sub-PCA and KShapeAD [72] demonstrate exceptional performance despite being overlooked as basic baselines in prior extensive evaluations [93, 112].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the TSB-UAD benchmark [74] from univariate-only to a unified univariate-and-multivariate collection, while building on the VUS-PR evaluation measure [69] as the primary accuracy metric. It directly addresses the critical flaws identified by Wu & Keogh [106] in prior datasets (mislabeling, run-to-failure bias, trivial anomalies) and the evaluation biases exposed by Kim et al. [48] and Wagner et al. [104] regarding Point Adjustment. The key departure from prior benchmarks (Schmidl et al. [93], Zhang et al. [112]) is the systematic investigation of evaluation measure reliability, comprehensive hyperparameter tuning across all datasets rather than a single synthetic set, and the inclusion of foundation models (MOMENT, TimesFM, Chronos, Lag-Llama, OFA) for the first time in an anomaly detection benchmark.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that simple statistical methods (Sub-PCA, KShapeAD) consistently outperform complex neural architectures in univariate anomaly detection challenges the field's prevailing assumption that deeper models are inherently superior, and the demonstration that Point Adjustment allows random scores to rival SOTA methods exposes a widespread evaluation flaw that has likely inflated reported progress. The benchmark's practical impact is substantial: it provides the first large-scale curated dataset (1070 time series, 40 datasets) with a principled label quality assessment pipeline, and identifies VUS-PR as the most robust evaluation measure. Open questions include the risk of data contamination in foundation model evaluations, whether the statistical-methods-dominance finding persists as architectures improve, and how to properly assess LLM-based approaches that currently yield unsatisfactory results.
