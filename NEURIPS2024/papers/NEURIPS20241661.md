## Problem

Fixed kernel regression is fundamentally limited by the alignment between the kernel's eigenvalue ordering and the target function's coefficient structure: even with identical eigenfunctions, a mismatch in eigenvalue decay order can degrade convergence rates from near-optimal to severely suboptimal (e.g., suffering the curse of dimensionality for low-dimensional signals). The NTK regime, which fixes the kernel throughout training, cannot resolve this misalignment. The paper asks whether over-parameterization can allow the effective eigenvalues to adapt to the signal structure during gradient flow, thereby recovering near-optimal rates without prior knowledge of the signal.

## Input / Output

The method operates on the Gaussian sequence model z_j = θ*_j + ξ_j (the diagonalized limit of kernel regression), taking as input the noisy observations, an initial eigenvalue sequence λ_j ∝ j^{-γ} (γ > 1), and the noise level ε (with ε² ∝ n^{-1}). It produces a recovered signal sequence θ̂_j via early-stopped gradient flow, along with adapted eigenvalue parameters a_j(t)b_j^D(t) that grow for signal components and remain bounded for noise components. A key practical feature is that the optimal stopping time t ∝ ε^{-(2D+2)/(D+2)} is universal—it depends only on the depth D and noise level, not on the unknown signal parameters p and q that govern misalignment severity.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the fixed parameterization θ_j = λ_j^{1/2} β_j with the over-parameterized form θ_j = a_j β_j (two-layer) or θ_j = a_j b_j^D β_j (deeper), where a_j(0) = λ_j^{1/2} and β_j(0) = 0 (with b_j(0) = b_0 ∝ ε^{1/(D+2)} for the deeper case). Both a_j and β_j (and b_j) are trained simultaneously via component-wise gradient flow on the loss L_j = ½(θ_j − z_j)², so the effective eigenvalue a_j²b_j^{2D} is no longer fixed but evolves during training. Conservation quantities (a² − β² ≡ λ, b² − Dβ² ≡ b_0²) allow explicit analysis: signal components drive a_j b_j^D to grow proportionally to |θ*_j|^{(D+1)/(D+2)}, while noise components with small λ_j remain bounded near their initial values. This adaptivity yields a generalization error of ε²[Φ(ε) + ε^{-2/(D+2)·1/γ}] + Ψ(ε ln(1/ε)), which under the misalignment model (4) recovers the near-optimal rate ε^{2p/(p+1)} (up to a log factor) regardless of the misalignment parameter q, whereas the vanilla fixed-eigenvalue method only achieves ε^{2p/(p+q)}. Deeper parameterization (larger D) further reduces the residual overfitting term ε^{-1/γ} to ε^{-2/(D+2)·1/γ}, providing a theoretical justification for depth in enhancing eigenvalue adaptation.

## Results

The over-parameterized gradient descent (OpGD) achieves a nearly optimal convergence rate of ε^(2p/(p+1)) (up to a logarithmic factor), regardless of the misalignment parameter q, while vanilla fixed-eigenvalue gradient descent only achieves ε^(2p/(p+q)). In the headline numerical experiment (p=1, q=2, γ=1.5 or 3), OpGD empirically achieves a convergence rate of ~0.50 (matching the theoretical optimum r*=0.5), whereas vanilla GD achieves only ~0.33. Deeper over-parameterization (D=1, D=2) further reduces the extra error term from ε^(-1/γ) to ε^(-2/(D+2)·1/γ), yielding modest but consistent rate improvements (e.g., from 0.50 to 0.44–0.52 depending on settings).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is vanilla (fixed-eigenvalue) gradient descent, which is the diagonalized kernel gradient flow. Under the misalignment setting (4) with p=1, q=2, OpGD achieves a convergence rate of ~0.50 (the near-optimal 2p/(p+1)=0.5), while vanilla GD is stuck at 2p/(p+q)=0.33 — a substantial gap that widens as q grows. The gap is most dramatic in the low-dimensional structure example (Example 2.2), where vanilla GD with an isotropic kernel suffers the curse of dimensionality (rate 2t/(2t+d)) while OpGD recovers the dimension-free rate 2t/(2t+d₀). Deeper over-parameterization (D=1, D=2) provides incremental improvements over D=0, particularly when the eigenvalue decay rate γ is small (e.g., γ=1.1): for p=0.6, q=2, the rate improves from 0.50 (D=0) to 0.52 (D=1) to 0.49 (D=2), and for p=1, q=2 from 0.48 to 0.49 to 0.48, confirming the theoretical prediction that depth reduces the ε^(-1/γ) penalty. On real-world data (California Housing, Concrete Compressive Strength), the paper demonstrates that Fourier coefficients of the regression function are indeed misaligned with the kernel's eigenvalue ordering, validating the practical relevance of the misalignment phenomenon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of kernel regression theory and the over-parameterization literature. It builds on the NTK framework (Jacot et al., 2018) and classical kernel regression analysis (Yao et al., 2007) to establish the fixed-kernel baseline, then draws on the over-parameterized gradient descent line of work (Hoff, 2017; Zhao et al., 2022; Vaškevičius et al., 2019; Li et al., 2021a) for the algorithmic mechanism. The key departure is interpretive: whereas prior over-parameterization results in high-dimensional linear regression frame the benefit as implicit regularization toward sparsity, this paper shows that in the sequence model / kernel regression setting, over-parameterization with component-wise eigenvalue initialization (a_j(0) = λ_j^{1/2}) functions as an eigenvalue adaptation mechanism that aligns the effective kernel spectrum with the signal structure, rather than merely inducing a sparsity bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete theoretical mechanism explaining why over-parameterized networks can outperform their NTK (fixed-kernel) counterparts: the trainable eigenvalues adapt to the signal's coefficient ordering, recovering near-optimal rates even under severe misalignment (large q) where vanilla kernel gradient descent degrades. This reframes over-parameterization from a regularization phenomenon to a structural adaptation phenomenon, connecting to the 'feature learning' literature (Gatmiry et al., 2021; Yang and Hu, 2022). Open questions include extending the analysis to coupled (non-diagonal) kernel regression where components interact, and whether the eigenvalue adaptation mechanism generalizes to learning the eigenfunctions themselves (a truly adaptive kernel). The current results are confined to the diagonalized sequence model, so the practical transfer to finite-width neural networks remains conjectural.
