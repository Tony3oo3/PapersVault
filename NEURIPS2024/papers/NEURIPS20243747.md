## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detection datasets consist primarily of fully synthesized images, often limited to human faces, and lack fine-grained control over the degree and type of perturbation. This makes it impossible to determine whether detectors are genuinely robust or merely biased toward specific augmentation magnitudes, data distributions, or generation methods. The paper asks: do state-of-the-art detectors reliably identify images with varying levels of targeted, localized perturbation, and where exactly do they fail?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243747/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes real images (27,600) from six semantic segmentation benchmarks (CityScapes, SUN RGBD, CelebA HQ, Human Parsing, ADE20K, OpenImages) paired with their instance segmentation masks and class labels. It produces 1,329,155 AI-augmented images where perturbations are targeted to specific masked regions, each accompanied by structured metadata: area ratio of change, semantic magnitude scores (LPIPS, DreamSim, Sentence Similarity), directional semantic edit pairs (original → perturbed caption/label), scene complexity and diversity, and a localized-vs-diffused classification derived from connected-component analysis of the post-perturbation mask.

## Method

The core contribution is a plug-and-play perturbation pipeline extending LANCE that combines two augmentation techniques—conditional inpainting (guided by LLM-perturbed mask labels) and prompt-based-editing (guided by LLM-perturbed captions)—across five diffusion models (Stable Diffusion v1.4/v1.5, SDXL, OpenJourney, Kandinsky 2.2). A semantic taxonomy (small/medium/large) drives LLaVA-Mistral-7B and LLAMA-7B to generate targeted caption or label perturbations, enabling controlled variation in semantic change magnitude. Two rounds of saliency checks filter outputs: caption-level filtering uses CLIPScore and CLIP text-embedding cosine similarity, while image-level filtering uses BRISQUE (reference-free quality), CLIP image similarity, and CLIP directional similarity to verify structural integrity and semantic alignment. Post-augmentation, a custom thresholding-and-connected-component algorithm computes a surface-area change mask and classifies each edit as localized or diffused, while percentile-based binning (25th/75th) categorizes all metrics into small, medium, and large bins for standardized stress testing of six detectors.

## Results

On a 10% evaluation sample of SEMI-TRUTHS (87,000 images: 27,000 real, 60,000 AI-augmented), UniversalFakeDetect achieves the highest overall Precision (64.84%) and Fake-class Recall (87.03%), while DE-FAKE achieves the highest Fake-class Precision (73.33%) and demonstrates the most robust performance across varying magnitudes of augmentation. Three of the six detectors (DINOv2, CNNSpot, DIRE) fail almost entirely, with Fake-class Recall at or near 0%, indicating they cannot distinguish AI-augmented images from real ones. Among the three viable detectors, performance varies significantly by data distribution, diffusion model used for augmentation, and degree of perturbation, revealing distinct sensitivities for each model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UniversalFakeDetect [59] leads on Fake-class Recall (87.03%), substantially outperforming DE-FAKE (71.48%) and CrossEfficientViT (30.28%), making it the most sensitive detector to AI-augmented content. DE-FAKE [73] achieves the highest Fake-class Precision (73.33%) and is the most robust across varying magnitudes of augmentation (both area ratio and semantic change), though it struggles with human-face augmentations. CrossEfficientViT [11] is intermediate overall but shows the largest sensitivity to data distribution, with significant performance drops on non-face benchmarks (ADE20K, CityScapes, SUN-RGBD) due to its face-centric training. DINOv2 [60], CNNSpot [86], and DIRE [88] all fail catastrophically with Fake-class Recall at 0.00–0.01%, confirming that zero-shot foundation models and single-domain detectors (GAN-only or diffusion-only) are ineffective against localized, partial AI augmentations. The paper further reveals that no single detector dominates across all axes: UniversalFakeDetect performs worst on prompt-based-editing and Kandinsky 2.2 augmentations, while DE-FAKE excels on Kandinsky 2.2 but is weakest on StableDiffusion v1.5, suggesting complementary ensemble strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LANCE stress-testing framework [62] from general visual model evaluation to the specific domain of AI-generated image detection, introducing targeted, localized perturbations of real images rather than fully synthetic ones. It departs from prior AI-generated image datasets (DFDC, FaceForensics++, Celeb-DF, GenImage, etc.) that focus on binary real-vs-fully-synthetic classification, often limited to human faces or single generator models, by providing fine-grained control over perturbation magnitude along two axes (area ratio and semantic change) across 6 diverse source distributions and 5 diffusion models. The use of LLMs (LLaVA, LLaMA) to generate semantically controlled caption/label perturbations, combined with diffusion inpainting and prompt-based editing, creates a spectrum of 'semi-truths' that prior benchmarks do not capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic characterization of complementary detector weaknesses—e.g., UniversalFakeDetect excels on facial edits but fails on vegetation, while DE-FAKE shows the inverse pattern—provides actionable guidance for ensemble design and highlights that no single detector is robust across all perturbation axes. The finding that the novel Area Ratio metric correlates most strongly with human perception (Spearman 0.50) while standard perceptual metrics like LPIPS and SSIM show near-zero or negative correlation suggests that pixel-space structural change, not learned perceptual similarity, better captures what humans notice as 'edited.' Key limitations include reliance on manual segmentation masks (limiting scalability), LLM perturbation quality requiring aggressive filtering (~74% and ~55% pass rates), and the open question of whether detectors can be made robust across all simultaneously varying axes of distribution, magnitude, and augmentation method.
