## Problem

Standard Neural Algorithmic Reasoning (NAR) trains networks in a closed-book supervised paradigm where each prediction depends solely on a single problem instance, ignoring the rich contextual information available in the training set. This paper asks whether granting the network access to training-set examples during both training and inference—an open-book setting—can substantially improve algorithmic reasoning, and whether such access reveals interpretable relationships among different algorithmic tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240314/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each reasoning step t, the network receives the graph-structured problem instance x, the previous step's state y^(t-1), and 240 auxiliary data points sampled from the training set (or from all 30 CLRS datasets in multi-task mode, 8 per dataset). The auxiliary points are compressed into a fixed-size representation matrix R via the dataset encoder, which randomly samples one pair of adjacent states per point and pools node-level linear projections. The output is the predicted next-step graph state y^(t), i.e., the node/edge/graph-level labels describing the algorithm's execution at step t.

## Method

The open-book framework augments the standard encode–process–decode NAR pipeline with two plug-in modules: a Dataset Encoder that maps each auxiliary data point (x_i, y_i) to a single latent vector r_i by averaging linear projections over randomly sampled adjacent state pairs, and an Open-Book Processor inserted between the processor and decoder that performs cross-attention (query from current hidden states h^(t), key/value from the training-set representation R) followed by a gating function to blend attended information back into the hidden states. The framework is architecture-agnostic and is instantiated on PGN, MPNN, and Triplet-GMPNN. In multi-task mode, auxiliary points are drawn uniformly across all 30 CLRS datasets, and the learned cross-attention weights over these points serve as an interpretable measure of inter-task relevance, enabling a 'paired-task' training strategy that matches or exceeds full multi-task training while training only on two tasks.

## Results

On the CLRS Algorithmic Reasoning Benchmark (30 tasks, 8 categories), the open-book framework applied to PGN, MPNN, and Triplet-GMPNN achieves state-of-the-art F1 scores in 7 out of 8 task categories. Headline improvements include Strings (72.41% vs. 4.08% prior best), Sorting (83.65% vs. 71.53%), and Graphs (85.37% vs. 64.98%). The only category where the open-book approach does not lead is Divide and Conquer (74.52% vs. 76.36% for Triplet-GMPNN alone), though it still surpasses the prior best of 65.23%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the open-book framework (applied to PGN, MPNN, and Triplet-GMPNN) against four prior architectures: Memnet [26], PGN [29], MPNN [9], and NPQ [12] (grouped as 'Prior Best'), as well as Triplet-GMPNN [11] individually. The open-book approach outperforms the prior best in 7 of 8 categories, with the largest gains on Strings (+68.33 pp), Graphs (+20.39 pp), and Sorting (+12.12 pp). Against Triplet-GMPNN specifically, the open-book framework wins in 6 of 8 categories (e.g., Sorting: 83.65% vs. 60.38%; Strings: 72.41% vs. 49.09%) but trails on Divide and Conquer (74.52% vs. 76.36%). In the multi-task setting, the open-book framework nearly replicates full multi-task training on Triplet-GMPNN while training on a single task, and in the paired-task experiment it surpasses both single-task and full multi-task training on 12 of 15 evaluated tasks (e.g., SCC: 73.39% paired vs. 43.43% single vs. 48.48% multi-task).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard encode-process-decode paradigm for Neural Algorithmic Reasoning (NAR) by introducing an 'open-book' learning framework where the network can access representations of all training instances during reasoning, rather than relying solely on the single input instance. It builds directly on three existing processor architectures—PGN, MPNN, and Triplet-GMPNN—by inserting two additional modules (a Dataset Encoder and an Open-Book Processor with cross-attention) between the processor and decoder. Conceptually, the framework fuses parametric deep learning with non-parametric learning, drawing an analogy to Kossen et al.'s self-attention between datapoints in image classification, but applied to sequential algorithmic reasoning over graph-structured data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The open-book framework offers a practical and interpretable alternative to full multi-task training: by feeding other tasks' training data into the dataset encoder, a single-task-trained network can nearly replicate (and sometimes exceed) multi-task training performance at roughly the cost of single-task training. The attention-weight analysis revealing that tasks attend more to tasks in *other* algorithmic categories than their own is a counterintuitive insight into how algorithmic knowledge transfers. However, the approach introduces variance from random auxiliary-point sampling, can occasionally be counterproductive, and has been validated only on the CLRS benchmark; whether the framework generalizes to other reasoning domains or scales to much larger training sets remains open.
