## Problem

Current multi-threshold metrics for evaluating Selective Classification (SC) systems, particularly the widely used AURC, are built on Selective Risk—a conditional probability designed for single working-point evaluation—which is not suitable for holistic aggregation across rejection thresholds. This leads to excessive over-weighting of high-confidence failures, violating monotonicity and ranking interpretability, and ultimately producing misleading method rankings that hinder SC research progress. The paper identifies five formal requirements for SC evaluation metrics and demonstrates that no existing metric satisfies all of them.

## Input / Output

The metric takes as input a set of N predictions, each paired with a confidence score from an arbitrary confidence scoring function (CSF) and a ground-truth label, along with a chosen error function (defaulting to 0/1 misclassification). It produces a single scalar AUGRC value bounded in [0, 1/2], where lower values indicate better SC performance. Unlike AURC, which integrates the conditional Selective Risk over coverage, AUGRC integrates the joint Generalized Risk P(Y_f=1, g(x)≥τ) over coverage, making it directly interpretable as the average probability of a silent failure across all working points.

## Method

The key insight is shifting from Selective Risk (conditional on acceptance) to Generalized Risk (the joint probability of misclassification and acceptance) when aggregating performance across thresholds. AUGRC is defined as the integral of Generalized Risk over coverage, yielding a closed-form expression AUGRC = (1−AUROC_f)·acc·(1−acc) + ½(1−acc)², which decomposes into the probability that two random samples are both failures plus the probability that one is a failure with higher confidence than the other. This formulation guarantees monotonicity in both classifier accuracy and CSF ranking quality (both partial derivatives are strictly negative), provides a permutation-distance-based ranking interpretation analogous to AUROC, and accommodates arbitrary error functions and external CSFs. Empirically, on the FD-Shifts benchmark spanning 6 datasets and 13 CSFs, AUGRC substantially changes top-3 method rankings on 5 out of 6 datasets compared to AURC, with bootstrap-based Wilcoxon tests confirming the ranking differences are statistically significant and not due to test-data variability.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical finding is that replacing AURC with the proposed AUGRC metric substantially changes confidence scoring function (CSF) rankings: the top-3 methods (out of 13) change on 5 out of 6 i.i.d. test datasets. On CIFAR-10, the best AUGRC score is 3.38×10⁻³ (DG-MCD-MSR), while the worst is 5.56×10⁻³ (Devries et al.); on CAMELYON-17-Wilds, DG-Res and DG-PE achieve the best scores of 3.46×10⁻³. A concrete monotonicity violation of AURC is demonstrated on CIFAR-10, where DG-Res (higher accuracy and AUROC_f) is erroneously ranked below MCD-PE by AURC but correctly ranked above it by AUGRC. Bootstrap-based Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (500 resamples, Holm-corrected) confirm that the ranking differences are statistically significant and not due to test-set variability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 13 CSFs (MSR, MLS, PE, MCD-MSR, MCD-PE, MCD-MI, ConfidNet, DG-MCD-MSR, DG-Res, Devries et al., TEMP-MLS, DG-PE, DG-TEMP-MLS) under both AURC and AUGRC. The central comparison is not between methods but between the two metrics: AUGRC reorders the top-3 CSFs on 5 of 6 datasets relative to AURC. On CIFAR-10, AURC erroneously ranks MCD-PE above DG-Res despite DG-Res having both higher classification accuracy and higher AUROC_f, violating the monotonicity requirement (R2); AUGRC corrects this. Monte Carlo Dropout variants (MCD-MSR, MCD-PE) consistently outperform their deterministic counterparts (MSR, PE) under AUGRC, while temperature scaling (TEMP-MLS) shows no consistent improvement over MLS. DeepGamblers-based methods (DG-Res, DG-PE, DG-TEMP-MLS) achieve the best AUGRC on CAMELYON-17 and CIFAR-10, whereas ConfidNet performs poorly on iWildCam (82.0×10⁻³) but well on CAMELYON (4.36×10⁻³), highlighting dataset-dependent CSF performance that the metric makes transparent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the AURC framework introduced by Geifman et al. (2018), which has become the de facto standard multi-threshold metric for Selective Classification. The key conceptual departure is identifying that AURC's reliance on Selective Risk (a conditional probability over accepted predictions) is inappropriate for holistic aggregation across thresholds, and replacing it with Generalized Risk (a joint probability over all predictions). The paper draws an explicit analogy to the AUROC in standard classification, where evaluating at a single operating point is insufficient for method development, and positions its contribution within the broader SC evaluation literature that includes e-AURC, NLL/Brier scores, AUROC_f, and F1-AUC, systematically showing how each fails to meet five formalized requirements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that AURC violates monotonicity—meaning it can rank a strictly worse CSF as better—is a significant correction to the field's primary evaluation tool, with practical consequences demonstrated by substantially different top-3 method rankings on 5 of 6 benchmark datasets. This matters because SC is increasingly deployed in safety-critical settings (clinical diagnostics, autonomous systems), and using a flawed metric could lead to selecting inferior confidence scoring functions. Open questions include the relationship between confidence ranking and calibration, whether partial AUGRC (analogous to partial AUROC) is appropriate for application-specific risk/coverage constraints, and how to extend the benchmark to newer SC methods beyond the FD-Shifts framework.
