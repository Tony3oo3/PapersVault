## Problem

Despite the proliferation of ML models for enzyme function prediction, no standardized benchmark exists to evaluate how well these models generalize to unseen protein sequences or, critically, to unannotated reactions. Existing classification tasks are limited to a closed vocabulary of EC numbers, meaning models cannot be tested on reactions that have never been linked to any known enzyme. This paper formalizes two complementary tasks—sequence-to-EC classification and reaction-to-EC retrieval—with carefully designed out-of-distribution splits to expose real-world generalization failures.

## Input / Output

Task 1 takes a protein sequence (filtered to length 100–1024) and outputs a single EC number at four hierarchical levels (X.X.X.X through X.-.-.-), evaluated as k=1 accuracy. Task 2 takes a chemical reaction in SMILES/SMARTS format and outputs a ranked list of EC numbers with associated protein sequences; unlike Task 1, the output vocabulary is open, meaning test reactions may correspond to EC numbers entirely absent from training. The benchmark provides four Task 1 splits (by sequence identity threshold, previously misclassified, and promiscuous enzymes) and three Task 2 splits (easy/medium/hard) that progressively increase the dissimilarity between test and train reactions.

## Method

CREEP (Contrastive Reaction-EnzymE Pretraining) is a multimodal contrastive learning model that aligns three modalities—protein sequence (via fine-tuned ProtT5), reaction SMILES (via fine-tuned rxnfp), and gene ontology text (via SciBERT)—into a shared 256-dimensional latent space. Training uses an EBM-NCE objective (adapted from GraphMVP) that maximizes mutual information between positive pairs from the same EC family while contrasting negatives sampled from a Gaussian approximation of the data distribution, with batches constructed so all triplets come from distinct EC numbers. Downstream retrieval ranks EC numbers by distance from the query reaction (or text) representation to EC-clustered protein centroids, and CREEP w/ Text averages rankings from both reaction-to-protein and text-to-protein retrieval paths. The benchmark itself contributes a curated dataset of 184,529 sequence-EC pairs and 61,766 reaction-EC pairs (4,960 unique ECs) with splits designed to test sequence-identity extrapolation, activity-cliff generalization, and reaction-space extrapolation at increasing difficulty.

## Results

On Task 1 (enzyme classification), CLEAN achieves the best Level-4 accuracy on the <30% identity split (55.1%) and 30-50% identity split (80.2%), while Foldseek leads on the challenging Price split (41.2%) and BLASTp dominates the promiscuous split (93.7%). On Task 2 (enzyme retrieval), CREEP with text achieves 60.3% Level-4 accuracy on the easy split, outperforming the Similarity Baseline (59.3%) and CLIPZyme (12.2%), but all methods drop sharply on harder splits (CREEP w/ Text: 7.4% medium, 1.3% hard). Overall, the results indicate substantial room for improvement, particularly on low-identity sequences and out-of-distribution reactions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Task 1, CLEAN (supervised contrastive learning) outperforms BLASTp and Foldseek on the <30% and 30-50% identity splits by 1–4 points at Level 4, but Foldseek surpasses both on the Price split (41.2% vs. 35.1% BLASTp, 31.8% CLEAN), suggesting structural conservation helps where sequence is misleading. On the promiscuous split, BLASTp (93.7%) and Foldseek (88.0%) far exceed CLEAN (69.4%), indicating that simple homology search remains strong when test sequences are highly similar to training data. ChatGPT performs near-random on all Task 1 splits (0.0% Level 4), while Pika bridges the gap but still trails standard tools. On Task 2, CREEP with text (60.3% Level 4, easy) matches the DRFP Similarity Baseline (59.3%) and substantially outperforms CLIPZyme (12.2%); on medium and hard splits, CREEP variants consistently beat both the Similarity Baseline and CLIPZyme, though absolute accuracy remains low (≤7.4% Level 4), highlighting the difficulty of generalizing to entirely unseen EC numbers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the contrastive learning paradigm for multimodal alignment (EBM-NCE from GraphMVP) and pretrained protein/reaction language models (ProtT5, rxnfp) to propose CREEP, a three-modality (protein, reaction, text) contrastive pretraining method for enzyme retrieval. For Task 1, it positions itself within the enzyme EC classification literature (CLEAN, HiFi-NN, Enzhier, ProtEx) but departs by formalizing standardized out-of-distribution evaluation splits that prior work lacked. For Task 2, it extends CLIPZyme's reaction-protein graph alignment by adding a textual modality and using sequence-based language models rather than 3D structure graphs, while drawing benchmark design inspiration from protein fitness prediction suites (TAPE, FLIP, ProteinGym).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CARE is the first standardized benchmark for enzyme function prediction, filling a critical gap that has hindered fair comparison of the many ML methods proposed for EC classification. The introduction of Task 2 (reaction-to-enzyme retrieval) opens an entirely new research direction for generalizing beyond annotated reactions, which is essential for applications like retrobiosynthesis and orphan enzyme annotation. Performance on the hard splits remains weak across all methods (best ~10% at EC level 4), indicating substantial room for improvement. Open questions include how to handle multi-complex enzymes, improve text annotations to reduce indirect data leakage, and whether direct reaction-to-sequence retrieval (bypassing EC numbers) will become feasible as higher-resolution annotations accumulate.
