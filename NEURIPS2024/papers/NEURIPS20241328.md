## Problem

Traditional persistent homology and its existing robustness extensions (DTM, Fermat distances, core distance) fail to detect correct topology when data with low intrinsic dimensionality is embedded in a high-dimensional ambient space, because Euclidean distances become dominated by noise dimensions. This paper identifies this specific high-dimensional noise failure mode—distinct from the low-dimensional outlier problem—and seeks distance metrics that preserve topological signal under such conditions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241328/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a high-dimensional point cloud (e.g., single-cell RNA-seq data with 10–5156 dimensions, n up to ~1000 points) and constructs a symmetric k-nearest-neighbor graph from it. The kNN graph's spectral decomposition yields a pairwise distance matrix (effective resistance or diffusion distance) that replaces the raw Euclidean distance as input to Vietoris-Rips persistent homology via ripser. The output is a persistence diagram of detected loops and voids, scored by a relative-gap loop detection metric s_m that measures the persistence gap between the m-th and (m+1)-th most persistent features.

## Method

The key insight is that spectral distances on the kNN graph—effective resistance and diffusion distance—incorporate information from all graph edges via random walks, making them robust to the distance concentration that plagues Euclidean metrics in high dimensions. The paper derives a novel closed-form spectral embedding for von Luxburg et al.'s corrected effective resistance (Proposition 6.1), showing it equals a squared Euclidean distance in a space of eigenvectors of the symmetrically normalized graph Laplacian scaled by (1−μ_i)/√μ_i. A further result (Corollary 6.2) proves that corrected effective resistance equals the sum of all diffusion distances except the two most local ones (t=0 and t=1/2), unifying the two spectral methods. Practically, the pipeline is: build a symmetric kNN graph → compute the effective resistance or diffusion distance matrix via eigendecomposition of the graph Laplacian → pass this distance matrix to ripser for Vietoris-Rips persistent homology, requiring only a single hyperparameter (k) for effective resistance.

## Results

On a noisy circle in R^50, Euclidean-distance persistent homology drops to a loop detection score of zero at noise σ≈0.2, while effective resistance and diffusion distance maintain scores above 0.9 up to σ≈0.3. On the Malaria scRNA-seq dataset, both effective resistance and diffusion distance achieve s₂ > 0.9 for the two ground-truth cell-cycle loops, whereas correlation distance and DTM fail to identify them. Across all six single-cell RNA-seq datasets, spectral methods (effective resistance, diffusion distance, Laplacian Eigenmaps) consistently achieve higher loop detection scores than Euclidean, Fermat, DTM, geodesic, UMAP/t-SNE graph, and embedding-based distances. On synthetic benchmarks in R^50, spectral methods outperform all competitors on every dataset except the torus, where effective resistance is on par with Euclidean.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Spectral distances (effective resistance, diffusion distance, Laplacian Eigenmaps) clearly outperform Euclidean distance in high-dimensional settings: on the circle in R^50, Euclidean reaches zero detection score at σ≈0.2 while spectral methods remain above 0.9 until σ≈0.3. DTM distance collapses even earlier than Euclidean (at σ≈0.15 due to the paper's thresholding heuristic), and Fermat distance provides essentially no improvement over Euclidean on most datasets. Among non-spectral graph distances, geodesic distance, UMAP/t-SNE graph affinities, and HDBSCAN core distance all offer only modest improvement over Euclidean, confirming that simple kNN-graph-based distances cannot handle high-dimensional noise. Embedding-based methods (t-SNE, UMAP 2D) perform well on the circle but have inherent limitations (e.g., a 2D embedding cannot represent a void) and UMAP struggles with 3D surfaces and the torus void. Against the concurrent work of Hiraoka et al. who recommend normalized PCA preprocessing, effective resistance outperforms PCA on the linked circles and eyeglasses datasets and matches it on the circle, without requiring knowledge of the true data dimensionality. Among the three spectral methods, effective resistance is recommended over diffusion distance due to its single hyperparameter (k) and greater outlier resistance, though diffusion slightly outperforms effective resistance at the highest noise levels on some synthetic datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on using non-Euclidean distances as input to persistent homology, building on Bendich et al.'s suggestion to use diffusion distances (validated only in 2D) and von Luxburg et al.'s corrected effective resistance formula. It departs from prior robustness-focused extensions (DTM, Fermat distances, core distance) that target outliers in low-dimensional settings, instead addressing the distinct failure mode of high-dimensional noise where all Euclidean distances concentrate. The key theoretical contribution is a new closed-form spectral embedding for corrected effective resistance (Proposition 6.1) and a proof that it aggregates all but the most local diffusion distances (Corollary 6.2), unifying these spectral methods under a common eigendecomposition framework. The work is complementary to the concurrent theoretical analysis of Hiraoka et al., who propose normalized PCA as a remedy but require knowledge of the true data dimensionality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides a practical, drop-in solution for topology detection in the high-dimensional noisy regime that dominates real-world applications (scRNA-seq, neural network activations, sensor data), where standard persistent homology silently fails. The finding that existing spectral graph distances—already well-understood tools from dimensionality reduction—resolve the curse of dimensionality without requiring new algorithmic machinery is both reassuring and actionable. Open questions include: a theoretical proof that spectral distances provably mitigate distance concentration, a principled criterion for choosing between effective resistance and diffusion distance, and automatic evaluation of whether detected cycles are biologically/structurally correct. The methods also require dense sampling, which in high dimensions demands prohibitively large sample sizes, limiting applicability to very sparse datasets.
