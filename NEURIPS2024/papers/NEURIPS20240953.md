## Problem

Subsampling pretrained features is a practical way to reduce computational cost, but the precise relationship between the implicit regularization induced by subsampling and explicit ridge regularization was previously established only for linear features, with conjectures left open for random and kernel features. More broadly, no general framework existed to characterize how arbitrary observation weighting (bootstrapping, importance weighting, variance-adaptive weighting) interacts with ridge regularization in high-dimensional settings.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained feature matrix Φ ∈ R^{n×p} (e.g., from ResNet-50), a general weight matrix W ∈ R^{n×n} (diagonal for subsampling/bootstrapping, or non-diagonal for observation sketching), a ridge penalty λ, and a response vector y. It produces the equivalent full-data ridge level μ via the S-transform of W^T W, a tuned ridge ensemble estimator, the optimal subsample size k* (bounded by rank of the Gram matrix), the optimal ensemble size M*, and extrapolated risk estimates. A key structural detail is that the equivalence path is entirely data-dependent and computable from the available Gram matrix without requiring knowledge of the feature distribution.

## Method

The core mechanism is to view subsampling as a weighted regression problem and exploit asymptotic freeness between the weight matrix W^T W and the feature Gram matrix ΦΦ^T/n, using tools from free probability theory (S-transforms and Cauchy transforms) developed for feature sketching. Theorem 1 establishes that for any weight matrix satisfying a freeness assumption, the weighted ridge estimator (W, λ) is asymptotically equivalent to the unweighted ridge estimator (I, μ) whenever λ = μ / S_{W^T W}(−df/n), where df is the effective degrees of freedom. For subsampling without replacement, this general formula collapses to the remarkably simple linear relation (1 − df/n)(1 − λ/μ) = 1 − k/n, which resolves prior conjectures for random and kernel features. The paper further derives a bias-variance risk decomposition for M-ensembles showing the excess risk over the full-data estimator decays as 1/M, and builds a cross-validation tuning algorithm (combining CGCV and extrapolated CV) that selects optimal k, λ, and M from a small number of base estimators.

## Results

The paper's headline result is Theorem 2, which gives a closed-form equivalence path for subsampling: (1 − df/n)·(1 − λ/μ) = (1 − k/n), showing that subsampling fraction k/n, regularization inflation λ/μ, and normalized complexity df/n are linked by a simple linear relation. Theorem 6 establishes that the ensemble risk of M weighted ridge estimators equals the full-data ridge risk plus an O(1/M) variance term, enabling efficient tuning via small ensembles. Proposition 7 shows the optimal subsample size satisfies k* ≤ rank(G_I), meaning one never needs more observations than the feature dimension for optimal risk. Empirically, on ResNet-50 pretrained features applied to CIFAR-100 and Flowers-102, the predicted equivalence paths (Equation 4) match empirical degrees-of-freedom paths closely, and the CGCV+ECV risk estimator (Algorithm 1) accurately tracks true prediction risk even at a subsampling ratio of k/n = 0.01 with M₀ = 25 base estimators.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper that extends prior work rather than competing with specific baselines on a benchmark. The key extension is over Patil & Du (2023, [50]), which established subsampling–ridge equivalence only for linear features; here, Propositions 3–5 generalize the path to random features (resolving Conjecture 7 of [50]) and kernel features including NTKs (resolving Conjecture 8 of [50]), and Theorem 1 generalizes the weight structure from subsampling to arbitrary weight matrices satisfying asymptotic freeness. Compared to risk-characterization approaches in [37, 51] that require Gaussian or linear feature assumptions to compute population-level risk formulas, this paper's estimator-equivalence framework avoids specifying the risk in population quantities, requiring only bounded operator norms and asymptotic freeness. The empirical validation on ResNet-50 features (CIFAR-100, Flowers-102) confirms that the theoretical path predictions hold for real pretrained representations, with the predicted equivalence lines matching empirical degrees-of-freedom contours across architectures and datasets. The tuning algorithm (Algorithm 1) is validated against actual prediction errors, showing close agreement between estimated and true risks at various ensemble sizes, demonstrating practical utility over naive full-data ridge tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the subsampling-ridge equivalence framework of Patil & Du (2023) [50], which was established only for linear features, to general weight matrices and feature structures (random features, kernel features, and arbitrary pretrained representations) by reframing subsampling as a weighted regression problem and leveraging free probability tools from LeJeune et al. (2024) [39]. The key methodological departure is avoiding population-quantity risk characterizations in favor of directly relating estimators at different regularization levels via asymptotic freeness, which resolves Conjectures 7 and 8 from [50]. For practical tuning, the paper combines CGCV [13] and ECV [26] into a unified cross-validation procedure for subsample ridge ensembles, building on the risk characterization framework of Patil et al. (2023) [51].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified theoretical lens for understanding how subsampling acts as implicit regularization across diverse pretrained feature types, with the practical consequence that one can tune subsample size instead of ridge penalty on large datasets, reducing computational cost. The bound k* ≤ rank(G_I) on optimal subsample size is a clean generalization of prior linear-feature results and has direct implications for efficient use of pretrained representations when n >> p. However, the analysis is limited to ridge regression on fixed pretrained features; extending to generalized linear models, iterative methods (early stopping), or combining multiple implicit regularization sources (mini-batch subsampling + gradient descent) remains open.
