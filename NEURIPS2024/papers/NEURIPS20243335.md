## Problem

The aleatoric uncertainty of the treatment effect—captured by the conditional distribution of the treatment effect (CDTE)—is not point-identifiable due to the fundamental problem of causal inference, yet it is essential for quantifying the probability of benefit and treatment-effect quantiles in medical decision-making. Existing methods for estimating the resulting Makarov bounds rely on naive plug-in estimators that are sensitive to nuisance misspecification and lack the orthogonal learning theory available for identifiable quantities like CATE. No comprehensive meta-learning framework existed for covariate-conditional estimation of these partially identifiable bounds.

## Input / Output

The method takes an observational dataset of i.i.d. samples (x_i, a_i, y_i) with binary treatment, continuous outcome, and potentially high-dimensional covariates (d_x up to 785 in HC-MNIST), along with a discretized δ-grid (for CDF) or α-grid (for quantiles) of size 50. It produces upper and lower Makarov bounds on the CDF of the CDTE, F(δ|x), and on the quantiles F^{-1}(α|x), evaluated at each covariate point x. The working model is constrained to be non-decreasing and bounded in [0,1] for CDFs, reflecting the structural properties of the target estimand.

## Method

The AU-learner is a two-stage orthogonal learner: the first stage estimates nuisance functions (propensity score and conditional outcome CDFs/quantiles) via cross-fitting, and the second stage fits a working model to the Makarov bounds using a Neyman-orthogonal loss derived from a newly established efficient influence function for these bounds (Theorem 1). The orthogonal loss applies a γ-scaled one-step bias correction to the covariate-adjusted (CA) learner loss, where γ ∈ (0,1] interpolates between the theoretically optimal full correction (γ=1) and the constraint-respecting CA-learner (γ=0), addressing the fact that pseudo-CDFs from the bias correction may violate monotonicity or [0,1] bounds. The neural instantiation, AU-CNFs, uses conditional normalizing flows as the backbone: a nuisance CNF fits the first-stage functions via conditional log-likelihood, while two target CNFs (for upper and lower bounds) are trained in the second stage by minimizing the orthogonal CRPS or W₂² loss. The AU-learner achieves Neyman-orthogonality and quasi-oracle efficiency, meaning the bias from nuisance misspecification is second-order, and it outperforms plug-in and CA baselines particularly in high-dimensional and out-of-sample settings.

## Results

On the high-dimensional HC-MNIST benchmark (d_x=785), AU-CNFs(CRPS) achieves the best out-sample rCRPS of 0.347±0.114 (upper bound) and 0.357±0.097 (lower bound), outperforming the next-best CA-CNFs(CRPS) (0.382/0.388) and Plug-in CNF (0.399/0.391). For the Wasserstein-2 metric, AU-CNFs(CRPS) also leads with 0.940±0.389 (upper) and 0.948±0.344 (lower). In synthetic experiments across three outcome distribution settings (normal, multi-modal, exponential) and training sizes from 100 to 1000, AU-CNFs achieve the best mean out-sample rCRPS in the majority of settings, with the advantage growing as training data increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares AU-CNFs against a hierarchy of learners (Plug-in CNF, IPTW-CNF, CA-CNFs) and one external baseline, Plug-in DKME (distributional kernel mean embeddings from Muandet et al. [97]). On HC-MNIST, AU-CNFs(CRPS) outperforms all internal baselines on both rCRPS and W₂ for both upper and lower Makarov bounds, with the largest gap over Plug-in CNF (~0.05 rCRPS) and CA-CNFs(CRPS) (~0.035 rCRPS). The Plug-in DKME baseline is omitted from HC-MNIST due to prohibitive runtime at d_x=785, but in the synthetic experiments (Figure 5) it is included and AU-CNFs consistently achieve lower rCRPS, particularly as training data grows. The CRPS-based AU-learner outperforms the W₂²-based variant, suggesting CDF-space losses are easier to optimize. In low-sample settings (n_train=100), CA-learners and even plug-in approaches sometimes match or slightly beat AU-learners, but the AU-learner's quasi-oracle efficiency ensures it is asymptotically superior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of partial identification theory (Makarov bounds for the CDTE, from Fan et al. 2010 and Makarov 1982) and the orthogonal learning / debiased ML framework (Chernozhukov et al. 2018, Kennedy 2023, Nie & Wager 2021). Prior estimators for Makarov bounds relied on plug-in (single-stage) approaches or were restricted to population-level, binary-outcome, or known-propensity settings (Kallus 2022, Semenova 2023, Ji et al. 2023). The key departure is deriving the efficient influence function for covariate-conditional Makarov bounds—a non-trivial step due to the sup/inf convolution structure—and building a general two-stage orthogonal learner (AU-learner) with a conditional normalizing flow instantiation (AU-CNFs), extending the authors' prior work on interventional normalizing flows (Melnychuk et al. 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable gap: aleatoric uncertainty of the treatment effect (as opposed to potential outcomes or distributional treatment effects) has been largely neglected in causal ML, yet it is essential for clinical decision-making (e.g., probability of harm). The quasi-oracle efficiency guarantee means the AU-learner is robust to nuisance misspecification, which is critical in observational settings. Limitations include reliance on pointwise (not uniformly) sharp Makarov bounds, continuous outcomes, and standard potential-outcomes assumptions; open questions include extensions to high-dimensional outcomes, interval probabilities, and connections to conformal prediction for total uncertainty.
