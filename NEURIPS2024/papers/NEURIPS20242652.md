## Problem

Existing model extraction methods using counterfactual explanations lack theoretical guarantees linking reconstruction error to query complexity, and suffer from decision boundary shift when counterfactuals are treated as ordinary training points—especially in the common one-sided setting where counterfactuals are only provided for rejected instances. Prior approaches (Aïvodji et al. 2020, Wang et al. 2022) also require counterfactuals from both sides of the decision boundary, which is often unavailable in practice.

## Input / Output

The method consumes query instances drawn from the unfavorable (rejected) region of a normalized [0,1]^d input space, the target model's binary predictions for those queries, and one-sided counterfactuals generated by methods such as MCCF (L2 or L1 norm), DiCE, ROAR, C-CHVAE, or 1-NN. Counterfactuals receive a special soft label of 0.5 to distinguish them from ordinary instances labeled 0 or 1. The output is a trained surrogate neural network (ReLU hidden layers with sigmoid output) whose fidelity—fraction of matching predictions with the target—is evaluated over both uniform and held-out test data.

## Method

The theoretical core shows that the line from a query to its closest counterfactual is perpendicular to the decision boundary (Lemma 3.1), so counterfactuals yield tangent hyperplanes whose intersection forms a circumscribing polytope; random polytope theory then gives an exact error bound of O(n^{-2/(d-1)}) for convex boundaries (Theorem 3.2) and a probabilistic reconstruction guarantee for ReLU networks via a grid-based covering argument (Theorem 3.6). For practical deployment, the paper proposes the Counterfactual Clamping Attack (CCA), which trains the surrogate with a novel loss L_k that assigns counterfactuals a label of 0.5 and applies a modified penalty—binary cross-entropy toward k minus a constant h(k)—only when the surrogate's prediction falls below k (typically 0.5), while using standard binary cross-entropy for ordinary instances. This clamping forces the surrogate's decision boundary to pass near the counterfactuals without over-penalizing those already deep in the favorable region, directly mitigating the decision boundary shift that plagues the baseline. The approach operates with one-sided counterfactuals only, and a Lipschitz-based bound (Theorem 3.10) extends the guarantees to non-closest counterfactuals and locally Lipschitz models.

## Results

The primary metric is fidelity (fraction of matching predictions between target and surrogate models), evaluated with 400 counterfactual queries. CCA achieves 94–99% fidelity on D_test and 91–96% on D_uni across the four real-world datasets (Adult Income, COMPAS, DCCC, HELOC) with a known surrogate architecture, consistently outperforming the Aivodji et al. baseline (89–92% on D_test, 84–95% on D_uni). The largest gain is on DCCC where CCA reaches 99±0.9% fidelity versus 89±8.9% for the baseline. CCA also maintains its advantage when the surrogate architecture is unknown (e.g., 94±3.2% vs 91±4.5% on Adult Income D_test).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CCA is compared against two prior model-extraction-from-counterfactuals methods. Against Aivodji et al. (2020) (the 'Baseline'), which treats counterfactuals as ordinary labeled training points, CCA wins on all four datasets and both evaluation splits, with fidelity improvements ranging from +2 to +10 percentage points (e.g., +10 on DCCC D_test: 99% vs 89%; +7 on Adult Income D_uni: 91% vs 84%). The baseline suffers from decision boundary shift under one-sided counterfactuals, which CCA's clamping loss explicitly mitigates. Against Wang et al. (2022) (DualCFX), which queries the counterfactual of the counterfactual to reduce boundary shift, a direct comparison is limited because DualCFX requires two-sided counterfactuals while CCA operates with one-sided only. In the two-sided setting (Table 6), CCA2 (CCA with both-sided data) matches or slightly exceeds DualCFX (e.g., 0.99 vs 0.99 on DCCC D_test at n=200; 0.98 vs 0.98 on HELOC D_test at n=200), while CCA1 (one-sided only) remains competitive, confirming that the one-sided restriction does not severely degrade performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-extraction-via-counterfactuals line initiated by Aïvodji et al. (2020) and Wang et al. (2022), both of which treat counterfactuals as ordinary labeled training points or use counterfactual-of-counterfactual queries, respectively. The key departure is twofold: (i) it restricts to one-sided counterfactuals (only for rejected instances), a more realistic MLaaS setting that prior methods cannot handle, and (ii) it grounds the reconstruction analysis in random polytope theory (Böröczky Jr and Reitzner, 2004), yielding the first explicit query-complexity-to-error relationships for counterfactual-based model reconstruction. The proposed Counterfactual Clamping loss function is a practical mechanism that operationalizes the theoretical insight that counterfactuals lie near the decision boundary, without requiring them to be exactly closest.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work formalizes a concrete privacy risk in MLaaS platforms that expose one-sided counterfactual APIs, providing both theoretical guarantees and a practical attack that outperforms existing baselines by 2–10 percentage points in fidelity across four real-world tabular datasets. Open questions include the lack of exact characterization of k(ε) and v*(ε) in Theorem 3.6, the inapplicability of local Lipschitz assumptions to non-smooth models like decision trees, and the absence of multi-class or image-domain extensions. The Lipschitz-constant-to-extractability connection also raises broader questions about whether regularizing models for robustness inadvertently reduces their extractability, and motivates future defense strategies for counterfactual APIs.
