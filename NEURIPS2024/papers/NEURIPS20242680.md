## Problem

Fairness criteria (e.g., independence, sufficiency, or absence of causal influence) satisfied by a continuous prediction score S are not guaranteed to hold after the simple thresholding operation that converts S into a binary predictor Ŷ, a step universally applied in practice. This means that a small disparity in the true outcome Y can be dramatically amplified into a large disparity in the final decision, or vice versa, with no existing framework to quantify or attribute the source of the resulting gap. The paper asks: how much of the disparity in a thresholded predictor is inherited from the real-world outcome, and how much is introduced by the optimization (rounding) procedure itself, along each causal pathway?

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational data structured according to the Standard Fairness Model (binary protected attribute X, confounders Z, mediators W, binary outcome Y), a prediction score S (typically the L2-optimal conditional probability), a threshold t, and a business necessity specification (which causal pathways—DE, IE, SE—are deemed justifiable and at what strength). It produces a path-specific decomposition of the total variation P(ŷ|x₁)−P(ŷ|x₀) into six terms (three causal pathways × two sources: Y and M), a binary SUCCESS/FAIL verdict on weak or strong business necessity via Algorithm 1, and per-sample causal influence values that identify subpopulations driving the disparity. All quantities are identifiable from observational data under the SFM, with explicit estimation formulas and bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification.

## Method

The key technical device is the margin complement M(u) = 1(S(u) ≥ t) − S(u), which captures the signed deviation introduced by thresholding for each unit. Theorem 1 proves that the TV measure of the optimal 0/1 predictor decomposes additively into x-specific direct, indirect, and spurious effects on both S and M, so that for each causal pathway the contribution from the probability prediction is separated from the contribution from the rounding step. Theorem 2 then establishes that, under the Standard Fairness Model, the causal effects of X on the L2-optimal score S are exactly equal to those on the true outcome Y, yielding Corollary 3: the disparity in Ŷ splits into an 'inherited' component (from Y) and an 'introduced' component (from M). Building on this, the paper defines weak business necessity (inherited disparity allowed, but margin-complement contribution must be zero) and strong business necessity (both allowed), and provides Algorithm 1 to audit a given predictor against these criteria. Identification and estimation formulas (Propositions 4–5) show all terms are computable from observational data via inverse-probability-weighted estimators, and sample-level influences are shown to be causally meaningful (proportional to the x-specific direct effect for the corresponding covariate stratum).

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that a 2% disparity in the true outcome Y (p₀=0.49 vs. p₁=0.51) amplifies to a 100% disparity in the optimal 0/1 predictor Ŷ after thresholding at 0.5, with 98% of the disparity originating from the margin complement rather than the underlying outcome. On MIMIC-IV, the direct effect decomposition reveals that minority-group patients have a lower probability of receiving acute care purely based on race, with margin complements further amplifying this gap for a small subgroup of individuals. On COMPAS, the predictor Ŝ^NP exhibits a statistically significant direct effect of race (x-DE ≠ 0) while the true outcome Y does not, constituting a fairness violation; margin complement contributions along indirect and spurious pathways are also significantly non-zero, exacerbating inter-group differences. On Census 2018, strong direct effects for both Y and M indicate men are more likely to receive a starting bonus, with the team requiring the direct margin complement effect to be reduced to zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods or baselines in the traditional benchmarking sense. It is a methodological contribution that introduces the margin complement decomposition (Thm. 1, Cor. 3) and weak/strong business necessity criteria (Def. 3, Alg. 1) as new analytical tools. The empirical evaluation applies these tools to three real-world datasets (MIMIC-IV, COMPAS, Census 2018) to demonstrate that thresholding prediction scores into binary decisions systematically amplifies existing disparities — a phenomenon not captured by prior causal fairness frameworks that analyze only the continuous score S. The key practical finding is that fairness guarantees satisfied at the score level (e.g., x-CE(s) = x-CE(y)) can be entirely violated at the decision level due to non-zero margin complement contributions, motivating the new regulatory distinction between weak and strong business necessity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal fairness framework of Zhang and Bareinboim (2018b) and Plecko and Bareinboim (2024), which decompose group disparities into direct, indirect, and spurious path-specific effects, by introducing the margin complement to account for the prediction-to-decision transformation. It builds on the causal business necessity literature (Kilbertus et al. 2017; Plecko and Bareinboim 2024b) by distinguishing weak from strong necessity based on whether thresholding-induced amplification is permitted. The work also connects to the fair decision-making literature (Chouldechova 2017; Dwork et al. 2020) that has noted fairness criteria can be violated after thresholding, but provides the first causal decomposition that isolates the thresholding contribution along each pathway.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, auditable tool for regulators and system designers to determine whether a binary decision amplifies or merely inherits existing disparities, with the weak/strong business necessity distinction offering a concrete regulatory specification that prior legal frameworks lacked. A key limitation is reliance on the Standard Fairness Model and its untestable assumptions (e.g., no confounding between X and Z), which may not hold in complex real-world settings. Open questions include extending the decomposition to multi-class outcomes, non-monotonic utilities, and dynamic settings where decisions feed back into future outcomes, as well as practical guidance for specifying which pathways fall under business necessity in contested legal contexts.
