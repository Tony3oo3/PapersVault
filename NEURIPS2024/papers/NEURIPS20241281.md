## Problem

Standard hyperparameter optimization (HPO) protocols use fixed resampling splits (e.g., a single train-validation holdout or a fixed cross-validation scheme) for all hyperparameter evaluations, an assumption made without strong justification. This paper investigates the surprising and largely unexplored question of whether reshuffling the splits for each new configuration can improve the final model's generalization performance, and provides the first theoretical explanation for when and why this effect occurs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular dataset, a learning algorithm (XGBoost, CatBoost, Elastic Net, or a funnel-shaped MLP), a hyperparameter search space, and a resampling scheme (1-fold holdout, M-fold CV, or M-fold holdout) as input, with the key control being whether splits are reshuffled between HPC evaluations. The output is the selected hyperparameter configuration (incumbent) whose generalization is assessed on a held-out outer test set, alongside a theoretical regret bound that quantifies the expected suboptimality as a function of the reshuffling parameter τ, the loss-surface curvature m, and the noise correlation constant κ.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretical analysis showing that reshuffling resampling splits changes the asymptotic covariance structure of the validation loss surface, introducing a correlation parameter τ that decorrelates validation losses across distinct HPCs. Theorem 2.1 derives the limiting Gaussian distribution of the loss surface, and Theorem 2.2 yields a regret bound E[μ(λ̂) − μ(λ*)] ≤ σ√d[8 + B(τ) − A(τ)], where B(τ) penalizes reshuffling (risk of selecting a far-away configuration by noise luck) and A(τ) rewards it (chance of selecting a near-optimal configuration by noise luck). Reshuffling is beneficial precisely when the loss surface is flat (small curvature m) and the noise is weakly correlated (large κ), because it hedges against the catastrophic scenario where all configurations near the optimum are simultaneously dominated by unfavorable noise. Empirically, the authors validate these predictions through controlled simulations and large-scale benchmarks on AMLB tabular datasets using random search (500 HPCs) and Bayesian optimization via HEBO and SMAC3 (250 HPCs), finding that reshuffled holdout achieves test performance competitive with 5-fold CV while requiring substantially fewer model fits per evaluation.

## Results

Across benchmark HPO experiments on tabular binary classification tasks (n ∈ {500, 1000, 5000}), reshuffling resampling splits during HPO yields equal or better test ROC AUC performance compared to fixed splits for all resampling protocols. The most striking result is that reshuffled holdout achieves final test performance on par with standard 5-fold CV while requiring substantially fewer model fits per HPC evaluation (1 vs. 5). For Bayesian optimization (HEBO, SMAC3), reshuffling also improves generalization and narrows the performance gap between BO and random search, suggesting that the choice of resampling protocol may matter more than the choice of optimizer for final generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three HPO optimizers—Random Search, HEBO, and SMAC3—each under fixed vs. reshuffled resampling splits. Reshuffling consistently improves or maintains test ROC AUC for all three optimizers, with the largest gains observed for Random Search under holdout. HEBO and SMAC3 outperform Random Search in absolute generalization performance under both fixed and reshuffled splits, but the performance gap between BO methods and Random Search narrows substantially when reshuffling is applied, indicating that the resampling protocol choice can partially compensate for a weaker optimizer. For M-fold CV, reshuffling has minimal effect (τ²=1 in both fixed and reshuffled cases), confirming the theoretical prediction that CV is largely unaffected by reshuffling. For logloss specifically, reshuffling rarely helps, likely due to the high sensitivity of that loss function violating the stability assumptions in the theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper addresses a largely overlooked aspect of hyperparameter optimization: the choice of whether to fix or reshuffle resampling splits across evaluated configurations. While the oversearching/overtuning problem has been recognized since Quinlan & Cameron-Jones (1995) and studied empirically by several groups, the specific practice of reshuffling splits was only briefly investigated in Lefesque's (2018) PhD thesis and applied in evolutionary optimization by Larcher & Barbosa (2022). The paper departs from the standard assumption that fixed splits facilitate fair comparison by providing the first theoretical analysis (a regret bound via Gaussian process concentration) explaining when and why reshuffling helps, and by systematically benchmarking the effect across multiple resampling schemes, optimizers (random search, HEBO, SMAC3), and learning algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that reshuffled holdout can match 5-fold CV in generalization performance while being substantially cheaper, which is actionable for practitioners with limited compute. The theoretical regret bound connects the benefit of reshuffling to the signal-to-noise ratio and curvature of the loss surface, providing a principled understanding of when the technique helps versus hurts. Open questions remain: reshuffling appears to hurt for logloss at small sample sizes (likely due to loss instability), the analysis is asymptotic and ignores the dynamic nature of sequential HPO algorithms, and experiments are limited to tabular binary classification. The finding that the choice of optimizer (random search vs. BO) matters less than the choice of resampling protocol for final generalization is a notable secondary insight.
