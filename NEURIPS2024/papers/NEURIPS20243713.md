## Problem

Learning credal set predictors that faithfully represent both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty remains challenging: standard approaches trained on zero-order (single-label) data produce biased epistemic uncertainty estimates, and existing credal set methods in machine learning lack formal validity guarantees. This paper addresses the gap by asking whether conformal prediction can be adapted to construct credal sets with distribution-free coverage guarantees, given access to first-order (probabilistic) training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243713/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires first-order training data—instances paired with full probability distributions over classes (e.g., aggregated multi-annotator label frequencies) rather than single labels—for both training and calibration. At prediction time, a single query instance (a premise-hypothesis text pair or an RGB image) is fed to the model, and the output is a convex credal set: a region within the (K−1)-dimensional probability simplex guaranteed to contain the true class distribution with probability at least 1−α. From this set, aleatoric uncertainty is read off as the lower Shannon entropy and epistemic uncertainty as the gap between upper and lower entropy.

## Method

The core mechanism is to conformalize either a first-order probabilistic classifier (trained by cross-entropy on soft labels) or a second-order Dirichlet-parameterized predictor (trained by negative log-likelihood) into a credal set predictor via a nonconformity score. For the first-order route, nonconformity is a distance (total variation, KL divergence, Wasserstein, or 1-minus-inner-product) between the candidate distribution and the model's point prediction; for the second-order route, it is 1 minus the relative likelihood under the predicted Dirichlet. A quantile of calibration scores at level α′ determines the threshold, and the credal set is the sublevel set of the nonconformity function at that threshold, inheriting the standard conformal coverage guarantee (Theorem 3.1). A key practical distinction is that the second-order approach yields adaptively shaped sets whose geometry depends on the local skewness of the predicted distribution, whereas first-order sets are rigid distance balls around the point prediction. The paper further extends the guarantee to noisy first-order data under a bounded-noise assumption (Theorem 3.2), showing that inflating the threshold by a noise tolerance ε restores validity for the ground-truth distribution.

## Results

On ChaosNLI (α=0.2), all five nonconformity functions (TV, KL, WS, Inner, SO) maintain the conformal coverage guarantee, with mean coverage across 10 random seeds aligning with or exceeding the nominal 80% level. Efficiency (fraction of simplex covered) varies substantially: for three illustrative instances, the SO (second-order) method achieves the smallest credal sets (efficiency 0.06–0.47) compared to first-order methods (0.18–0.41), demonstrating superior adaptivity. On CIFAR-10-H, the first-order predictors (pre-trained on zero-order CIFAR-10 data) produce uncertainty intervals that effectively capture the discrepancy between model predictions and human-annotated distributions. The adaptive variant (normalized nonconformity, τ=0.1) reduces average epistemic uncertainty for first-order methods, bringing their AU–EU scatter plots closer to the SO method's behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it compares five nonconformity functions (TV, KL, WS, Inner, SO) and two predictor types (first-order vs. second-order) under a unified conformal framework. The SO (second-order, Dirichlet-based) method consistently produces the most adaptive credal sets: its set geometry depends on both the calibration quantile and the skewness of the predicted distribution, whereas first-order methods produce fixed-radius neighborhoods around the point prediction. KL divergence tends to limit set expansion toward high-entropy regions, while the Inner (1-minus-inner-product) method systematically includes simplex corners, forcing lower entropy to zero and occasionally yielding empty credal sets. First-order methods exhibit a structural limitation: low epistemic uncertainty coincides only with high aleatoric uncertainty, an artifact of their lack of local adaptivity. The proposed normalized nonconformity variant (Appendix D) partially mitigates this issue, reducing average EU and shifting AU–EU scatter points closer to the SO method's behavior. Coverage is maintained across all methods and datasets, confirming that the conformal guarantee is independent of the nonconformity choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges conformal prediction (Vovk et al.) with imprecise probability theory by using the former's calibration machinery to construct credal sets—sets of probability distributions that jointly encode aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. It departs from prior credal-set methods (naive credal classifiers, imprecise Bayesian networks) that learn from zero-order labels and offer no formal validity guarantees, and from prior conformal prediction work (e.g., Stutz et al. on ambiguous ground truth) that produces sets of class labels rather than sets of distributions. The second-order predictor component draws on evidential deep learning (Sensoy et al.) with Dirichlet parameterization, while the adaptivity enhancement in Appendix D repurposes normalized nonconformity scores (Papadopoulos et al.) from regression conformal prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first conformal-prediction-based framework for constructing credal sets with a distribution-free coverage guarantee, enabling principled decomposition of total uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components via upper/lower Shannon entropy. A key practical limitation is the requirement for first-order (probability-labeled) calibration data, which restricts applicability to domains with multiple annotations or soft labels; extending the method to standard zero-order data remains an open challenge. Additional open questions include finding nonconformity functions that yield closed-form credal sets (avoiding numerical simplex discretization) and determining appropriate noise-bounding parameters (ε, δ) for the imprecise-data generalization in broader settings.
