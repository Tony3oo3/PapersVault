## Problem

Standard RL algorithms assume a fixed-frequency discrete-time MDP, but many real-world systems (greenhouse control, medical treatment) are inherently continuous in time and each measurement or action switch involves costly manual intervention. Choosing an appropriate global discretization frequency is difficult, especially for systems with multiple dominant time scales or unknown dynamics. This paper formalizes the problem of jointly deciding *what* control to apply and *how long* to hold it, minimizing costly interactions while maintaining or improving performance.

## Input / Output

The policy receives an augmented state s = (x, b, t) where x is the continuous system state, b is the integrated reward accumulated since the last interaction, and t is the time-to-go; in the bounded-interactions setting the index k of past interactions is appended. The policy outputs a pair (u, τ) — the control action and the duration for which it is held before the next interaction — so the effective action space is U × [t_min, t_max]. The transition dynamics depend on the chosen duration τ through the SDE flow, meaning the number of MDP steps per episode is itself a learned quantity rather than a fixed hyperparameter.

## Method

TACOS reformulates the continuous-time SDE control problem as an equivalent discrete-time MDP by defining a state flow Ξ(x, u, t) that integrates the drift and diffusion over a variable time interval, making the problem directly solvable by any standard RL algorithm. Two practical settings are handled: (i) interaction cost, where each measurement/action-switch incurs a penalty c(x,u), and (ii) bounded interactions, where a hard budget K limits total interactions over the horizon. For model-free learning, TACOS is combined with SAC or PPO by augmenting the action head to output the duration alongside the control. For model-based learning, OTACOS performs optimistic planning over a well-calibrated probabilistic model (e.g., Gaussian Process) of the flow function Φ*, and the paper proves sublinear regret bounds under Lipschitz continuity assumptions on the SDE drift and diffusion.

## Results

On the Pendulum swing-up task with a constant interaction cost of 0.1, TACOS reduces the number of interactions from 200 (equidistant) to just 24 while retaining performance. Under a hard budget of K=5 interactions, TACOS still solves the pendulum swing-up task, whereas equidistant discretization fails. For OTACOS, the model-based algorithm achieves higher rewards with significantly fewer episodes than SAC-TACOS (which requires ~6000 episodes on Pendulum and ~2000 on RC car) and outperforms PETS-TACOS and MEAN-TACOS baselines in sample efficiency. SAC-TACOS and PPO-TACOS are robust to the choice of minimum action duration t_min, while standard SAC suffers a significant performance drop at higher interaction frequencies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard SAC with equidistant time discretization, TACOS consistently outperforms: it drastically reduces the number of interactions (e.g., 200→24 on Pendulum with cost 0.1) while maintaining or improving episode reward, and it is robust to the choice of interaction frequency t_min where standard SAC degrades sharply. In the bounded-interactions setting (small K), TACOS achieves significantly higher rewards than equidistant discretization, particularly on Greenhouse and Pendulum swing-down where the system reaches equilibrium and requires fewer interactions. For the model-based comparison (Figure 5), OTACOS is more sample-efficient than both PETS-TACOS (adapted PETS) and MEAN-TACOS (planning with the mean model), achieving higher rewards in fewer episodes. OTACOS also outperforms the model-free SAC-TACOS in sample efficiency, requiring substantially fewer episodes to converge on both Pendulum and RC car environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads—adaptive sensing (Treven et al., 2023) and event-triggered/self-triggered control (Heemels et al., 2021)—into a single MDP formulation where the policy jointly decides both the control action and its duration. It builds directly on optimistic model-based RL (Curi et al., 2020; Sukhija et al., 2024) by extending the difference-lemma and regret analysis to the time-adaptive setting, and on standard policy-gradient methods (SAC, PPO) by showing they can be applied unchanged once the problem is cast as an MDP. Unlike prior heuristic approaches (Holt et al., 2023; Karimi, 2023; Ni and Jang, 2022) that add ad-hoc thresholds or regularization to discourage frequent interactions, TACOS provides a principled reformulation with no-regret guarantees via OTACOS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: in domains like greenhouse management, medical treatment scheduling, and autonomous racing, each measurement or actuator switch incurs real cost, and a fixed discretization frequency is often suboptimal or wasteful. The key open question is scalability—the regret bound for the bounded-K setting grows exponentially in K, and experiments are limited to low-dimensional continuous-control benchmarks (pendulum, RC car, Brax tasks); whether TACOS/OTACOS maintain their sample-efficiency advantages in high-dimensional or partially observable settings remains untested. A further limitation is the assumption of synchronized sensing and control, which excludes the more general asynchronous case.
