## Problem

Scaling down SRAM supply voltage in DNN accelerators saves energy but induces random bit-flips in stored weights, causing catastrophic accuracy loss. Existing mitigations require either hardware modifications with significant overhead or error-aware retraining of the model, which is infeasible in access-limited deployment settings (e.g., cloud APIs, non-configurable embedded hardware). No prior method recovers low-voltage accuracy without retraining the deployed model.

## Input / Output

NeuralFuse takes a standard RGB image (32×32×3 for CIFAR-10/GTSRB/CIFAR-100, 224×224×3 for ImageNet-10) and produces a pixel-wise transformed image clipped to [−1, 1], which is then fed unchanged into a frozen, quantized (8-bit) base model for classification. The base model's weights are perturbed by injecting random bit-flips into their two's-complement integer representation at a specified bit-error rate (0.35%–1.5%), simulating low-voltage SRAM failures. The final output is a class prediction from the unmodified base model.

## Method

NeuralFuse is a plug-and-play input-transformation module F(x) = clip_{[-1,1]}(x + G(x)), where G is a small encoder-decoder DNN (convolution-based, deconvolution-based, or UNet-based, in large/small variants) that learns input-aware perturbations making the data representation robust to weight bit-flips. Training uses a composite loss that simultaneously optimizes cross-entropy on the clean model M₀ and on N randomly perturbed models M_p (each with p% random bit errors injected into 8-bit quantized weights), balanced by a hyperparameter λ. A novel optimizer, EOPM (Expectation Over Perturbed Models), computes the gradient as the average over N perturbed-model losses per iteration (N=10 in practice), analogous to EOT in adversarial training but applied to model perturbations rather than input transformations. For restricted-access scenarios where backpropagation through the target model is disallowed, the generator is trained on a white-box surrogate model and transferred directly to the black-box target, with the paper showing that training at a higher BER (e.g., 1.5%) yields better generalization to lower BERs (e.g., 0.5%).

## Results

On CIFAR-10 at 1% bit-error rate, NeuralFuse recovers perturbed accuracy (PA) by up to 50.3% (VGG16 + ConvL: 35.7% → 86.0%) and up to 49.6% (ResNet18 + DeConvL: 38.9% → 88.5%), while simultaneously saving 19–29% of SRAM dynamic-memory access energy. On GTSRB at 1% BER, the best combination (VGG16 + UNetL) achieves a 63.5% recovery (15.1% → 78.6% PA), the paper's headline 57%+ figure. Energy savings range from 6.6% (VGG11 + ConvL) to 29.9% (ResNet50 + ConvS/UNetS) depending on the base-model/generator pairing. On the more challenging CIFAR-100 (100 classes), recovery is smaller (up to ~19.6% RP) but still consistent across all architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the unprotected base model (no NeuralFuse), whose PA drops dramatically under low-voltage bit errors (e.g., ResNet18 on CIFAR-10 falls from 92.6% CA to 38.9% PA at 1% BER). NeuralFuse consistently recovers 20–50% of the lost accuracy across all five base models and six generator variants, with large generators (ConvL, DeConvL, UNetL) outperforming small ones by 15–30 percentage points in RP. The only explicit method-level comparison is against Universal Input Perturbation (UIP, Moosavi-Dezfooli et al. 2017), a single fixed perturbation applied to all inputs. UIP achieves near-zero or negative recovery (RP = −5.1% to +1.4%) across all base models at 1% BER, whereas NeuralFuse achieves 8.5–50.3% RP, confirming that input-aware (per-sample) transformation is essential for error-resistant representations. The paper also notes that prior error-aware training methods (Stutz et al. 2021, Kim et al. 2018) require full model retraining and are infeasible in access-limited settings, which is the regime NeuralFuse targets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two dominant strategies for handling low-voltage SRAM bit errors in DNN accelerators—hardware-based fault mitigation (e.g., Reagen et al., Srinivasan et al.) and error-aware robust retraining (e.g., Stutz et al., Kim et al.)—by introducing a plug-and-play input-transformation module that requires no modification to the deployed model. Conceptually, the EOPM optimizer adapts the expectation-over-transformation (EOT) framework from adversarial robustness (Athalye et al., 2018) to average losses over a distribution of randomly perturbed models rather than over input transformations. The generator architectures draw on encoder-decoder designs from input-aware backdoor triggers (Nguyen and Tran, 2020) and UNet (Ronneberger et al., 2015), repurposed here for error-resistant data representation rather than attack or segmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NeuralFuse addresses a practically important gap: protecting DNN inference in access-limited deployment scenarios (cloud APIs, non-configurable embedded hardware) where model retraining is infeasible. The demonstrated energy savings (up to 24%) combined with substantial accuracy recovery (up to 57%) make low-voltage operation more viable for edge AI, with direct implications for energy efficiency and carbon footprint. Key limitations include the additional inference latency (1.3–5.2 ms depending on generator size), the restriction to CNN-based image classifiers, and the assumption that NeuralFuse itself runs at nominal voltage. Open questions include whether the approach extends to transformer architectures and other modalities, whether lighter-weight generators can reduce latency overhead, and how performance scales to larger bit-error rates or different SRAM failure distributions.
