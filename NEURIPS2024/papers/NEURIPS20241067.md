## Problem

Deployed ML models face simultaneous covariate and semantic distribution shifts in the wild, yet existing methods are highly specialized in either OOD generalization or OOD detection. This paper asks whether a small budget of human labels, strategically placed within an unlabeled wild mixture, can significantly improve both tasks simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241067/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a labeled ID training set, an unlabeled wild mixture of ID, covariate OOD, and semantic OOD examples (with unknown composition ratios), and a fixed human labeling budget B. The human annotator provides a class label for ID/covariate OOD examples or an 'OOD' label for semantic OOD examples. The output is a jointly trained multi-class classifier (for ID and covariate OOD accuracy) and a binary OOD detector that produces a continuous score and an ID/OOD decision via a threshold set at 95% TPR on ID data.

## Method

AHA identifies a novel 'maximum disambiguation region' where the weighted densities of covariate OOD and semantic OOD examples approximately equalize, making it the most informative region for human labeling. The algorithm operates in two phases: first, it uses a noisy binary search (via an adaptive labeling subroutine from Katz-Samuels et al. 2021) to locate the maximum ambiguity threshold with high probability, spending half the budget adaptively sampling within a shrinking confidence interval; second, it labels equal numbers of examples on both sides of the identified threshold. The labeled examples are then used to jointly train a multi-class classifier (cross-entropy on ID + covariate OOD) and a binary OOD detector (sigmoid loss separating ID from semantic OOD), with the OOD score function (e.g., energy or gradient-based) serving as the ordering statistic for the labeling region.

## Results

On the CIFAR-10 benchmark with a labeling budget of 1000, AHA achieves OOD accuracy of 89.01%, 90.69%, and 90.51% on SVHN, LSUN-C, and Texture semantic OOD datasets respectively, with FPR95 of 0.08%, 0.02%, and 5.63%. Compared to the SOTA wild-data method SCONE, AHA reduces FPR95 by 31.52% on Texture (37.15% → 5.63%) and improves OOD accuracy by 4.95% (85.56% → 90.51%). On the PACS domain generalization benchmark, AHA achieves 92.7% average accuracy, outperforming the best baseline HYPO (89.0%) by 3.7 points. The paper reports that just a few hundred human labels reduce average FPR95 by 15.79% and increase covariate OOD accuracy by 5.05% over non-human-assisted SOTA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AHA dominates all 32 baselines across both OOD generalization and detection metrics. Against the closest SOTA competitor SCONE (which also uses wild data but without human labels), AHA improves OOD accuracy by 4.3–5.1 points and reduces FPR95 by 9.8–31.5 points across SVHN, LSUN-C, and Texture. Against WOODS, AHA gains 36.25 points in OOD accuracy on SVHN (89.01 vs. 52.76) while maintaining comparable FPR. All post-hoc OOD detection methods (MSP, ODIN, Energy, Mahalanobis, ViM, KNN, ASH) are locked at 75.05% OOD accuracy since they share the same underlying classifier, and AHA exceeds this by 14–15.7 points. All OOD generalization methods (ERM, IRM, VREx, EQRM, SharpDRO, Mixup) suffer FPR95 of 5–65%, whereas AHA achieves 0.02–5.63%. On the PACS domain generalization benchmark, AHA reaches 92.7% average accuracy, beating the strongest baseline HYPO (89.0%) by 3.7 points and the next-best VNE (86.9%) by 5.8 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends SCONE (Bai et al., ICML 2023), which established the wild-data mixture formulation for simultaneous OOD generalization and detection, by introducing a human-in-the-loop labeling strategy. The first phase of the algorithm directly adapts a noisy binary search procedure from agnostic pool-based active classification (Katz-Samuels et al., ICML 2021) to identify the maximum ambiguity threshold. Conceptually, the work bridges active learning (uncertainty/diversity sampling) with OOD robustness, departing from prior OOD generalization methods (IRM, VREx, SharpDRO) and OOD detection methods (MSP, Energy, ViM) that each address only one type of distribution shift without leveraging human feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a few hundred strategically placed human labels—far fewer than typical active learning budgets—can substantially close the gap between specialized OOD generalization and detection methods, offering a practical path for deploying models in heterogeneous wild environments. The maximum disambiguation region provides an interpretable, principled criterion for where to spend limited annotation budget. Key limitations include the continued dependence on human annotators (cost, scalability, potential bias) and the assumption that a single OOD score function adequately separates the three data types; whether the approach extends to more complex multi-modal or high-dimensional settings remains open.
