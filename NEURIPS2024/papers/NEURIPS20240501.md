## Problem

LLM agents are increasingly deployed as simulation proxies for humans in social science and role-playing applications, yet the foundational assumption that they behave like humans remains insufficiently validated. This paper tackles the specific question of whether LLM agents can replicate human trust behavior—one of the most elemental behaviors in human interaction—by testing them against established behavioral economics paradigms (Trust Games) and comparing their actions and reasoning against published human study results.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240501/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each experiment feeds a diverse persona prompt (53 personas with name, age, gender, address, job, and background, generated by GPT-4) concatenated with a specific game scenario instruction into an LLM at temperature 1. The agent outputs a monetary decision (amount sent, trust rate, or trust/distrust choice) along with an explicit BDI trace (Beliefs, Desires, Intentions) that articulates the reasoning behind the decision. The behavioral alignment assessment is then computed by comparing these outputs against quantitative benchmarks from human studies (e.g., average amount sent, trust rate curves across probability levels, multi-round dynamics).

## Method

The paper constructs an empirical evaluation framework built on six Trust Game variants (Trust, Dictator, MAP Trust, Risky Dictator, Lottery, and Repeated Trust Games) drawn from behavioral economics, and uses the CAMEL multi-agent framework to instantiate LLM agents with diverse personas. The key analytical tool is the Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) framework, which forces agents to explicitly articulate their reasoning before making a decision, enabling the authors to verify that trust behavior is grounded in rational deliberation rather than random output. The authors introduce the concept of 'behavioral alignment'—defined as analogy between LLMs and humans along three behavioral factors (reciprocity anticipation, risk perception, prosocial preference) and behavioral dynamics over repeated rounds—and systematically test it by matching LLM agent outputs against published human data from Berg et al. (1995), Cox (2004), Bohnet & Zeckhauser (2004), Fetchenhauer & Dunning (2012), and Cochard et al. (2004). Beyond alignment, the method probes intrinsic properties of agent trust through controlled manipulations: varying trustee demographics, specifying trustee identity as agent vs. human, injecting explicit trust/distrust instructions, and switching from direct reasoning to zero-shot Chain-of-Thought.

## Results

GPT-4 agents exhibit high behavioral alignment with humans across all three trust behavioral factors: reciprocity anticipation (GPT-4: $6.9 vs $6.3 in Trust vs Dictator Game, p=0.05; humans: $6.0 vs $3.6, p=0.01), prosocial preference (GPT-4: 72% vs 21% in Lottery People vs Gamble Game; humans: 54% vs 29%), and risk perception (Trust Rate curves most closely tracking human curves). In the Repeated Trust Game, GPT-4 agents display the three canonical human trust dynamics in 87.50%, 87.50%, and 100.00% of 16 groups, compared to only 62.50%, 56.25%, and 43.75% for GPT-3.5. Smaller models (Llama2-13b, Vicuna-13b) fail to show reciprocity awareness or risk-adaptive trust, indicating lower behavioral alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks LLM agent trust against five canonical human studies: Berg et al. (1995) for the Trust Game baseline ($5.97 average sent), Cox (2004) for reciprocity anticipation ($6.0 vs $3.6), Bohnet & Zeckhauser (2004) for risk perception (MAP Trust Game curves), Fetchenhauer & Dunning (2012) for prosocial preference (54% vs 29%), and Cochard et al. (2004) for repeated trust dynamics. GPT-4 matches or exceeds human trust levels in the Trust Game and shows the closest curve alignment in the MAP Trust Game, while GPT-3.5-turbo-0613 sends the lowest amounts among tested models. Vicuna-7b exhibits the highest trust level (exceeding humans), and Llama2-70b/13b show convergent (low-variance) distributions unlike the more divergent human and Vicuna-7b distributions. Critically, GPT-4 is the only model that follows both 'trust' and 'do not trust' manipulation instructions, while most models can only be pushed to decrease trust (e.g., text-davinci-003 drops by $1.26). The CoT reasoning strategy has minimal impact on GPT-4 trust (only +$0.02), suggesting trust behavior is relatively robust to reasoning strategy changes in the strongest model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two converging lines of work: (1) the growing use of LLM agents as simulation tools in social science and role-playing (Gao et al. 2023b, Manning et al. 2024, Ziems et al. 2023), and (2) the study of LLM strategic behavior in game-theoretic settings (Akata et al. 2023, Brookins & DeBacker 2023, Aher et al. 2023). It departs from both by explicitly *validating* the assumption that LLM agents behave like humans rather than taking it for granted, and by introducing 'behavioral alignment' as a new analytical framework that goes beyond value alignment to capture analogy in behavioral factors and dynamics. The BDI framework (Rao et al. 1995, Andreas 2022) is repurposed to make LLM agents' reasoning processes inspectable, and the CAMEL framework (Li et al. 2023a) provides the multi-agent experimental infrastructure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first systematic empirical validation that LLM agents (particularly GPT-4) can simulate human trust behavior at both the action and reasoning level, which is a foundational prerequisite for the many social-science and role-playing applications that currently assume this without evidence. The discovery that agent trust is easier to undermine than to enhance, combined with demographic biases, raises concrete safety concerns for human-agent collaboration and multi-agent systems. Key open questions include whether behavioral alignment extends beyond trust to other social behaviors, whether it holds in more complex and dynamic environments than simplified trust games, and how to mitigate the identified biases and manipulation vulnerabilities in deployed LLM agents.
