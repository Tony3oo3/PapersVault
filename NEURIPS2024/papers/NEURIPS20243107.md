## Problem

Molecular pre-trained models are increasingly shared across organizations to enable collaborative drug discovery without exposing proprietary training data, yet no prior work has examined whether these non-generative, architecturally diverse graph models are vulnerable to training data extraction attacks. Unlike text and image models where generative outputs directly leak training samples, molecular pre-trained models only return representation vectors for queried graphs, and the vast combinatorial space of molecules (~10^60) makes brute-force search infeasible. This paper is the first to demonstrate that black-box query access to molecular pre-trained models carries a significant risk of recovering private training molecules, with an average extraction precision of 49.0%.

## Input / Output

The adversary operates under a strict black-box setting: they submit molecular graphs to the pre-trained model via API and receive only the resulting representation vector, with no knowledge of the model architecture, pre-training task, or internal parameters. An auxiliary molecular dataset (e.g., 20,000 molecules from ChEMBL, PubChem, or ZINC15) is used to train the scoring function and RL agent. The output is a set of K extracted molecular graphs (typically 50–200) ranked by the learned scoring function, evaluated by precision (fraction actually present in the private training set) and Fréchet ChemNet Distance (distributional similarity to the training set).

## Method

The approach combines three components. First, a molecule generation process constructs candidates by starting from ring template structures, iteratively attaching motifs from a bank of 91 common fragments, and forming bonds at chemically valid attachment positions identified via CReM decomposition of the auxiliary dataset. Second, a model-independent scoring function measures whether a generated molecule Ĝ = R ∪_B M likely exists in the training set by computing Sim(f(Ĝ), α·f(R) + (1−α)·f(M)), exploiting the observation that co-occurring components in training data yield representations that are approximately linearly decomposable; this is made learnable via an MLP adapter g_θ and a learned α = h_φ([f(R); f(M); f(Ĝ)]), trained with cross-entropy on the auxiliary dataset. Third, a Molecule Extraction Policy Network trained with Soft Actor-Critic RL navigates the combinatorial search space through a three-stage action at each step (select attachment point on the current graph, select a motif, select attachment point on the motif), using a delayed reward accumulated from the scoring function over the trajectory (weighted by 0.99^i) plus an intermediate reward for valency compliance, with entropy regularization to encourage diverse molecule generation.

## Results

Across nine molecular pre-trained models (GraphCL, SimGRACE, InfoGraph, GraphMAE, AttrMasking, EdgePred, Mole-BERT, ContextPred, Grover), the proposed RL-based extraction method achieves an average precision of 49.0% with only black-box query access. The method outperforms the MLP baseline by an average of 30.9% in precision and 3.97% in FCD. The most vulnerable model is AttrMasking (76% precision at two-step K=200), while Grover is the most resistant yet still yields 68% precision under the same setting. FCD values range from ~16.5 (best) to ~38.5 (worst model, GraphMAE), indicating the extracted molecules closely match the training distribution in chemical and biological properties.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three categories of baselines: chemical property scores (QED, SA, Docking) and a learning-based MLP classifier. QED (Bickerton et al., 2012) performs poorly, achieving only 5–37% precision, as drug-likeness is not a reliable indicator of training data membership. SA (Ertl & Schuffenhauer, 2009) is the strongest chemical property baseline (up to 43% precision at one-step K=50 on GraphCL), reflecting that molecular stability correlates with real dataset presence, but it still trails the proposed method by 7–35 percentage points. Docking scores (Trott & Olson, 2010; FA7, PARP-1, 5-HT1B targets) achieve 18–27% precision and are infeasible for two-step generation due to runtime exceeding three days. The MLP baseline (trained on auxiliary data to predict membership) is the closest competitor, but the proposed RL-based method consistently surpasses it by an average of 30.9% in precision and 3.97% in FCD across all nine pre-trained models, with the largest gap on Grover (69% vs. 29% precision at two-step K=100) and InfoGraph (55% vs. 50% at two-step K=100). Notably, the proposed method is the only approach that scales to two-step generation across all models without exceeding the runtime budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from prior data extraction attacks (e.g., Carlini et al. on LLMs and diffusion models) that exploit the generative nature of text and image models to directly recover training samples via prompting. Here, the target models are non-generative GNNs that only output graph representations, necessitating an entirely different attack paradigm: the authors must generate candidate molecules externally and score them against the model's representations. The RL-based molecule extraction policy network builds on Soft Actor-Critic [15] but repurposes it for a sequential molecular assembly task guided by a novel model-independent scoring function that exploits the linear-combination structure of pre-trained representations. The molecule generation process draws on CReM decomposition and motif-based assembly, but the scoring function design (Eq. 1–2) and its theoretical justification via pre-training task analysis are new contributions not present in prior extraction literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely held assumption in the pharmaceutical and AI-for-science communities: that sharing pre-trained molecular models (rather than raw training data) is a safe alternative for collaborative research. An average extraction precision of 49% under black-box query access is a concrete and concerning result that could influence how organizations structure model-sharing agreements. However, the attack requires access to an auxiliary molecular dataset (e.g., from PubChem or ZINC), which is a non-trivial assumption, and the evaluation is primarily on a single 2M-molecule ZINC15 subset without error bars or statistical significance tests. Open questions include whether the proposed defenses (behavior detection, prediction perturbation) are sufficient, how the attack scales to the full ~10^60 molecular space beyond 1–2 step generation, and whether the scoring function generalizes to 3D molecular representations or multi-modal pre-training.
