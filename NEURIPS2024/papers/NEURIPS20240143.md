## Problem

Explainable ML tasks such as Shapley value feature attribution and Data Shapley valuation require exponential or Monte Carlo computation per data point, making them intractable for large datasets. While amortizing these computations with a learned network is a natural solution, standard supervised training demands ground-truth labels that are themselves prohibitively expensive to generate. The paper asks whether amortized models can be trained effectively with cheap, noisy labels instead of exact ones.

## Input / Output

For feature attribution, the amortized model (e.g., a pretrained ViT-B) takes a raw image (224×224, partitioned into 196 patches of 14×14) or a tabular data point and directly outputs a vector of d attribution scores, one per feature or patch. For data valuation, an FCN or ResNet-18 takes a labeled example z=(x,y) and outputs a scalar score; for distributional valuation, the model also generalizes to unseen examples outside the training set. For datamodels-style data attribution, the input is a (training example, inference example) pair producing a scalar influence score. In all cases, the output replaces what would otherwise require thousands of model queries or retraining runs per example.

## Method

Stochastic amortization trains a neural network via standard MSE regression where the targets are noisy labels produced by a cheap unbiased estimator (e.g., KernelSHAP with 512 samples, TMC with 50 samples, or permutation sampling with a small number of permutations). The key theoretical contribution is a two-sided bound showing that unbiased noise in the labels only adds an irreducible variance floor to the loss, while any bias causes the model to converge to an incorrect function; a further SGD convergence theorem shows that label variance slows optimization but does not change the learned solution. In practice, the amortized model denoises the labels substantially, achieving estimation error comparable to 10–40× more Monte Carlo samples at a fraction of the compute, and generalizes to unseen data points. The approach is task-agnostic: any XML method admitting an unbiased estimator (Shapley values, Banzhaf values, LIME, Data Shapley, Distributional Data Shapley, datamodels) can be accelerated by plugging its estimator in as the noisy oracle.

## Results

For Shapley value feature attribution on ImageNette, amortized predictions trained with just 512 KernelSHAP samples achieve squared error comparable to running KernelSHAP for 10–40K samples per example, and in compute-matched comparisons amortization outperforms per-example estimation across all dataset sizes from 250 to 10K points. For Data Shapley on MiniBooNE with 50 Monte Carlo samples, amortized predictions maintain Pearson correlation >0.9 with ground truth, while raw MC estimates degrade as dataset size grows. For Distributional Data Shapley on CIFAR-10 with only 5 MC samples, amortization achieves 0.81 correlation versus 0.58 for the raw Monte Carlo estimates, with strong generalization to unseen external data points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against per-example KernelSHAP, stochastic amortization achieves lower squared error in compute-matched comparisons (2257 samples for amortization vs. 2440 for per-example) across all dataset sizes from 250 to 10K points on ImageNette, with the gap widening as the dataset grows. Against per-example permutation sampling, amortization shows a similar improvement pattern, confirming the method is not specific to one estimator. Against SGD-Shapley, amortization fails as predicted by theory because SGD-Shapley has non-negligible bias, validating the paper's theoretical requirement for unbiased oracles. Against per-example TMC for Data Shapley on MiniBooNE and Adult Census, amortized predictions are consistently more accurate in both MSE and Pearson correlation, with the advantage growing from 250 to 10K data points. Against per-example Monte Carlo for Distributional Data Shapley on CIFAR-10, amortization nearly doubles the correlation (0.81 vs. 0.58) when using only 5 MC samples. Against FastSHAP [50, 18], the paper reports similar estimation accuracy in compute-matched comparisons, indicating stochastic amortization is competitive with existing amortized Shapley methods while being more general (applicable to any unbiased estimator and to data valuation). Against per-example LIME and Banzhaf value estimators, amortization improves accuracy but is more challenging due to inconsistent attribution scales across inputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the regression-based amortization framework of Amos [2] to the explainable ML setting, where prior amortization approaches (FastSHAP [50,18], CoRTX [14], Schwarzenberg et al. [87]) either use objective-based amortization or require near-exact labels that are intractable to generate for large datasets. The key conceptual departure is showing that any unbiased noisy estimator (e.g., a few hundred Monte Carlo samples from KernelSHAP or TMC) suffices as a training label, supported by a clean two-sided bound relating the noisy and exact regression objectives. Concurrent work by Zhang et al. [110] proposes a similar idea for Shapley values specifically, while Li and Yu [65] do an analogous construction for data valuation but with memory scaling that limits applicability; this paper unifies both tasks under a single framework and provides the general theoretical guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: stochastic amortization yields order-of-magnitude speedups for feature attribution and data valuation, making methods like Data Shapley feasible on datasets of 10K+ points where prior work was limited to <1K. The theoretical result (unbiased labels → correct function, variance only slows optimization) is clean and broadly applicable beyond XML to any amortized computation. Key limitations include that very high noise can prevent effective optimization, and it is difficult to determine a priori how many noisy samples are needed for a target accuracy. Open directions include the trade-off between more noisy labels versus fewer accurate ones, scaling to millions of examples, and extending amortization to datamodels and other attribution methods.
