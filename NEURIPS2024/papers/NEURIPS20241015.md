## Problem

Energy minimization in molecular dynamics is bottlenecked by the disparity between fast modes (strong covalent bonds) and slow modes (weak van der Waals forces), forcing small learning rates that make convergence glacial. Traditional coarse-graining reduces degrees of freedom but introduces costly force-matching and back-mapping steps, and can oversimplify the system by discarding fine-grained detail needed for accurate energy minimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241015/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes particle positions X ∈ R^{n×d} and a pairwise energy potential (e.g., Lennard-Jones, AMBER force field with bond, angle, torsion, vdW, and electrostatic terms) as input. A Hessian backbone is constructed by aggregating Frobenius norms of Hessian blocks over perturbed configurations, yielding a weighted adjacency matrix that defines the graph structure. The output is an optimized configuration X = ρ(Z) in the original fine-grained space, requiring no back-mapping step; for protein folding, this is evaluated via RMSD against PDB structures and energy improvement over baseline gradient descent.

## Method

The core contribution is reparametrizing fine-grained DOF as X = ρ(Z) using an overparametrized neural network, where Z are CG-like latent variables and ρ can be a linear projection onto slow Hessian modes or a GNN. The key theoretical insight is that the Hessian of any invariant pairwise potential is a graph Laplacian, and slow modes of the weighted Laplacian overlap with those of the unweighted (backbone) Laplacian up to O(ε²) corrections, making a single backbone Hessian a robust proxy for slow modes across configurations. In the GNN variant, the backbone Hessian serves directly as the graph adjacency matrix for GCN layers (with self-loops), and a node-wise MLP projects the output to 3D particle positions. Optimization proceeds via Adam on L_CG(Z) = L(ρ(Z)) with no training data, optionally followed by a fine-grained refinement stage where δX is added to the converged ρ(Z₀). This eliminates force-matching and back-mapping entirely while allowing the latent space to be higher-dimensional than the physical DOF, providing paths around energy barriers.

## Results

On four small PDB proteins (Chignolin 5AWL, Trp-Cage 2JOF, Cyclotide 2MGO, Enkephalin 1PLW), the GNN reparametrization consistently outperforms the GD+Adam baseline in wall-clock time (speedup factor δt̂ > 1 for all proteins) and achieves lower final energies (energy improvement factor δÊ > 1) in most cases, with results averaged over 10 runs per protein. In the challenging pure Lennard-Jones synthetic loop (n=1000), GNN is the only method that reliably finds the correct folded configuration, while GD fails and CG reparametrization leaves some nodes disconnected. For the 2JOF protein, GNN at 3–5k optimization steps reaches energy depths comparable to 10k steps of OpenMM, and in some runs achieves slightly lower RMSD to the PDB structure than OpenMM at the same step count.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is gradient descent with the Adam optimizer (LR=10⁻²) applied directly to the fine-grained MD energy. The GNN reparametrization wins on both speed and energy depth across all four tested proteins, with the energy improvement factor (E₀/E_GNN) exceeding 1 in most cases while the speedup factor (t₀/t_GNN) is consistently above 1. Against OpenMM (used as a reference MD engine at 298 K, 2 fs timestep), the GNN reaches energy levels close to OpenMM's 10k-step result in only 3–5k steps, though OpenMM still has an advantage in raw wall-clock time due to its highly optimized C++ implementation. In the synthetic pure-LJ loop experiment, the GNN is the sole method to recover the correct loop layout, outperforming both direct GD (which leaves nodes far from the target) and linear CG reparametrization (which fails to bring all pieces together). In the easier Bond+LJ variant, CG reparametrization yields slightly better final energies than GNN but at a longer runtime, while GNN matches GD-level energies faster.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the GNN-based reparametrization framework from Both et al. (2023) on network layout optimization to molecular dynamics, replacing the graph adjacency with a Hessian-derived backbone. Conceptually, it draws on the Deep Image Priors idea of using overparametrized neural networks as flexible function approximators, but applies it to physical DOF rather than pixel values. The key departure from traditional coarse-graining (Pak & Voth, 2018; Jin et al., 2022) is that instead of strictly reducing degrees of freedom and requiring force-matching and back-mapping, the method reparametrizes fine-grained coordinates as a neural function of slow modes, maintaining continuous access to the full configuration. The theoretical contribution—that pairwise invariant potentials yield Hessians that are graph Laplacians—provides a principled basis for identifying robust slow modes without the laborious procedures of conventional CG.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The elimination of force-matching and back-mapping addresses two of the most practically burdensome steps in traditional coarse-graining, and the data-free nature of the approach (no training data required) broadens applicability to systems where labeled data is unavailable. However, the experimental validation is limited to four small proteins with simplified force fields and no explicit solvent, and OpenMM still achieves lower RMSD in most cases, suggesting the method is best positioned as an initializer or accelerator rather than a replacement for full MD. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger proteins or complex force fields, how it compares quantitatively to ML-based force-matching methods (Majewski et al., 2023), and whether the Hessian backbone construction remains stable over long optimization trajectories where the interaction topology changes significantly.
