## Problem

Despite extensive theoretical work on approximate matrix multiplication (AMM), no comprehensive and impartial empirical comparison exists across algorithmic strategies, operational specifics, and real-world datasets. Prior studies produce contradictory algorithm-selection advice due to task-specific biases, limited workload coverage, and inconsistent implementation standards (e.g., JIT vs. static compilation, manual nested-loop baselines). This paper addresses these gaps with a unified, static-compiled benchmark of twelve AMM algorithms across diverse datasets and downstream tasks.

## Input / Output

The framework takes two FP32 matrices A (M×K) and B (K×N), normalized to [-1, +1], and produces an approximate product C̃ (M×N) governed by a tuning parameter ω that controls the accuracy–latency trade-off. Beyond the product matrix, each algorithm is scored on three metrics: processing latency l (wall-clock time), AMM error ε (Frobenius-normalized relative error), and approximation impact factor ΔE (downstream task error difference between exact MM and AMM). Evaluation spans eight real-world MatrixMarket datasets (207×260 to 4929×10595) plus synthetic workloads, and four downstream tasks (PCA, ML training, ML inference, unitary transformation).

## Method

LibAMM unifies twelve AMM algorithms—three pruning-based (INT8, CRS, CS), five extraction-based (COOFD, BLOCKLRA, FASTJLT, VQ, PQ), and four hybrid (RIP, SMP-PCA, WEIGHTEDCR, TUGOFWAR)—alongside two MM baselines (NLMM, LTMM) into a single C++ codebase compiled statically via LibTorch's C++ API, inheriting AVX-512 FP32 instructions. The evaluation deliberately excludes algorithm-specific optimizations to ensure fair, broadly applicable comparisons, and uses PAPI to trace processor cycles (memory stalls, cache stalls, computing stalls) to isolate operational bottlenecks. Key empirical findings: pruning-based and hybrid AMM outperform extraction-based methods in both latency and downstream-task accuracy; memory access (not computation) is the dominant bottleneck, especially at scale; and dataset attributes such as value skewness and biased non-zero regions critically degrade accuracy, exposing weaknesses in existing error-bound guarantees. The toolkit is released as an open-source PyTorch-ecosystem framework for reproducible AMM research.

## Results

Across 8 real-world datasets (207×260 to 4929×10595), pruning-based AMM (CRS, INT8) achieves the lowest processing latency, with CRS reducing latency by 56.3% vs. the LTMM baseline on ECO (0.10 vs. 0.10 ms) and 99.9% on BUS (1.88 vs. 60.60 ms). Extraction-based methods (COOFD, BLOCKLRA, FASTJLT, VQ, PQ) consistently exhibit higher latency than both LTMM and NLMM baselines, with COOFD reaching 7999.6 ms on BUS. Hybrid AMM (SMP-PCA, RIP) offers a middle ground, with SMP-PCA achieving 3.16 ms on QCD vs. 11.43 ms for LTMM while maintaining ε < 0.001 on most datasets. In downstream tasks, INT8 and CRS reduce processing latency by up to 94% in unitary transformations, while extraction-based methods (COOFD, VQ, PQ) show ΔE up to 1.0 in inference, indicating severe semantic information loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 12 AMM algorithms against two exact MM baselines (NLMM: manual nested-loop, LTMM: LibTorch optimized). Pruning-based CRS and INT8 dominate on latency: CRS achieves 56.3% and 99.9% speedup over LTMM on ECO and BUS respectively, while INT8 is fastest overall (0.01 ms on ECO). However, CRS suffers from high ε (up to 1.0 on BUS) due to its loose error bound, whereas INT8 maintains ε < 0.025 across all datasets. Extraction-based methods (COOFD, BLOCKLRA, FASTJLT, VQ, PQ) consistently lose to both LTMM and NLMM on latency—COOFD is 7999.6 ms on BUS vs. 60.60 ms for LTMM—though they achieve near-zero ε on some datasets (e.g., COOFD ε=0.0000 on BUS). Among extraction-based methods, PQ is the fastest but still 8.8× slower than LTMM on BUS. Hybrid SMP-PCA offers the best practical trade-off, achieving 6.97 ms on BUS (8.6× faster than LTMM) with ε=0.0031, while RIP is slightly faster (7.15 ms) but with higher ε (0.0238). In downstream tasks (Table 7), pruning-based and hybrid AMM outperform extraction-based: INT8 reduces unitary transformation latency by 94% with ΔE=0.397, while COOFD's ΔE reaches 0.68 in inference and VQ/PQ fail entirely (ΔE=1.0), confirming that extraction-based feature extraction misaligns with task semantics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new AMM algorithm but instead provides the first unified empirical benchmark of twelve existing AMM methods (pruning-based, extraction-based, and hybrid) under a consistent static-compilation protocol using LibTorch's C++ API with AVX-512. It departs from prior evaluations that relied on inconsistent baselines (manual nested-loop MM, JIT compilation) and narrow synthetic datasets, which the authors argue led to contradictory recommendations in the literature (e.g., Adelman et al. favoring pruning for training vs. Blalock et al. favoring extraction for inference). The LibAMM toolkit it introduces aggregates these previously scattered implementations into a single PyTorch-ecosystem framework, repurposing existing algorithms rather than introducing new ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with concrete, evidence-based guidance on AMM algorithm selection, revealing that memory access patterns—not computational complexity—are the dominant practical bottleneck, and that dataset attributes (value skewness, NNZ distribution) can invalidate AMM's error guarantees. Key open questions remain: strengthening error bounds under severe data skewness, reducing CPU-GPU data transfer overhead for AMM, and integrating non-quantization AMM strategies into large transformer models. The single-threaded, in-memory focus and limited downstream task coverage (no LLM-scale evaluation) leave significant room for follow-up work, particularly in hardware-software co-design for AMM.
