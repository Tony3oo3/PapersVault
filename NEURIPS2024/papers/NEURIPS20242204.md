## Problem

Existing prompt-robustness studies partition user queries into task-level instructions and case-level inputs, then evaluate only the model's sensitivity to rephrased task-level instructions on task-specific test sets. This setup ignores that real-world queries rarely separate these two components, that the optimal instruction may differ per case, and that the worst-case (lower-bound) performance across semantically equivalent phrasings is the critical reliability metric for deployment. The paper asks: how large is the performance gap across paraphrases of a single query, and can the worst-performing prompt be identified or predicted without a task-specific test set?

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a single natural-language user query (drawn from TinyAlpacaEval) and, for each query, supplies the model with 11 prompts: the original plus 10 semantically equivalent paraphrases generated by GPT-4 and manually verified for fluency and meaning preservation. The model's response to each prompt is scored by GPT-4-turbo as a weighted win-rate against its own reference output (0.5 = parity), and the paper reports the worst, best, average, and standard deviation of these 11 scores per case, then averages across all cases. No labeled test set is required at inference time; the evaluation is purely prompt-variation-based.

## Method

RobustAlpacaEval is constructed by prompting GPT-4 with a carefully designed few-shot instruction to produce 10 diverse paraphrases per TinyAlpacaEval query (mean length-normalized edit distance 0.7234), followed by manual review. The paper then conducts a multi-perspective analysis: (i) model-agnostic, measuring the overlap rate of worst-k prompts across models, Kendall's W concordance of prompt rankings, and IoU of 'sensitive cases' (performance range > threshold); (ii) model-dependent, correlating prompt perplexity and Min-K% Prob with performance, probing hidden states via PCA visualization and a 3-layer MLP reward model trained with a Bradley-Terry pairwise loss, and testing whether the model itself can prefer the better-performing prompt. For improvement, the paper tests self-refinement (model rewrites its own prompt), voting-based generation (averaging token probabilities across all paraphrases), and swarm distillation (LoRA fine-tuning with an unsupervised consistency regularizer that minimizes variance in log-likelihood across paraphrases, selected by a consistency metric C(X,Y)).

## Results

On RobustAlpacaEval, all seven evaluated models exhibit a large gap between worst and best performance across semantically equivalent paraphrases. Llama-2-70B-chat shows the most dramatic range: 9.38% worst vs. 54.86% best win-rate (a 45.48-point spread), with an average of 29.18%. Scaling within the Llama family (7B→13B→70B) raises average performance from 19.52% to 29.18% but does not reduce the standard deviation (13.32→16.25→15.61), indicating that larger models are not more robust. Across all models, the worst performance drops to as low as 4.22% (Mistral-7B-instruct), far below the 0.50 equivalence threshold to the GPT-4 reference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark a single proposed method against competing baselines; instead, it compares models and prompt-improvement strategies. Across models, Llama-2-70B-chat achieves the highest original performance (32.23%) but its worst-case win-rate (9.38%) is only marginally above Mistral-7B-instruct (4.22%) and Gemma-1.1-2b-it (4.42%), showing that scale does not close the robustness gap. Among prompt-improvement methods (Table 4), voting-based generation is the only strategy that reliably lifts worst performance—boosting Llama-2-70B-chat from 9.38% to 31.36% (+21.98)—but at the cost of capping best performance at the same value (−23.50) and increasing compute by several-fold. Self-refinement consistently degrades all metrics (e.g., Llama-2-7B-chat average drops from 19.52% to 9.48%), and swarm distillation slightly improves consistency (std. dev. 15.61→14.83 for 70B) while reducing overall average performance (29.18%→26.44%). Model-agnostic worst-prompt identification fails: the overlap rate of worst-1 prompts across all models is near zero, and Kendall's W across all seven models is only 0.238 (weak agreement), confirming that no universal 'bad prompt' exists.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the prompt-robustness evaluation line (Mizrahi et al., 2024; Sclar et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023) by shifting the unit of analysis from task-level instructions to case-level user queries, and by introducing worst-case performance as the primary metric rather than average performance. It builds on TinyAlpacaEval (Polo et al., 2024) as the base query set and adopts the weighted win-rate evaluation paradigm from AlpacaEval (Li et al., 2023). For the improvement experiments, it adapts the swarm distillation consistency regularization from Zhou et al. (2022b) and tests it alongside voting-based generation and self-refinement, positioning itself against both prompt engineering and prompt consistency literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 70B model's performance can collapse to 9.38% win-rate on a semantically equivalent paraphrase, and that worst prompts are essentially model-specific (near-zero cross-model overlap), challenges the assumption that a single well-crafted prompt or a universal prompt-quality heuristic can guarantee reliable LLM behavior. The negative result that self-refinement degrades performance by 10–13% and that scaling does not improve robustness (standard deviation remains flat or worsens) narrows the practical toolbox for practitioners. Open questions include whether the voting-based mitigation (which works but multiplies inference cost) can be made more efficient, whether training-time interventions beyond swarm distillation can close the worst-case gap, and whether the findings generalize to newer model architectures and longer contexts.
