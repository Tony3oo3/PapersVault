## Problem

As AI-generated content increasingly contaminates training corpora, models trained on their own outputs degrade in a phenomenon called 'model collapse,' yet no analytical theory existed to quantify how test error scales with the number of synthetic-data generations, how it alters classical scaling laws, or what regularization can prevent catastrophic failure. This paper provides the first closed-form characterization of model collapse in high-dimensional regression, bridging the gap between empirical observations and a rigorous theoretical framework.

## Input / Output

The framework takes Gaussian feature vectors x ~ N(0, Σ) with linear ground-truth labels y = x^T w_0 + ε, and iteratively generates fake labels through n generations of OLS fitting on T_0 samples each. The downstream ridge predictor is trained on T samples from the n-th fake distribution but evaluated against the true distribution P_{Σ,w_0,σ²}. The output is an exact test-error formula decomposed into clean error, a bias-increase term ΔBias, and a variance term n·σ_0²·ρ that depends on all hyperparameters (covariance spectrum, regularization λ, noise levels, dataset sizes), plus a corrected optimal regularization exponent ℓ★ for power-law spectra.

## Method

The core technique is a random-matrix-theory (RMT) analysis of the test error for a ridge predictor trained on n-fold iteratively synthesized data. The key decomposition (Theorem 4.3) writes E_test ≃ Bias̃ + Var + n·σ_0²·ρ, where ρ is an explicit trace expression involving the resolvent of the sample covariance and the 'degree of freedom' functions df_m(κ), and Bias̃ ≥ Bias captures the additional bias from the synthetic generation process. In the under-parametrized regime (T_0 ≥ d), ΔBias vanishes and collapse is purely a linear-in-n variance effect; in the over-parametrized regime (T_0 < d) with independent design matrices, the fake labelling vector ŵ_n = P_{n-1}…P_0 w_0 contracts exponentially toward zero, causing catastrophic bias growth even with σ_0 = 0. For power-law covariance spectra (λ_j ∝ j^{-β}), the authors derive modified scaling laws (Theorem 5.1) showing a crossover from the fast noiseless rate T^{-2βr} to a slower rate T^{-(b-a)c}, and identify a corrected optimal regularization exponent ℓ★ = min((b-a)ℓ_crit, β) that replaces the classical ℓ_crit — using the latter leads to diverging test error. The theory is validated on simulated Gaussian data and MNIST via kernel ridge regression and two-layer neural networks.

## Results

The paper derives exact analytical formulas for test error under n-fold iterative retraining on synthetic data. The headline result (Theorem 4.3) shows test error decomposes as E_test ≈ Bias + Var + nσ₀²ρ, where the collapse term grows linearly in the number of generations n. In the over-parametrized regime with independent design matrices (Theorem 4.6), even noiseless synthetic data causes exponential collapse: ΔBias ≈ (1−φ₀⁻ⁿ)||w₀||², driving the model to zero. For power-law spectra (Theorem 5.1), the modified scaling law exhibits a crossover from the fast noiseless rate T^(−2βr) to a slower rate T^((b−a)c), where b = log T₀/log T and a = log n/log T.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is with the classical optimal ridge regularization exponent ℓ_crit = β/(2βr̲+1) from Cui et al. [14], which is minimax-optimal for clean data. The paper shows that applying ℓ_crit in the presence of synthetic data leads to catastrophic failure: the test error diverges as T^−(c+b−a−1) → ∞ whenever b ≥ a+1−c (i.e., when T₀ ≳ T). In contrast, the proposed corrected exponent ℓ★ = (b−a)ℓ_crit (Corollary 5.2) achieves the optimal test error T^−(b−a)c and prevents divergence. This is validated empirically on MNIST with both RBF and polynomial kernels (Figure 4), where solid curves (ℓ_crit) show non-decreasing or diverging test error with sample size T, while broken curves (ℓ★) maintain a decreasing error curve. The comparison thus demonstrates that the standard clean-data regularization strategy is not merely suboptimal but actively harmful under synthetic data contamination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the random matrix theory (RMT) framework for high-dimensional ridge regression (Hastie et al. [22], Richards et al. [41]) to the setting of iteratively synthesized training data, building directly on the scaling-law analysis of Cui et al. [14] for clean-data kernel regression. It departs from prior model-collapse studies (Shumailov et al. [44], Alemohammad et al. [2], Bertrand et al. [7]) by providing closed-form analytical expressions rather than lower bounds or local stability results, and from the concurrent discrete-data analysis of Dohmatob et al. [18] by working in the continuous Gaussian regression setting. The key conceptual departure is showing that the RMT machinery for clean-data bias-variance decomposition must be fundamentally modified when the training labels are themselves outputs of a previous model generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous analytical characterization of model collapse in a continuous regression setting, revealing that the phenomenon is fundamentally a modification of scaling laws rather than a separate failure mode. The practical implication is stark: the minimax-optimal regularization exponent for clean data (ℓ_crit) can cause test error to diverge to infinity when training on synthetic data, necessitating a corrected exponent ℓ★ that depends on the synthetic data generator's sample size. Limitations include the restriction to Gaussian design and linear/kernel regression; the neural network experiments (MNIST) are preliminary and the theory does not directly apply to fully trained non-lazy networks. Open questions include whether these results extend to non-Gaussian data distributions, to the non-lazy training regime, and to the practical LLM setting where the 'fake data generator' is a black-box model rather than a linear regression.
