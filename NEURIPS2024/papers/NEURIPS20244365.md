## Problem

Neural collapse (NC) is a well-studied geometric phenomenon in classification, but it traditionally requires conditions that are all violated in language modelling: the vocabulary far exceeds the embedding dimension, token frequencies are heavily imbalanced, contexts are ambiguous, and models are undertrained. This paper asks whether NC properties nonetheless emerge in causal language models (CLMs) as they scale, and whether their development is causally linked to generalization rather than being a mere byproduct of training.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes trained GPT-Neo-based CLMs (widths 64–1024, depths 1–12, trained 1/3/10 epochs on TinyStories) and extracts top-layer context embeddings h(x_{1:t}) ∈ R^d for every token position in the training set, treating the 29,233-token vocabulary as the class space. From these embeddings it computes per-class means and variances, then derives NC measurements: class-distance normalized variance (CDNV) for NC1, interference and logarithmic kernel distances for (G)NC2, cosine similarity between classifier weights and class means for (U)NC3, and the proportion of validation samples where the linear MAP classifier agrees with the implicit nearest-class-centre classifier for NC4. The final outputs are R² correlations between each NC metric and validation cross-entropy loss across 230 models, plus permutation-test p-values from 21 identically-architected models differing only in data-shuffle seeds.

## Method

The paper trains 230 causal language models on TinyStories across a grid of widths, depths, epochs, and two weight-decay factors (β=0.0005, 0.1), then performs a two-pass embedding collection over the training set to accumulate class means μ_c and sample variances σ_c² for each of the 29,233 token classes. Because the vocabulary vastly exceeds the embedding dimension (C ≫ d+1), the authors replace the infeasible simplex-ETF criterion (NC2) with hyperspherical uniformity (GNC2) measured via pairwise logarithmic kernel distances, and augment the standard CDNV with an extra power-k=2 distance normalization to emphasize confusable class pairs. They further introduce uniform duality (UNC3), measuring the coefficient of variation of class-wise cosine similarities between classifiers and means, as a more informative alternative to raw self-duality. To disentangle NC from scale as a confounder, they train 21 identical two-layer 768-wide models with different shuffle seeds and run a 10⁴-trial permutation test, finding that NC1, equiangularity, GNC2, NC3, and NC4 remain statistically significant predictors of validation loss even at fixed architecture.

## Results

Across 230 causal language models trained on TinyStories, all four NC properties (NC1, NC2/GNC2, UNC3, NC4) develop with increasing model width, depth, and training epochs, and are correlated with improved validation loss. A permutation test on 21 identical 2-layer 768-wide models (varying only data-shuffling seeds) confirms that NC4 classifier agreement (R²=0.49, p=0.0001), GNC2 hyperspherical uniformity (R²=0.49, p=0.0002), NC3 self-duality (R²=0.32, p=0.0063), NC2 equiangularity (R²=0.22, p=0.0317), and NC1 within-class variability collapse (R²=0.19, p=0.0485) are all statistically significant predictors of generalization independent of scale. Notably, UNC3 uniform duality (R²=0.000036, p=0.9784) and NC2 equinormness (R²=0.026, p=0.487) are not significant at the fixed scale, suggesting they are confounded by model capacity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper without direct method-to-method baselines; instead, it compares different NC metrics against each other as predictors of generalization. The strongest and most consistent predictors of validation performance are NC4 classifier agreement and GNC2 hyperspherical uniformity, both achieving R²≈0.49 in the scale-controlled permutation test. NC3 self-duality shows a moderate correlation (R²=0.32) at fixed scale but does not develop consistently with model width in the full scaling experiments, whereas UNC3 (its uniformity variant) improves with width yet fails to predict generalization at fixed scale — the authors term this the 'duality of duality.' Traditional NC2 equiangularity (measured via interference CoV) actually worsens with scale, supporting the use of the relaxed GNC2 hyperspherical uniformity metric when C > d+1. Weight decay β=0.1 consistently produces stronger NC development than β=0.0005 across all properties, affirming the role of regularization in NC emergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural collapse (NC) framework from Papyan et al. [1] and the generalized NC (GNC) formulation of Liu et al. [19] and Jiang et al. [9] to the setting of causal language modeling, where none of the traditional NC prerequisites (few classes, balanced labels, noise-free data, terminal-phase training) are satisfied. While prior GNC works simulated large class spaces by shrinking embedding dimensions, this paper studies realistic next-token prediction over a full 29K-token vocabulary with imbalanced frequencies and ambiguous contexts. It also introduces 'uniform duality' (UNC3) as a more relevant geometric property than the original self-duality (NC3), drawing on the cosine-similarity measurement approach of Kothapalli [5] and the CDNV metric of Galanti et al. [3].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first empirical study of NC in realistic stochastic token prediction, demonstrating that NC properties emerge with scale and correlate with generalization even when all traditional prerequisites are violated. The permutation test showing that NC-generalization correlation persists when fixing architecture and varying only random seeds suggests NC may be a genuine factor of generalization rather than a training artifact. However, the evidence is limited to small GPT-Neo models (≤1024 dim, ≤12 layers) on a single synthetic dataset (TinyStories) with validation CE loss as the sole metric, leaving open whether these findings transfer to frontier-scale LLMs, encoder architectures, or downstream task performance. The paper opens promising directions for using NC metrics as interpretability tools for token-level representations and for designing NC-informed objectives for language modeling.
