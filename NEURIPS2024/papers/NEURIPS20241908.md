## Problem

The 'feature hypothesis' posits that adversarial perturbations, while appearing as random noise, contain class-specific features that explain phenomena like transferability and the counterintuitive success of perturbation learning (training on mislabeled adversarial examples yet generalizing to clean data). However, the only prior theoretical justification [28] relied on stringent assumptions—mutually orthogonal training samples, oracle-based perturbation design, infinite training time, and homogeneous networks—making it inapplicable to realistic settings. This paper provides the first theoretical justification for the feature hypothesis and perturbation learning under any data distribution and standard gradient-based perturbation design.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on two-layer networks with inputs z ∈ R^d and binary labels ±1. Classifier f is trained on correctly labeled clean samples {(x_n, y_n)}, while classifier g is trained either on the raw perturbations {(r_n, y_n^adv)} (Scenario a) or on full adversarial examples {(x_n^adv, y_n^adv)} (Scenario b), where y_n^adv ≠ y_n represents the mislabeled target. The key output is not a single prediction but a prediction agreement guarantee: under three verifiable conditions (two functional margin inequalities and one sign-agreement condition), sgn(f(z; T_f)) = sgn(g(z; T_g)) holds with high probability for any test point z.

## Method

The analysis operates in the lazy training regime for wide two-layer networks with (Leaky-)ReLU activations, where hidden-layer derivatives remain approximately constant during training, reducing the network to a linear model governed by the neural tangent kernel. The core mechanism is a two-step proof: first, Theorem 3.3 shows that each adversarial perturbation r_n is parallel to a weighted sum of all training samples (with weights y_k Φ(x_n, x_k)), demonstrating that a single perturbation encodes information about the entire training set; second, Theorems 3.4–3.5 show that the predictions of g trained on these perturbations match those of f trained on clean data when the functional margins of two kernel-based surrogate functions exceed thresholds and their signs agree. The width requirement m > Õ(d²(T_f + T_g)²) for identity loss is the main assumption, but no constraints are placed on data distribution, perturbation budget, training time, or loss function (beyond differentiability and monotonicity), substantially relaxing prior work.

## Results

The main contributions are theoretical: Theorem 3.3 proves that adversarial perturbations are parallel to the weighted sum of all training samples (with a remainder of Õ(1)), and Theorems 3.4–3.5 prove that under three mild conditions (two functional margin conditions and one agreement condition), the predictions of a classifier trained solely on mislabeled adversarial examples coincide with those of a classifier trained on correctly labeled clean samples. Empirically, the authors validate these theorems on synthetic (mean-shifted and zero-mean Gaussian) and real (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST) datasets, showing that a network width of m≈100 is sufficient for the theoretical guarantees to hold in high-dimensional settings (d=100–10,000). The experiments confirm that larger input dimension d consistently facilitates prediction agreement between the clean and perturbation-trained classifiers, while larger perturbation size ε, sample size N, and training times provide partial but not definitive benefits.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is with the prior theoretical work by Kumano et al. (ICLR 2024) [28], which was the only existing theoretical justification for the feature hypothesis and perturbation learning. This paper achieves equivalent conclusions (perturbations contain class features; classifiers trained on mislabeled adversarial examples can match clean classifiers) under substantially milder assumptions: it removes the requirement for mutually orthogonal training samples (allowing any data distribution), replaces the oracle-based perturbation with a standard single-step gradient-based method, permits arbitrary perturbation budgets and training times, supports a broader class of loss functions, allows network biases, and accommodates both ReLU and Leaky-ReLU activations. The sole additional assumption is a sufficiently wide (but finite) network width m > Õ(d²(T_f+T_g)²) for identity loss, which is the main limitation. In exchange for this width requirement, the paper provides the first theoretical support for perturbation learning under realistic data distributions and practical training settings, a substantial advancement over [28] whose orthogonal-sample assumption is hard to satisfy even for zero-mean Gaussian data when N > d.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical justification of perturbation learning initiated by Kumano et al. (ICLR 2024), which proved that adversarial perturbations contain class-specific features but only under the restrictive assumption of mutually orthogonal training samples and an oracle-based perturbation design. The key methodological departure is switching from the feature learning regime to the lazy training framework (Chizat et al., NeurIPS 2019; Li & Liang, NeurIPS 2018), which allows the authors to drop the orthogonality requirement entirely and work with any data distribution, standard gradient-based perturbations, arbitrary training time, and a broad class of loss functions. The cost of this relaxation is a sufficiently wide (but finite) network width assumption, which is the main limitation relative to prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous theoretical justification of the feature hypothesis and perturbation learning under realistic data distributions, providing interpretable geometric and quantitative conditions (functional margins and sign agreement) under which a classifier trained on mislabeled adversarial examples will predict identically to one trained on clean data. The result that a single perturbation is provably parallel to a weighted sum of all training samples offers a concrete mechanism for why perturbations encode class-specific information. However, the analysis is confined to two-layer networks with a wide-width assumption, leaving open whether these insights extend to the deeper, narrower architectures commonly used in practice, and whether the three sufficient conditions are also necessary.
