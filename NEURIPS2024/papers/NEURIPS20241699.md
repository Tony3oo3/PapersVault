## Problem

Existing backdoor constructions in language models can be elicited by defenders using input-space optimization (e.g., GCG) or activation-space attacks (e.g., latent adversarial training), making pre-deployment detection feasible. No prior work had demonstrated backdoors in transformer models that are truly unelicitable—impossible to trigger or observe without the exact trigger—even under full white-box access. Additionally, prior universal backdoor constructions (Goldwasser et al.) were restricted to Random Fourier Features and had not been extended to the transformer architecture that dominates modern language models.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard autoregressive transformer language models, inserting compiled cryptographic circuits as additional dimensions in the residual stream. At inference time, the model accepts a text sequence; if the sequence contains the specific trigger (a solution to a hardcoded 3-SAT instance or a digital locker key), the model appends a harmful payload to its output. Without the trigger, the model's behavior is unchanged with high probability, and the trigger is computationally infeasible to discover via any polynomial-time search over the input space.

## Method

The paper inserts handcrafted backdoors by compiling cryptographic circuits directly into transformer weights using two tools: Tracr (for the NP-complete 3-SAT verifier design) and a new domain-specific language called Stravinsky (for the SHA-256-based encrypted design, which Tracr cannot handle due to numerical instability at the required sequence lengths). The encrypted backdoor implements a digital locker: f(x) = h(x) ⊕ K where K = h(T) ⊕ B, so the payload B is recovered only when input x equals the trigger T, and any other input yields a pseudorandom string with probability 2^{-256} of matching B. A 128-bit verification suffix prevents partial noise from corrupting outputs on non-trigger inputs. The key insight is that cryptographic hash functions provide unelicitability: no polynomial-time method—whether operating in input space, activation space, or via gradient-based optimization—can find the trigger or extract the payload, as doing so would break the preimage resistance of SHA-256. The authors empirically validate this by showing latent adversarial training fails to elicit the encrypted backdoor in 10^4 steps, while it successfully elicits both the password-locked and NP-complete variants.

## Results

The paper's main empirical results evaluate three backdoor constructions (password-locked, NP-complete, and encrypted) against Latent Adversarial Training (LAT), the state-of-the-art elicitation method. The password-locked backdoor is elicited in just 8 LAT steps when optimising input embeddings. The NP-complete backdoor resists LAT on the first six layers but is elicited by LAT on the last four layers; under noisy circuit conditions, it is elicited ~60% of the time within 10,000 steps. The encrypted backdoor (using a simplified SHA-256 with 2 mixing steps instead of 64) is never elicited across 10,000 LAT steps at any layer, with loss failing to increase at all, confirming its unelicitability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods in the traditional sense; instead, it constructs a hierarchy of three backdoor designs of increasing elicitation difficulty and tests each against LAT (the strongest known elicitation technique) and GCG-style input optimisation. The password-locked backdoor serves as the weakest baseline and is trivially defeated by any gradient-based method, including LAT on the input layer (8 steps). The NP-complete backdoor defeats all input-space gradient attacks (GCG, LAT on early layers) but is vulnerable to activation-space attacks (LAT on the final four layers), since the 3-SAT verification bottleneck can be bypassed by directly perturbing post-verification activations. The encrypted backdoor, the paper's main contribution, defeats all polynomial-time elicitation methods including LAT at every layer, with the cryptographic hash function preventing any useful gradient signal from propagating. Under noisy circuit conditions (Gaussian perturbation of trigger weights), the encrypted backdoor remains fully unelicitable while the NP-complete backdoor becomes partially vulnerable (~60% elicitation rate), demonstrating that the cryptographic construction provides additional robustness beyond the NP-hardness guarantee alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Goldwasser et al.'s (2022) universal backdoor constructions from the Random Fourier Features paradigm to transformer architectures, while introducing a fundamentally new property—unelicitability—that prior backdoor designs lacked. The cryptographic core (digital lockers) draws on Canetti et al. (2016), and the compiled-transformer implementation builds on Tracr (Lindner et al., 2023) and Newhouse's register-based abstraction, but the authors introduce Stravinsky as a new domain-specific language specifically to overcome Tracr's numerical instability when compiling SHA-256 circuits. The key conceptual departure is that prior backdoors (even Goldwasser's) could in principle be elicited via activation-space optimization, whereas the encrypted construction here is provably resistant to all polynomial-time elicitation under standard cryptographic assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fundamentally challenges the assumption that pre-deployment safety evaluations (red-teaming, ELK, formal verification, latent adversarial training) can catch all dangerous model behaviours, by showing that cryptographic structure creates a genuine barrier to gradient-based elicitation. This has direct practical implications for the open-source model ecosystem (e.g., HuggingFace's 250K+ models) where supply-chain backdoor risk is acute. Key open questions include whether full white-box undetectability is achievable, whether unelicitable structures can arise naturally through end-to-end training or data poisoning, and how to design backdoors that are context-aware rather than relying on exact string matching. The authors acknowledge remaining vulnerabilities (weight noise, fine-tuning, heuristic detection, ZK proofs of provenance) that limit the immediate practical threat but highlight the need for more advanced mitigation research.
