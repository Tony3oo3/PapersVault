## Problem

Constraint-based causal discovery methods (PC, FCI) rely on the faithfulness assumption, which is always violated when deterministic relations exist among variables (e.g., BMI = weight/height²), causing spurious conditional independences that lead to incorrect graph structures. While exact score-based methods can handle this under the weaker Sparsest Markov Representation (SMR) assumption, they are computationally intractable for all but the smallest graphs. The paper addresses the gap of providing a scalable, principled framework for causal discovery in the presence of deterministic relations under general functional models, without restricting to specific noise or distributional assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method takes an observational data matrix D ∈ ℝ^{n×d} (or discrete equivalent) as input and outputs a causal graph in the form of a Markov equivalence class (CPDAG). The output is guaranteed to correctly identify the bridge set (edges between deterministic and non-deterministic clusters) and the non-deterministic cluster up to their true MEC, while the deterministic cluster is identified as a set of variables but its internal skeleton and edge directions require stronger functional model assumptions. The method accommodates both continuous and discrete variables, and both linear and nonlinear causal relationships.

## Method

DGES is a three-phase framework that combines the efficiency of greedy search with the correctness of exact search. Phase 1 detects minimal deterministic clusters (MinDCs) by checking, via linear regression or kernel ridge regression in an RKHS, whether each variable can be deterministically represented by a minimal subset of other variables (residual variance ≈ 0). Phase 2 runs a modified GES with two key changes: (a) during edge addition and deletion, 'constant independences' induced by deterministic relations (where the conditioning set determines one of the variables) are ignored and treated as dependencies, preventing spurious edge removal; and (b) the BIC score is adjusted by adding a small constant ξ to the noise variance to avoid numerical instability when the residual variance approaches zero, or the non-parametric generalized score with cross-validated likelihood is used for nonlinear models. Phase 3 performs exact A* search exclusively on the deterministic clusters and their neighboring variables, leveraging the SMR assumption to select the sparsest valid bridge set and eliminate the redundant edges introduced by Phase 2. The key insight is that exact search is only needed on the small DC+neighbor subgraph, making the overall procedure scalable while retaining the faithfulness-free guarantees of exact methods.

## Results

On simulated datasets (Erdős-Rényi DAGs with 6–16 variables and 50–2000 samples), DGES and A* consistently achieve the lowest SHD and highest F1, precision, and recall among all methods, while DGES exhibits substantially more stable runtime growth than A* as the number of variables increases. On the PK-DB pharmacokinetics dataset (32 variables, 4194 samples), DGES with the generalized score correctly detects three MinDCs ({height, weight, BMI}, {k_el, V_d, Clearance}, {k_el, T_half}) and produces more reasonable edges than the BIC-score variant. On the US Census PUMS dataset (5 variables, 3000 samples), DGES outputs a 5-edge graph that correctly identifies the AI–AAI deterministic relation, compared to 6 edges from GES and only 3 edges from PC (which loses the entire bridge set due to faithfulness violation).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DGES outperforms both DPC (Glymour 2007) and GES (Chickering 2002) across all evaluation metrics (SHD, F1, precision, recall) on simulated deterministic datasets, with the gap widening as the number of deterministic variables increases. Compared to the exact A* method (Yuan 2013), DGES achieves comparable accuracy but with much more stable runtime scaling: A* runtime surges rapidly with variable count, while DGES grows gradually because exact search is restricted to the small deterministic cluster and its neighbors. On the PUMS real-world dataset, PC (Spirtes 1991) fails catastrophically—its Fisher-Z test removes all bridge-set edges due to faithfulness violation, yielding only 3 edges versus DGES's 5. GRaSP (Lam 2022), a greedy relaxation of the sparsest permutation algorithm, performs slightly better than standard GES on SHD and F1 but at higher runtime, and still trails DGES and A* in the deterministic setting. Overall, DGES matches A* in accuracy while being significantly more scalable, and clearly dominates the constraint-based baselines (DPC, PC) in the presence of deterministic relations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Greedy Equivalent Search (GES) framework of Chickering (2002) and the exact A* search of Yuan & Malone (2013), combining them into a three-phase pipeline (DGES) that explicitly handles deterministic relations. It departs from constraint-based methods (PC, FCI, and Glymour's DPC) that fail under determinism due to faithfulness violation, and from prior deterministic-relation work (Daniusis et al., Janzing et al., Luo, Mabrouk et al.) that lacked identifiability guarantees or assumed specific functional forms. The theoretical foundation draws on the SMR assumption (Raskutti & Uhler 2018) and the generalized score (Huang et al. 2018), extending the result of Ng et al. (2021) that exact score-based search with BIC recovers the true MEC under SMR to the nonlinear setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Deterministic relations are ubiquitous in real-world data (BMI, pharmacokinetic parameters, adjusted census figures), yet most causal discovery tools silently fail on them. DGES provides a practical, scalable compromise: greedy search globally for efficiency, exact search only on the small deterministic cluster and its neighbors for correctness, with a clear identifiability theorem (partial identifiability of the bridge set and non-deterministic part under SMR + non-deterministic faithfulness). Key open questions remain: the method cannot identify skeleton or edge directions within a deterministic cluster without strong functional-form assumptions, and runtime grows with the number of overlapping MinDCs, limiting scalability to very large deterministic subgraphs.
