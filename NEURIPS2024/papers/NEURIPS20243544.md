## Problem

Existing interventional causal representation learning (CRL) methods assume single-node interventions, where exactly one latent variable is intervened per environment. This assumption fails in realistic settings such as genomics (off-target gene effects) and robotics (multi-variable actuation), where the subset of intervened nodes is fully unknown. The paper establishes the first identifiability results for general latent causal models under unknown multi-node (UMN) stochastic interventions with linear transformations, matching the best-known guarantees for the more restrictive single-node setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes observed data samples X from an observational environment and M interventional environments {E^m}, where the intervention targets I^m ⊆ [n] are unknown. The intervention signature matrix D_int must be full row rank (n linearly independent intervention vectors), and the linear transformation G ∈ R^{d×n} is full rank with d ≥ n. The output is the latent causal DAG (or its transitive closure under soft interventions) and the latent variables Z, recovered up to permutation and diagonal scaling (hard interventions) or up to a linear function of ancestors (soft interventions). Score functions are estimated from the data, with the algorithm being agnostic to the specific score estimator used.

## Method

The UMNI-CRL algorithm is a four-stage score-based procedure that exploits the structural properties of score differences across interventional environments. The key insight is that score differences under UMN interventions can be linearly aggregated to reconstruct score differences for any hypothetical intervention set, via the decomposition ΔS_X = [G†]^T · Λ · D, where Λ encodes latent score differences and D is the basis intervention matrix. Stage 1 selects n linearly independent score differences as a basis; Stage 2 iteratively searches over integer mixing vectors w in a bounded set to find 1-dimensional projections, achieving identifiability up to an unknown causal order; Stage 3 refines the mixing matrix to enforce ancestor-descendant sparsity, yielding identifiability up to ancestors; Stage 4 (hard interventions only) applies an unmixing step using covariance and conditional independence tests to achieve perfect latent variable and DAG recovery. The search space is governed by κ, the maximum determinant of (n−1)×(n−1) 0-1 matrices, giving worst-case O((2κ)^n) complexity per stage.

## Results

On linear Gaussian causal models with Erdős-Rényi graphs (density 0.5), UMNI-CRL achieves mean correlation coefficients (MCC) above 0.87 across all settings (n=4–8, d=10–50), with MCC ≥ 0.93 for n ≤ 6. The structural Hamming distance (SHD) between the recovered graph and the ground truth (or its transitive closure for soft interventions) ranges from 0.66 (n=4, d=50, hard) to 9.70 (n=8, d=10, hard), which is reasonable given the expected edge count of n(n−1)/4. The ratio of incorrect mixing entries (ℓ_hard, ℓ_soft) stays below 0.28 in all cases, confirming that latent variable recovery is close to the theoretical identifiability guarantees.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run direct algorithmic baselines; instead, it positions its theoretical guarantees against two prior multi-node intervention results (Table 1). Compared to [14] (Jin & Syrgkanis), which requires a linear non-Gaussian latent model and |pa(i)|+1 distinct interventional mechanisms per node to achieve identifiability only up to surrounding variables, UMNI-CRL handles general (nonparametric) latent models with a single interventional mechanism per node and achieves identifiability up to ancestors (soft) or perfect identifiability (hard). Compared to [16] (Bing et al.), which achieves perfect identifiability for general latent models but requires strongly separated multi-node do-interventions (2⌈log₂n⌉ environments) and non-stochastic interventions, UMNI-CRL uses only n+1 stochastic hard interventions under the milder Assumption 1 (full row rank of the intervention signature matrix). The paper further shows that identifiability is impossible under strongly separating sets of UMN stochastic hard interventions (Appendix A.6), highlighting that the stochastic setting fundamentally differs from the do-intervention setting of [16]. Empirically, the paper notes that n=8 is the largest latent graph size tested in closely related single-node intervention studies [4, 7, 8, 9], suggesting the method scales to problems of comparable or greater complexity than prior work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the score-based CRL framework of Varıcı et al. [5, 7], which established identifiability under single-node (SN) interventions, to the substantially more general unknown multi-node (UMN) intervention setting. The key conceptual departure is that UMN interventions allow multiple concurrent causal mechanism changes and unknown intervention targets, rendering the sparsity-based arguments central to prior SN methods inapplicable. The paper also differentiates from [14] (which requires linear non-Gaussian models and multiple mechanisms per node) and [16] (which uses strongly separated do-interventions), by providing identifiability under stochastic soft/hard interventions with general latent models and only n+1 environments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to establish that UMN stochastic interventions achieve the same identifiability guarantees as the best-known SN results (perfect ID for hard, up-to-ancestors for soft), removing a restrictive assumption that limited CRL's applicability to domains like genomics and robotics where interventions inherently affect multiple nodes. The main limitation is the linear transformation assumption; extending to general transformations with UMN interventions remains open. Additionally, the paper shows that strongly separating intervention sets (sufficient for do-interventions in [16]) actually render identifiability impossible for stochastic hard interventions, an important negative result that clarifies the boundary of what intervention structures can support.
