## Problem

Existing federated learning methods for semantic segmentation rely on transferring model weights or gradients between clients and the server, making them vulnerable to gradient inversion and model-architecture-based data reconstruction attacks that can recreate private training data. No prior FL segmentation approach avoids both gradient transfer and model information sharing, leaving a gap in truly privacy-preserving collaborative segmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243583/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each client processes RGB images (road-view scenes at 256×512 for Cityscapes/CAMVID, or medical images at 256×256 for ISIC/PolypGen) through a lightweight two-layer convolutional encoder that outputs 64-channel feature maps. These encoded features are the only information sent to the server, which runs the full segmentation decoder (DeepLabv3, UNext, or SegFormer starting from its second layer) to produce pixel-level segmentation masks of shape H×W×N_c. No raw images, gradients, or model weights cross the client-server boundary.

## Method

BlackFed formulates federated segmentation as a split-network problem where the client owns a 2-layer CNN encoder and the server owns the segmentation decoder, eliminating the need for the server to know the client architecture. Client weights are updated via zero-order optimization (SPSA-GC), which perturbs weights and approximates gradients from loss changes without backpropagation, while server weights are updated via standard first-order optimization (Adam-W) with full backpropagation. The two are trained in an alternating fashion—fix the server, update the client with ZOO; fix the client, update the server with FOO—across a round-robin schedule over all clients. To counter catastrophic forgetting in the shared server, BlackFed v2 stores a per-client snapshot of server weights in a hashmap during training and retrieves the appropriate snapshot at inference time, so each client is evaluated against the server state best adapted to its data distribution.

## Results

On CAMVID, BlackFed v2 achieves OOD mIoU of 0.72, 0.66, 0.70, and 0.72 across the four centers, substantially outperforming individual training (0.46–0.65 OOD) while maintaining competitive local mIoU (0.70–0.81). On Cityscapes (18 centers), BlackFed v2 reaches an average OOD mIoU of 0.74 versus 0.44 for individual training, approaching the white-box upper bound of 0.75. On ISIC, BlackFed v2 achieves OOD mIoU of 0.80, surpassing individual training (0.76–0.79) and the white-box baseline (0.66–0.70). All BlackFed results are statistically significant with p < 0.001.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BlackFed v2 outperforms FedPer [3] on OOD mIoU across all CAMVID centers (e.g., 0.72 vs. 0.57 on C1, 0.72 vs. 0.66 on C4) and on Cityscapes average (0.74 vs. 0.72), while also matching or exceeding FedPer's local performance. Against FedAvg [34] and FedSeg [36], direct OOD comparison is not possible since these methods train a single global model without per-client evaluation; however, BlackFed v2's local mIoU is competitive (e.g., 0.70–0.81 on CAMVID vs. 0.64–0.84 for FedAvg/FedSeg). Most notably, BlackFed v2 achieves OOD performance on par with the white-box upper bound (0.74 vs. 0.75 on Cityscapes; 0.70–0.72 vs. 0.68–0.73 on CAMVID) despite not sharing gradients or model architecture, demonstrating that the black-box constraint incurs negligible performance cost. On ISIC, BlackFed v2 (0.80 OOD) surpasses the white-box baseline (0.66–0.70 OOD), suggesting the split-network formulation with ZOO can even exceed full-gradient training in some settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the split-learning paradigm (Gupta & Raskar 2018; Vepakomma et al. 2018), which partitions a network into client and server segments to avoid full model sharing, and on the zero-order optimization technique SPSA-GC (BlackVIP, Oh et al. 2023), originally designed for black-box adaptation of pretrained foundation models. It departs from both standard federated averaging (FedAvg), which requires exchanging full model weights, and conventional split-learning, which still transmits gradients from server to client—two channels that recent work has shown are vulnerable to data-reconstruction attacks. By replacing client-side backpropagation with SPSA-GC and retaining first-order optimization only on the server, BlackFed eliminates both attack surfaces simultaneously, a combination not previously explored for segmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in offering a privacy-preserving alternative for multi-institutional segmentation (especially medical imaging) where gradient or weight leakage could expose patient data. The finding that a fully black-box pipeline can approach white-box split-learning performance on OOD metrics is encouraging, though the gap is small and the white-box baseline itself is constrained to the same split architecture. Open questions include whether the shared intermediate features (masks) are themselves vulnerable to generative-model-based reconstruction attacks, how the hashmap-based anti-forgetting strategy scales to many clients with highly heterogeneous distributions, and whether the ZOO client updates remain tractable as model complexity grows. The approach also offloads most computation to the server, which may be impractical in settings where the server is resource-constrained.
