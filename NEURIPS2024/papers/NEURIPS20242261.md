## Problem

Standard RLHF finetunes language models on pairwise preference data that is inherently ambiguous because the user's context (expertise, goals, location) is left unspecified, leading to only ~65% inter-annotator agreement. While production systems use system prompts to condition behavior, there has been little research on whether preference models actually attend to added context, and no open-source datasets where preference is strongly determined by context. This paper asks whether decomposing preference into an explicit context plus a context-specific judgment can reduce reward modeling error, and whether current models can reliably evaluate context-specific preference.

## Input / Output

The method takes a prompt, a context string (a short criteria sentence, a scenario description, or a user profile), and two completions, and outputs either a binary preference judgment or an independent scalar reward score per completion. The RPR datasets are structured as paired 5-tuples (prompt, response A, response B, criteria/scenario X, criteria/scenario Y) where preference between A and B is fully ambiguous without context and reverses between the two contexts. For the 'llm-as-a-judge' models, scoring is done via a single forward pass extracting the expected value of score-token logits (1–7 scale) rather than full chain-of-thought generation.

## Method

The paper introduces a context decomposition upper bound (Equation 3–4) showing that absolute reward modeling error is bounded by a context-weighted prediction error plus a preference-weighted inference error, formalizing the claim that accurate context-specific prediction reduces preference modeling to a context inference problem. To test and improve context-specific prediction, the authors construct the RPR (Reasonable Preference Reversal) datasets—over 20,000 paired samples generated by GPT-4 Turbo and filtered through multi-step self-critique—where each prompt has two completions whose preference reverses under two different criteria or scenarios. They finetune Mistral RM (7B) with LoRA (r=16, lr=1e-5, 1 epoch) on ~34,000 samples from RPR Criteria, RPR Scenarios, and Ultrafeedback to produce Mistral CARM. Experiments show that while all tested models benefit from added context, they frequently ignore adversarial or nonsensical criteria; Mistral CARM achieves 98.5% accuracy on RPR Criteria (vs. 86.7% for the base model) and outperforms GPT-4 Turbo and Llama 3 70B on context-specific benchmarks. The paper further demonstrates that a single persistent user profile inferred from as few as 16–32 preference samples can substantially improve preference prediction across diverse prompts.

## Results

On the context-specific RPR Criteria test set, the finetuned Mistral CARM (7B) achieves 0.985 agreement with gold labels, substantially outperforming GPT-4 Turbo (0.901) and Llama 3 Instruct 70B (0.925). On RPR Scenarios, Mistral CARM reaches 0.962, again exceeding GPT-4 Turbo (0.748) and Llama 3 Instruct 70B (0.811). On the context-augmented Chatbot Arena dataset (Table 4), Mistral CARM achieves 0.909 with oracle context (CTX*), outperforming all other models including Llama 3 Instruct 70B (0.892) and GPT-4 Turbo (0.858). Without context, all models perform near chance (~0.50–0.52), confirming that the RPR datasets are genuinely ambiguous without contextual information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mistral CARM (7B) outperforms all baselines on the two RPR datasets where context is essential to resolve preference: it achieves 0.985 on RPR Criteria CTX vs. GPT-4 Turbo (0.901), Llama 3 Instruct 70B (0.925), and its own base model Mistral RM (0.867), and 0.962 on RPR Scenarios CTX vs. GPT-4 Turbo (0.748) and Llama 3 Instruct 70B (0.811). On Preference Bench, CARM (0.919) is slightly behind Prometheus-2 (0.951, which was finetuned on the same distribution) and Llama 3 Instruct 70B (0.929), but still exceeds GPT-4 Turbo (0.860) and UltraRM (0.910). On Multifaceted Bench, CARM leads all models at 0.787, ahead of Mistral RM (0.679), Llama 3 Instruct 70B (0.687), and GPT-4 Turbo (0.640). On the context-augmented general preference datasets (Table 4), CARM achieves the best CTX* score on Chatbot Arena (0.909 vs. 0.892 for Llama 3 Instruct 70B and 0.858 for GPT-4 Turbo) and the best CTX score on HHH (0.919) and Reward Bench (0.871). The key takeaway is that a 7B model finetuned on ~34K RPR samples can match or exceed 70B models and GPT-4 Turbo on context-specific preference evaluation, while all models collapse to near-chance without context on the RPR datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard Bradley-Terry preference modeling framework by decomposing preference into a context inference step and a context-specific prediction step, formalized via a context decomposition upper bound on reward modeling error. It builds architecturally on Mistral RM (Xiong et al., 2024) and connects conceptually to the pluralistic alignment literature (Sorensen et al., 2024; Siththaranjan et al., 2023), which identifies that standard RLHF implicitly aggregates diverse hidden contexts via the Borda rule. The key departure is making context explicit rather than leaving it implicit in a distributional model, and contributing the first datasets (RPR) specifically designed so that preference is determined solely by context, enabling clean evaluation of context sensitivity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT-4 Turbo and Llama 3 70B frequently ignore adversarial or strongly-phrased context (Table 3) is a practical concern for deployed systems that rely on system prompts and user profiles to steer behavior. The RPR datasets and the demonstration that a 7B finetuned model can outperform much larger models on context-specific preference tasks provide a concrete path toward more reliable context-aware alignment. However, the datasets are synthetic, limited to single-turn English conversations, and the context inference component (inferring the right context from a prompt) remains largely unexplored, leaving the full two-step pipeline incomplete. The paper also highlights the need for human-sourced context-annotated preference data to validate these findings in realistic settings.
