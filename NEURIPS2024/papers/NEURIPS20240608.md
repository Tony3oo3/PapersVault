## Problem

Existing Concept-Based Models (CBMs) use task predictors that are either black-box neural networks, non-differentiable decision trees, or overly restrictive linear layers, none of which allow global interpretability or formal verification of the decision-making process prior to deployment. This prevents domain experts from inspecting how concepts are composed into predictions, verifying safety properties, or intervening on the prediction logic itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240608/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CMR takes RGB images (processed via CNNs or pretrained ResNet18 embeddings) or text reviews (processed via BERT embeddings) and produces a binary task prediction along with a fully transparent rulebook of conjunctive logic rules. Each rule assigns every concept one of three discrete roles—positive, negative, or irrelevant—making the entire decision logic a propositional formula over human-interpretable concepts. At inference time, the rulebook is collapsed to deterministic rules (most-likely role per concept), and the final prediction is the symbolic evaluation of the selected rule on hard (thresholded at 50%) concept predictions.

## Method

CMR decomposes task prediction into a neural rule selector and a symbolic rule evaluator. The rule selector is an attention mechanism over a differentiable memory (the rulebook) of n_R learnable rule embeddings; a neural network produces mixing weights over the embeddings, and each embedding is decoded by a small network into a logic rule assigning P/N/I roles to all n_C concepts. The task predictor is a degenerate deterministic distribution that symbolically evaluates the selected rule as a conjunction over concepts (Equation 3). Training maximizes a tractable O(n_C·n_R) log-likelihood with an additional regularization term that encourages selected rules to act as prototypes of concept activations for positive instances. A key theoretical result proves CMR is a universal binary classifier when n_R ≥ 3, and the global prediction formula (a disjunction over all rules) can be converted to a propositional formula for automatic verification via model checking.

## Results

CMR achieves task accuracy of 97.25% on MNIST+, 94.65% on MNIST+* (incomplete concepts), 99.12% on C-MNIST, 63.17% on CelebA, 60.07% AUC on CUB, and 85.14% on CEBaB. These results are competitive with or superior to all concept-based baselines and approach black-box accuracy (83.26%, 83.26%, 99.19%, 65.33%, 64.07%, 88.67% respectively) while maintaining full global interpretability. Notably, on the incomplete-concept setting MNIST+*, CMR (94.65%) substantially outperforms bottleneck-based competitors like CBM+MLP (72.51%) and CBM+DT (77.63%), demonstrating robustness to missing concepts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMR outperforms CEM [27] on MNIST+ (97.25 vs. 92.44), MNIST+* (94.65 vs. 92.94), CUB (60.07 vs. 57.03 AUC), and CEBaB (85.14 vs. 83.30), while CEM slightly edges out CMR on CelebA (65.44 vs. 63.17). CMR dominates DCR [11] across all six datasets, with the largest gaps on CelebA (63.17 vs. 35.65) and CEBaB (85.14 vs. 67.30). Against the standard CBM+MLP baseline [4], CMR matches or exceeds accuracy on MNIST+ (97.25 vs. 97.41) and C-MNIST (99.12 vs. 99.42), and dramatically outperforms it on the incomplete-concept MNIST+* setting (94.65 vs. 72.51), where the concept bottleneck severely limits MLP-based predictors. On CUB, CMR (60.07 AUC) narrows the gap to the black-box ResNet (64.07) compared to all other CBMs, and on CEBaB it reaches 85.14% versus the black box's 88.67%, a gap of only 3.5 points. Overall, CMR achieves near-black-box accuracy while being the only method offering global interpretability, rule-level intervention, and pre-deployment formal verification.

## Relation to Prior Work

CMR extends the Concept Bottleneck Model architecture (Koh et al., 2020) by replacing the opaque task predictor with a differentiable memory of learnable logic rules selected via an attention mechanism (Weston et al., 2014), followed by symbolic evaluation. It departs from CEM (Espinosa Zarlenga et al., 2022), which closes the accuracy gap using vectorial concept embeddings at the cost of interpretability, and from DCR (Barbiero et al., 2023), which predicts a fuzzy logic rule per example rather than maintaining a globally inspectable rulebook. The key conceptual departure is that CMR's rules are learned jointly in a persistent memory, enabling global interpretability, rule interventions, and formal verification—capabilities absent in all prior CBM variants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CMR introduces pre-deployment formal verification of task-prediction properties (e.g., 'never predict apple when blue is active') via model checking, a capability no prior CBM offers, which is critical for safety-sensitive deployments. However, the paper itself notes that if the selector degenerates into a proxy for y (as can happen with the three universal rules from Theorem 4.1), interpretability is compromised, and the verification examples tested are simple propositional properties on small concept sets. Open questions include whether the approach scales to multi-class tasks, whether negative-reasoning explanations can be incorporated, and whether verification remains tractable and meaningful in realistic safety-critical domains with hundreds of concepts.
