## Problem

Aligning language models to follow behavioral principles (constitutions) traditionally requires human preference labels, curated SFT examples, or in-context demonstrations, making it resource-intensive and technically challenging. The paper asks whether a pretrained base model can learn to follow principles without any of these, by exploiting the hypothesis that base models already contain a weak statistical connection between natural-language principles and the corresponding behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241960/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained base LM, a dataset of queries (dialogue prompts from HH-RLHF or Reddit posts from TL;DR), and a set of constitutions generated by a separate 'principle writer' LM that produces principles paired with their antitheses (e.g., helpful/harmless vs. not helpful/not harmless). At inference, the finetuned model receives a query conditioned on a single constitution and produces a text response that adheres to those principles. During training, for each query the model generates one response per constitution in a batch, and the loss is computed over the resulting N_c × N_c matrix of log-probabilities (typically 2×2), with constitutions and queries masked so gradients flow only through the response tokens.

## Method

SAMI is an iterative algorithm (a simplified Expert Iteration) that maximizes a two-sided InfoNCE lower bound on the conditional mutual information I(Y;C|X) between self-generated responses Y and constitutions C given queries X. At each iteration, responses are sampled from the previous iteration's model under different constitutions, then the base model scores each response under every constitution in the batch; row-wise and column-wise log-sum-exp normalization yields logits that are trained against an identity matrix via cross-entropy (analogous to CLIP's contrastive loss). The key distinction from standard contrastive learning is that the model being optimized simultaneously defines the sample distribution and the scoring function, creating a self-referential training loop. Regularization against over-optimization (which produces gibberish) is achieved by using only a small number of gradient steps in early iterations, progressively increasing the number of batches per iteration. The approach requires no preference labels, no reward model, no SFT examples, and no human oversight—only a principle-writer LM to generate constitutions and a query dataset.

## Results

On HH-RLHF dialogue (mistral-7b), SAMI achieves length-corrected win rates of 66% (helpful) and 77% (harmless) against the base model, and 54% (helpful) and 57% (harmless) against mistral-7b-instruct after three iterations. On TL;DR summarization, SAMI-trained mistral-7b reaches 71% win rate against its base model and 47% against mistral-7b-instruct, while SAMI-trained mixtral-8x7b reaches 62% against its base model and 65% against mistral-7b-instruct. On diverse summarization principles with llama3-70b, SAMI achieves 60% (68% with CoT) win rate on in-distribution principles and 63% (67% with CoT) on held-out principles against the base model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAMI consistently outperforms the base model across all experiments: 66–77% win rate on HH-RLHF dialogue (mistral-7b), 62–71% on TL;DR summarization (mistral-7b and mixtral-8x7b), and 60–68% on diverse principles (llama3-70b). Against the instruction-tuned baseline mistral-7b-instruct, SAMI-trained mistral-7b surpasses chance on dialogue (54–57%) but remains below 50% on summarization (44–47%), indicating that instruction tuning still has an edge for summarization on smaller models. However, SAMI-trained mixtral-8x7b clearly beats mistral-7b-instruct on summarization with 65–68% win rates, demonstrating that a strong base model aligned via SAMI can outperform a weaker instruction-tuned model. On llama3-70b with diverse principles, SAMI generalizes to held-out principles (63–67%) at a level comparable to in-distribution principles (60–68%), showing that the learned alignment transfers to unseen behavioral norms.

## Relation to Prior Work

SAMI sits at the intersection of Constitutional AI (which uses natural-language principles but requires RLAIF/reward modeling), contrastive mutual-information estimation (InfoNCE, CLIP-style two-sided cross-entropy), and expert iteration (STaR-style self-improvement loops). It departs from prior alignment work by eliminating all three common dependencies—preference labels, curated SFT/in-context examples, and a separate reward-modeling stage—replacing them with a self-supervised two-sided InfoNCE objective over the conditional mutual information I(Y;C|X). The weak-to-strong principle-writing experiment also extends the weak-to-strong generalization line [7] into the alignment setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAMI provides a principled, label-free objective for teaching a base model to follow constitutions, and the generalization to held-out principles (67% win rate on llama3-70b) suggests the learned skill is not merely memorization of specific principle-response pairings. Key limitations constrain the impact: evaluation is restricted to single-turn dialogue and summarization, the margin over the instruct baseline is thin (54–57%), the method requires careful regularization to avoid degenerate outputs, and it presupposes a weak initial statistical link between principles and behavior. Open questions include whether SAMI extends to multi-turn interaction, diverse task suites, and whether the mutual-information framing can be combined with length penalties or KL regularization to address the known length bias.
