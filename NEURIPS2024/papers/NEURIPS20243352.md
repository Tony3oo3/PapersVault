## Problem

State-of-the-art no-reference speech quality metrics trained with L2 loss fail to generalise to unseen degradations and out-of-domain test sets. The root cause is that L2 minimisation does not induce an ordered representation with respect to the continuous MOS target in the feature space, producing fragmented embeddings that cluster by degradation type rather than quality ordering. This paper demonstrates the generalisation gap systematically and proposes a contrastive regression loss that learns quality-ordered representations.

## Input / Output

In no-reference (NR) mode, the model takes a single degraded speech waveform (48 kHz for telephone-domain models, 16 kHz for synthesis-domain models; trimmed to 4 s during training, full length at test time) and outputs an interpretable MOS score via a linear head on frozen encoder representations. In non-matching-reference (NMR) mode, it additionally takes an unpaired clean speech sample and outputs the Euclidean distance between the two 256-dimensional embeddings, which serves as a quality ranking signal usable as a differentiable perceptual loss. The encoder is a finetuned wav2vec 2.0 transformer (12-layer BASE or 4-layer w2vlight) whose time-averaged output is projected through a ReLU + linear layer to the 256-d embedding space.

## Method

SCOREQ replaces the standard L2 regression loss with a batch-all triplet loss adapted for continuous targets. For every ordered triplet (i, j, k) in a training batch of size N, a 3D boolean mask M(i,j,k) is computed on-the-fly: it is 1 when the MOS distance |y_i − y_j| < |y_i − y_k| (i.e., sample j is a valid positive relative to anchor i and negative k), and 0 otherwise. The loss sums the standard triplet margin term over all valid triplets, effectively contrasting every possible combination in the batch in a relative manner that mirrors human quality judgement. An adaptive-margin variant replaces the fixed margin m with the normalised MOS distance difference (|y_i−y_j|−|y_i−y_k|)/(N−1), allowing training to continue until the embedding gap exceeds the label-derived threshold. Training proceeds in two stages: (1) finetune the wav2vec 2.0 transformer and projection head with the SCOREQ loss (batch size 128, Adam, early stopping on Spearman correlation in NMR mode), then (2) freeze the encoder and train a linear MOS head with L2 loss for NR mode. The key insight is that by learning an ordered quality manifold rather than regressing a scalar, the encoder representations become robust to distribution shifts and unseen degradation types.

## Results

NR-SCOREQ (trained on NISQA TRAIN SIM) achieves the best or near-best Pearson Correlation (PC) across 11 test sets spanning three speech domains. On out-of-domain (ODM) sets, the gains are most pronounced: on TENCENT-Rev (real-world reverberation, Chinese), NR-SCOREQ reaches 0.79 PC versus 0.40 (NISQA), 0.36 (NR-PESQ), 0.32 (NR-SI SDR), and 0.36 (NORESQA-MOS). On VoiceMOS Test 1 (speech synthesis), NR-SCOREQ scores 0.86 PC, outperforming NISQA (0.54) and NR-PESQ (0.71). Bootstrap statistical tests confirm the SCOREQ improvement over L2 loss is significant (p < 0.05) on 7 of 11 ODS/ODM test sets for the simulated-degradations domain and 5 of 11 for the speech-synthesis domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NISQA (trained on simulated telephone degradations, D1), NR-SCOREQ matches or exceeds performance on in-domain sets (0.97 vs 0.91 on NISQA TEST FOR) and substantially outperforms on ODM sets: 0.86 vs 0.54 on VoiceMOS Test 1, 0.79 vs 0.40 on TENCENT-Rev, and 0.91 vs 0.85 on NOIZEUS. Against NR-PESQ and NR-SI SDR (trained on DL speech enhancement, D2), NR-SCOREQ wins on 9 of 11 test sets, with the largest gaps on ODM data (e.g., 0.96 vs 0.70/0.82 on P23 EXP1; 0.79 vs 0.36/0.32 on TENCENT-Rev). Against NORESQA-MOS (trained on speech synthesis, D3), NR-SCOREQ is competitive on in-domain synthesis sets (0.86 vs 0.85 on VoiceMOS Test 1) but clearly superior on all ODM sets, including a dramatic 0.91 vs 0.15 on NOIZEUS and 0.96 vs 0.40 on P23 EXP1. In the controlled L2-loss ablation (Table 3), SCOREQ Adapt outperforms the L2 baseline on all ODM test sets (e.g., 0.86 vs 0.75 on VoiceMOS Test 1; 0.79 vs 0.74 on TENCENT-Rev) and on most ODS sets, while NOMAD (which uses offline triplet mining with NSIM labels) consistently underperforms SCOREQ in both NR and NMR modes.

## Relation to Prior Work

SCOREQ adapts the batch-all triplet loss from person re-identification [13, 18]—a classification framework—to the regression setting of MOS prediction, using on-the-fly masks that exploit the relative nature of continuous labels. It builds directly on the NOMAD metric [51], which also uses contrastive regression for speech quality but relies on offline triplet preparation and NSIM-based labels; SCOREQ replaces both with in-batch triplet mining and MOS labels. The adaptive margin component is borrowed from Ha and Blanz [16], and the broader motivation aligns with RnC [66], which showed that L1/L2 loss fails to induce ordered representations for regression. The paper positions itself as a supervised contrastive regression method, distinct from the unsupervised quality prediction targeted by NOMAD and NORESQA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Domain generalization remains a critical unsolved problem in speech quality assessment, and SCOREQ provides a principled loss-level fix that consistently improves out-of-domain performance across 11 test sets and two speech domains without architectural changes. The NMR mode also offers a differentiable perceptual training loss useful for speech codecs and enhancement. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to the speech domain, a small computational overhead from the batch-all strategy, and unexplored cross-domain transfer between encoder training and MOS mapping. Whether the contrastive regression principle transfers to other regression tasks (gaze estimation, age prediction, etc.) remains an open question the authors flag as future work.
