## Problem

Existing video editing methods focus almost exclusively on altering visual content (via text prompts or reference images) while leaving motion uncontrolled, and no prior work supports simultaneous localized editing of both content and motion trajectories. The core difficulty is a training imbalance and condition coupling: dense unedited content is easy to learn and carries rich inter-frame motion cues, causing the model to ignore the sparse, abstract trajectory lines that specify new motion in the edited region.

## Input / Output

The method takes four inputs: an edited first frame (specifying new content appearance), user-drawn trajectory lines (2-channel displacement maps per frame specifying horizontal/vertical motion), the original video, and a binary editing-region mask. The edited first frame is broadcast to all subsequent frames via SVD's image-to-video conditioning, while the masked original video provides the unedited content. Output is a full video at the same resolution where the masked region shows the new content following the specified trajectories and all unmasked regions remain unchanged. Training uses 512×320 resolution on 4 A100 GPUs; inference runs at 768×768 in ~26 seconds. The method extends to multi-area editing and irregular hand-drawn masks without additional training.

## Method

ReVideo builds on Stable Video Diffusion with a single ControlNet-style module (Structure A) that injects both content and motion conditions via a shared UNet encoder with zero-conv outputs. A three-stage coarse-to-fine training strategy addresses the content-motion coupling: (1) motion prior training on trajectory control alone, (2) decoupling training where the edited and unedited regions are sourced from two different videos so the model cannot infer editing-region motion from unedited content, and (3) deblocking training on normal video data that fine-tunes only the key and value embeddings in temporal self-attention layers to remove boundary artifacts without degrading motion control. A Spatiotemporal Adaptive Fusion Module (SAFM) replaces naive summation of content and motion embeddings by predicting a spatial weight map Γ conditioned on both the editing mask and the diffusion timestep, allowing the model to adaptively emphasize motion control in the edited region and content control in the unedited region at each denoising step.

## Results

On a 16-video test set (720×1280 resolution), ReVideo achieves the best text alignment score (0.2304 CLIP) and dominates human evaluation, winning 59.1% for overall quality and 67.0% for editing target achievement — far ahead of Pika (27.9% / 23.9%), InsV2V (10.2% / 5.1%), and AnyV2V (2.8% / 4.0%). ReVideo is also the fastest method at 26 seconds per video, compared to 132 s for InsV2V and 380 s for AnyV2V. It trails Pika slightly on PSNR (32.85 vs. 33.07) and temporal consistency (0.9864 vs. 0.9956), though the authors note Pika's higher consistency is partly an artifact of generating static motion in edited regions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReVideo outperforms all three baselines on text alignment (CLIP score) and both human-evaluation metrics. Against Pika — the closest competitor — ReVideo gains +0.012 in text alignment and more than doubles the human preference for overall quality (59.1% vs. 27.9%) and editing-target achievement (67.0% vs. 23.9%), while sacrificing only 0.22 dB in PSNR and 0.009 in temporal consistency. The authors attribute Pika's consistency edge to its tendency to produce static motion in edited regions, which inflates frame-to-frame CLIP similarity. Against InsV2V and AnyV2V, ReVideo leads on every metric: PSNR improves by ~3 dB, text alignment by 0.016–0.028, and human preference by 49–56 percentage points on overall quality. ReVideo also runs 5× faster than InsV2V and ~15× faster than AnyV2V (26 s vs. 132 s / 380 s on an A100 at 768×768).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Stable Video Diffusion as the base image-to-video generation model, adopts ControlNet's zero-conv architecture for injecting external control signals, and repurposes DragNUWA's trajectory-line representation as the motion control signal. It departs from prior local video editing work (AnyV2V, Pika, EVE) by adding explicit motion trajectory control alongside content editing, and from prior motion-controlled generation work (DragNUWA, MotionCtrl, DragAnything) by operating in an editing setting where unedited content must be preserved. The key conceptual departure is identifying and addressing the training-time coupling between dense unedited content (which carries implicit inter-frame motion) and sparse trajectory lines, which prior work did not need to handle since it either edited content or controlled motion but not both simultaneously in a local region.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to unify local content editing and local motion editing in a single video generation pipeline, opening a practical path for users to precisely specify both appearance and movement in edited regions. The three-stage training strategy (motion prior → cross-video decoupling → deblocking) and the spatiotemporal adaptive fusion module provide a reusable recipe for handling competing control signals of different sparsity levels in diffusion models. Limitations include dependence on SVD's generation quality (artifacts in complex lighting or scene changes), error accumulation in long videos via sliding-window inference, and the fact that the decoupling training requires constructing paired samples from two different videos, which may not generalize to all editing scenarios. Open questions include whether the training strategy extends to additional control modalities (e.g., depth, optical flow) and whether the approach scales beyond the 512×320 resolution used in training.
