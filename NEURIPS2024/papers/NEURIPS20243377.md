## Problem

Layer normalization is widely used to prevent plasticity loss in nonstationary learning (RL, continual learning), but the precise mechanisms by which it helps—and the hidden vulnerabilities it introduces—remain poorly understood. Specifically, normalization makes the effective learning rate (ELR) depend on parameter norm, which grows during training, causing ELR to silently decay and eventually stall learning in long-running tasks. The paper asks: can we retain normalization's benefits (e.g., reviving dead ReLU units) while eliminating its vulnerability to vanishing gradients under norm growth?

## Input / Output

NaP is a training protocol applied to standard architectures (CNNs, transformers, MLPs) without changing their input/output interfaces: RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10, 224×224 ImageNet) yield class labels, text sequences (C4, 128-token batches) yield next-token predictions, and Atari game frames yield Q-value vectors. The protocol modifies only the forward pass (inserting LayerNorm before each nonlinearity) and the optimizer update (periodic per-layer weight projection), so it is drop-in compatible with existing training pipelines and does not alter the network's functional output at any given parameter setting.

## Method

Normalize-and-Project (NaP) couples two steps: (1) inserting a LayerNorm (or RMSNorm) layer immediately before every nonlinearity in the network, which—via Proposition 1—ensures that gradients to the incoming weights of a saturated ReLU unit remain nonzero through the mean/variance mixing terms, giving dead units a path back to activation; and (2) periodically projecting each layer's weight matrix back to its initial Frobenius norm (WeightProject), which keeps the effective learning rate constant by preventing the scale-invariance-induced ELR decay that would otherwise occur as norms grow. Scale and offset parameters are handled separately (jointly normalized for homogeneous activations, or regularized toward initialization for general nonlinearities). Applied to Rainbow agents on Atari, NaP reveals that the implicit ELR decay from parameter norm growth is actually critical for value-function learning, and that an explicit linear LR schedule (6.25e-5 → 1e-6) that approximates this decay outperforms both constant LR and the implicit schedule, while in continual learning NaP nearly eliminates plasticity loss across architectures and plasticity-preserving baselines.

## Results

On stationary benchmarks, NaP matches or slightly exceeds baselines: 94.64% top-1 on CIFAR-10 (vs. 94.65% baseline) and 77.26% on ImageNet-1k (vs. 77.08% baseline), while a 400M transformer trained with NaP on C4 achieves 45.7% per-token accuracy (vs. 44.8% baseline) and outperforms on downstream tasks like Lambada (56.6 vs. 54.1) and PIQA (68.8 vs. 67.3). On the sequential ALE benchmark (400M frames, 10 games × 2 cycles), NaP maintains performance comparable to a randomly initialized network on each game's second visit, whereas standard Rainbow exhibits severe plasticity loss. On single-task Atari (57 games), Rainbow + NaP with a linear LR schedule (6.25×10⁻⁵ → 10⁻⁶) outperforms standard Rainbow, Rainbow + LayerNorm, and Rainbow + NaP without a schedule, with the implicit parameter-norm-driven ELR decay being critical but suboptimal compared to an explicit schedule.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the continual CIFAR-10 random-label memorization task (200 relabelings, 20M steps), NaP applied on top of each plasticity-preserving method (Regenerative Regularization [Kumar et al.], Shrink and Perturb [Ash & Adams], ReDo [Sokar et al.], leaky/concatenated ReLU [Abbas et al.]) yields near-monotonic improvements and dramatically shrinks the performance gap between methods, with all curves becoming nearly indistinguishable in the MLP architecture. The one exception is ReDo, whose reset mechanism does not account for normalization layers. On single-task Atari, standard Rainbow [Hessel et al.] is outperformed by Rainbow + NaP with a linear LR schedule across the 57-game suite, while Rainbow + NaP without a schedule frequently underperforms standard Rainbow, revealing that the implicit ELR decay from parameter norm growth is necessary but not sufficient. On sequential ALE (the setting from Abbas et al.), NaP dramatically outperforms both standard Rainbow and Rainbow + LayerNorm: after 200M frames of sequential training, NaP agents on their second visit to each game perform comparably to a freshly randomized network, whereas standard Rainbow shows severe degradation on all games. On stationary benchmarks (CIFAR-10, ImageNet-1k, C4), NaP ties or marginally beats the baseline, confirming it does not sacrifice performance on well-conditioned tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the effective learning rate framework introduced by Van Laarhoven (2017) and the sphere-constrained training of scale-invariant networks studied by Kodryan et al. (2022), but departs from prior plasticity-loss work (regenerative regularization, ReDo, Shrink-and-Perturb) by identifying two previously underappreciated mechanisms of layer normalization: its gradient-mixing property that enables ReLU revival, and its vulnerability to vanishing gradients as parameter norms grow. Rather than treating normalization as a black-box fix for plasticity loss, the paper disentangles these two effects and proposes NaP as a unified protocol that captures the former while eliminating the latter. The use of NaP as an analytical lens to expose the role of implicit learning rate schedules in deep RL represents a novel application of the ELR framework to a domain where constant learning rates have been standard practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NaP provides a simple, architecture-agnostic protocol (add LayerNorm before nonlinearities, periodically project weights to initial norm) that virtually eliminates plasticity loss across continual learning and sequential RL benchmarks, and does not hurt stationary supervised performance. The most impactful finding is that the implicit ELR decay from parameter norm growth in Rainbow agents is not a side effect to be corrected but a critical component of their performance, directly challenging the widespread assumption that RL's nonstationarity precludes learning rate decay. Open questions include why certain value-function components require low ELR, whether adaptive schedules can be determined automatically, and how NaP should be adapted for proprioceptive domains where normalization removes spatial information. The paper also notes that scale/offset parameter drift remains a practical concern in long-running RL, requiring additional regularization.
