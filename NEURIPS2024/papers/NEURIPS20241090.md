## Problem

In cross-domain personalized federated graph learning, structural heterogeneity between clients causes two failures: (1) sharing non-generic structural knowledge globally leads to knowledge conflict that degrades collaboration, and (2) aggregated message-passing schemes exhibit biases that produce unsuitable features for individual clients' graph structures. Previous pFGL methods either share non-generic encodings (causing conflict) or restrict collaboration to avoid it (sacrificing benefits), without addressing the spectral-level structural shifts or client-specific graph preferences.

## Input / Output

Each client holds an entirely distinct graph dataset (e.g., one client has MUTAG molecules, another has IMDB social networks, another has letter-shape graphs), with node features and adjacency matrices as input. The method produces a single graph-level class label per graph via average pooling of node representations. Unusually, only spectral encoder weights and graph feature means are communicated to the server—no raw graph data or full model parameters are shared—and aggregation uses uniform averaging across clients rather than sample-size weighting to prevent large-dataset domains from dominating.

## Method

FedSSP combines two strategies. First, Generic Spectral Knowledge Sharing (GSKS) operates in the spectral domain: eigenvalues of the normalized graph Laplacian are mapped to vectors via sinusoidal encoding, processed through multi-head attention transformer blocks, and decoded to produce M filtered eigenvalue sets that construct personalized graph convolution bases. Only the eigenvalue encoder and filter encoder weights are shared globally (containing domain-agnostic knowledge), while attention modules and decoders remain client-specific, thereby avoiding knowledge conflict from structural shifts. Second, Personalized Graph Preference Adjustment (PGPA) adds a learnable preference vector δ to the extracted graph feature to satisfy each client's structural preference, with an MSE regularization term aligning local graph feature means to a dynamically updated global consensus—this prevents the preference module from absorbing features that the shared spectral backbone should capture, preserving collaboration quality.

## Results

FedSSP achieves the best average test accuracy on 5 out of 6 cross-dataset/cross-domain settings, reaching 79.62% on single-domain (SM), 84.29% on double-domain (SM-CV), and 79.12% on multi-domain (SM-SN-CV). On the one setting where it does not lead (SM-BIO-SN), FedSSP scores 72.37% versus FedStar's 72.60%, a marginal gap. Traditional FL baselines (FedAvg, FedProx) underperform even local self-training across all settings, confirming the severity of cross-domain structural heterogeneity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedSSP outperforms all eight baselines on five of six settings. The closest competitor is FedCP (KDD 2023), which achieves 81.59% on SM-CV and 78.17% on SM-SN-CV, trailing FedSSP by 2.70 and 0.95 points respectively. FedStar (AAAI 2023), the strongest FGL-specific baseline, is the only method that beats FedSSP, narrowly winning SM-BIO-SN by 0.23 points (72.60 vs. 72.37). Traditional FL methods FedAvg and FedProx fail to surpass even local self-training on most settings, with FedProx dropping as low as 63.89% on SM-BIO-SN. Among FGL baselines, GCFL and FGSSL show inconsistent performance across domains, while FedSage and APPLE remain competitive but consistently below FedSSP by 3–10 points depending on the setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of spectral GNNs (Specformer, LanczosNet, Chebyshev-based filters) and personalized federated graph learning (FedStar, GCFL, FGSSL, FedCP). It departs from prior FGL methods that share structural encodings or limit collaboration to mitigate non-IID issues, arguing instead that domain structural shifts manifest as spectral biases and that only the eigenvalue encoder contains truly generic knowledge worth sharing globally. The PGPA preference module is a novel local adjustment mechanism not present in prior pFL or pFGL work, drawing on the message-passing literature to justify why aggregated spectral filters deviate from client-specific optima.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete failure mode in cross-domain federated graph learning—knowledge conflict from non-generic structural sharing—and offers a principled spectral decomposition to resolve it. However, the approach inherits the computational overhead of spectral GNNs (eigendecomposition, storage of eigenvectors), limiting scalability to large graphs. Open questions include whether the generic/non-generic split generalizes beyond graph classification to node-level or link-level tasks, whether the direct-averaging aggregation strategy holds under more extreme sample-size disparities, and whether the spectral perspective offers advantages over spatial-domain alternatives at scale.
