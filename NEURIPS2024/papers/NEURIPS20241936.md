## Problem

Empirical neural scaling laws predict that test error decreases monotonically with model size, yet standard statistical learning theory decomposes risk into approximation, bias, and variance errors where the variance term grows with model complexity. This creates a fundamental tension: the theoretical bound predicts eventual performance degradation as models grow, while the observed scaling law predicts continued improvement. The paper resolves this by identifying the regime and mechanism under which the variance error becomes negligible, reconciling theory with the clean power-law form observed in practice.

## Input / Output

The method operates on infinite-dimensional feature vectors x in a Hilbert space, but only observes M-dimensional sketched covariates Sx (where S is a fixed Gaussian sketch with entries N(0,1/M)) and their scalar responses y. The model is a linear predictor f_v(x) = <v, Sx> with M trainable parameters, trained by one-pass SGD over N samples. The output is a matching upper and lower bound on the expected population risk as a joint function of M and N, yielding explicit scaling exponents (a−1) for model size and (a−1)/a for data size under a power-law spectrum of degree a. The bounds hold for every finite M, N ≥ 1 with high probability over the sketch, and the variance error is shown to be of strictly higher order, making it unobservable in the leading risk formula.

## Method

The paper studies one-pass SGD with geometrically decaying stepsizes on an M-dimensional sketched linear regression problem in infinite dimensions, assuming a power-law spectrum (λ_i ≈ i^{-a}, a > 1) on the data covariance and a Gaussian prior on the optimal parameter. The key technical contribution is deriving matching upper and lower bounds on the excess risk by combining sharp finite-sample SGD analysis (from Wu et al.) with new concentration results for the spectrum of the sketched covariance SHS^⊤ under the power-law assumption. The central insight is that the implicit regularization of one-pass SGD keeps the variance error at order min{M, (N_eff·γ)^{1/a}}/N_eff, which is strictly dominated by the sum of approximation error (≈ M^{1−a}) and bias error (≈ (N_eff·γ)^{1/a−1}), causing the variance to vanish from the leading risk bound. This yields the clean scaling law R(M,N) = σ² + Θ(M^{-(a-1)}) + Θ(N^{-(a-1)/a}), consistent with empirical neural scaling laws. The analysis is extended to anisotropic source conditions (where the exponent becomes b−1 and (b−1)/a) and logarithmic power-law spectra (where the risk becomes a polynomial of log M and log N).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) establishes that under a power-law spectrum of degree a > 1 and a Gaussian prior, the reducible test error of one-pass SGD on M-parameter sketched linear regression is Θ(M^{-(a-1)} + N^{-(a-1)/a}), with the variance error being of strictly higher order and thus invisible in the scaling law. Numerical simulations (Figure 1) confirm the predicted exponents: for a = 1.5, fitted exponents are (a₁, a₂) = (0.54, 0.34) ≈ (0.5, 0.33) = (a−1, 1−1/a); for a = 2, fitted exponents are (1.07, 0.49) ≈ (1.0, 0.5). Under a compute budget C = MN, the optimal allocation is M = Õ(C^{1/(a+1)}) and N = Õ(C^{a/(a+1)}), suggesting data size should slightly exceed model size.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are against prior theoretical frameworks rather than empirical baselines. Compared to Bahri et al. [4] and Bordelon et al. [8], who derived scaling-law exponents only in asymptotic regimes (one of M, N, or T → ∞) using statistical physics heuristics, this paper provides matching upper and lower bounds that hold for all finite M and N simultaneously. Compared to standard statistical learning theory bounds (e.g., Mohri et al. [30], Wainwright [41]), which predict a variance term growing with model size M and thus contradict the empirically observed monotone improvement, this paper shows the variance error is dominated by approximation + bias due to the implicit regularization of one-pass SGD, reconciling theory with the clean neural scaling law. Compared to Hoffmann et al. [21] (Chinchilla law, a₁≈0.34, a₂≈0.28) and Besiroglu et al. [7] (a₁≈0.35, a₂≈0.37), the paper's linear-regression theory predicts task-dependent exponents (a−1, 1−1/a) that match the empirical range when a is between ~2 and ~4.5. The paper also explains why Muennighoff et al. [31] observed breakdown of the clean scaling law under multiple passes: repeated passes increase the variance error, making it no longer negligible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of empirical neural scaling laws (Kaplan et al. 2020, Hoffmann et al. 2022) and the classical bias-variance decomposition from statistical learning theory, which appear to make contradictory predictions about how test error scales with model size. Technically, it builds directly on the sharp finite-sample SGD analysis of Wu et al. (2022, 2023) and Zou et al. (2021, 2023) for overparameterized linear regression, extending those tools to a sequence of sketched models with varying dimension M. Unlike prior theoretical scaling-law work (Bahri et al. 2021, Bordelon et al. 2024) that derived power-law dependence on only one quantity while sending others to infinity via statistical physics heuristics, this paper proves matching upper and lower bounds for finite M and N simultaneously, and unlike random-feature regression analyses (Rudi & Rosasco 2017, Carratino et al. 2018) that focused on the small-approximation-error regime, it treats both model size and data size as free variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine tension in the field: standard statistical learning theory predicts that variance error grows with model size (eventually causing overfitting), yet empirical scaling laws show monotonic improvement with M. By showing that one-pass SGD's implicit regularization makes the variance term strictly higher order than approximation + bias, the paper explains why the clean two-term scaling law is observed in practice and provides a principled criterion for when it breaks down (e.g., multiple passes over data). Limitations are significant: the analysis is restricted to linear models, one-pass SGD with geometric decay, Gaussian design, and power-law spectra, leaving open whether similar clean scaling laws emerge for nonlinear architectures, Adam, or other optimizers. The compute-allocation prescription (M ∝ C^{1/(a+1)}, N ∝ C^{a/(a+1)}) offers concrete guidance but its transfer to real deep networks remains unverified.
