## Problem

Current preference-based evolutionary multi-objective optimization (PBEMO) methods are inefficient and misaligned with decision-makers' true preferences because they rely on reward models or parameterized transitivity models (e.g., Bradley-Terry-Luce) that require cumbersome model selection, and they treat human feedback as deterministic rather than stochastic. Additionally, conventional dueling bandits formulations scale poorly to the large populations typical in evolutionary multi-objective optimization, and no principled termination criterion exists for managing the number of human consultations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multi-objective optimization problem (n-dimensional decision vector, m objective functions), an evolving population of non-dominated solutions generated by an EMO algorithm, and human pairwise comparison feedback (which of two presented solutions is preferred) as input. It produces solutions of interest (SOI) that reconcile conflicting objectives per the DM's preferences, a Gaussian mixture preference distribution encoding both the learned preference and its uncertainty, and the optimal subset of the population most likely to cover the SOI. The preference is encoded as a density ratio of winning over losing distributions estimated via moment matching, rather than as a scalar reward or a parametric utility function.

## Method

The core contribution is a clustering-based stochastic dueling bandits algorithm that partitions the evolutionary population into K subsets (arms) via Euclidean-distance clustering in objective space, then applies double Thompson sampling at the subset level to select comparison pairs and uniform sampling within subsets to select individual solutions for DM query, achieving a regret bound of O(K² log T) versus O(N² log T) for conventional dueling bandits. The learned preferences are transformed into a unified probabilistic format via moment-matching density-ratio estimation, yielding a Gaussian distribution per consultation session that is maintained as a convex-combination Gaussian mixture across sessions. This distribution is adapted to EMO algorithms by replacing the crowding distance metric in NSGA-II or by transforming weight vectors via the inverse Gaussian in MOEA/D. A principled termination criterion is derived from the KL divergence between consecutive preference distributions, adaptively stopping consultations when the learned preference stabilizes, thereby managing both human cognitive load and computational budget.

## Results

Across 48 benchmark problems (33 synthetic: ZDT, DTLZ, WFG; 10 RNA inverse design; 5 protein structure prediction) evaluated over 20 independent runs, D-PBNSGA-II and D-PBMOEA/D achieve the best approximation accuracy ε*(S) and average accuracy ε̄(S) in 77 out of 96 pairwise comparisons (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, α=0.05). The Scott-Knott test rank (Figure 4) confirms that both D-PBEMO instances consistently outperform the three state-of-the-art PBEMO baselines, with the advantage becoming more pronounced for many-objective problems (m ≥ 3). On the RNA inverse design task (Eterna ID 852950), D-PBNSGA-II produces predicted structures with higher similarity σ to the target and lower MFE than all baselines; on PSP, D-PBEMO instances yield significantly lower RMSD to the native structure.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares D-PBNSGA-II and D-PBMOEA/D against three state-of-the-art PBEMO algorithms: I-MOEA/D-PLVF [36] (decomposition-based with progressively learned value functions), I-NSGA-II/LTR [38] (learning-to-rank for preference elicitation), and IEMO/D [58] (decomposition-based interactive EA). Across all 96 metric-problem comparisons, the two D-PBEMO instances jointly achieve the best result in 77 cases (≈80%), with the gap widening for many-objective settings (m = 3, 5, 8, 10) where the baselines struggle with the enlarged search space. On the RNA inverse design tasks, D-PBEMO instances produce sequences with higher structural similarity σ and lower MFE than all three baselines (Figure 5). On the five four-objective PSP tasks, D-PBEMO instances yield protein structures with significantly lower RMSD to the native conformation (Figure 6). The ablation in Section 4.3 further shows that replacing the proposed clustering-based dueling bandits with standard double Thompson sampling (D-PBEMO-DTS) or a parameterized Bayesian optimization model (D-PBEMO-PBO) consistently degrades performance, confirming that the clustering strategy is critical for handling the large population sizes typical of EMO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of preference-based evolutionary multi-objective optimization (PBEMO) and dueling bandits, departing from the three existing consultation strategies (reward/ranking models, parameterized transitivity models like Bradley-Terry-Luce, and standard N-armed dueling bandits) by introducing a clustering-based stochastic dueling bandits that reduces the arm space from N solutions to K subsets. The preference elicitation module draws on density-ratio estimation via moment matching (Sugiyama et al.) to produce a unified Gaussian mixture representation, which is then adapted to NSGA-II and MOEA/D through modified crowding distance and weight vector projection. The theoretical analysis builds on the regret bounds for double Thompson sampling in dueling bandits (Wu & Liu, 2016) and the tight-clustering assumption from categorized bandits (Jedor et al., 2019), extending them to the non-stationary setting induced by solution-level sampling within clusters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical bottleneck in PBEMO: the number of DM queries scales poorly with population size in standard dueling bandits, making human-in-the-loop optimization infeasible for realistic EMO populations. The principled KL-divergence-based termination criterion replaces ad-hoc fixed interaction counts, and the O(K² log T) regret bound (vs. O(N² log T)) provides a concrete efficiency guarantee. However, the regret analysis applies only to identifying the optimal subset (region of interest), not the exact SOI, and no convergence analysis of D-PBEMO as a whole is provided. Future directions include best-arm identification within the optimal cluster, extending the termination analysis to full PBEMO convergence, and building expert-in-the-loop scientific discovery platforms.
