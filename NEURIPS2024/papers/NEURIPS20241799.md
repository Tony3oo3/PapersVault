## Problem

Calibrating expensive computer simulators against real observations typically requires rerunning simulations at fixed design points co-located with observed data, neglecting informative correlations across the design space and demanding large simulation budgets. Existing Bayesian calibration methods either fix the design points or optimize only calibration parameters, and information-theoretic adaptive design methods assume the simulator is cheap to evaluate. The paper addresses the gap of jointly selecting both simulation design points and calibration parameters in an adaptive, data-efficient manner that maximizes information gain about the true calibration parameters.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of noisy real observations with their associated design points, a black-box simulator h(x, θ) that is expensive to evaluate (minutes to hours per run), and a prior over calibration parameters. It produces a full posterior distribution over the calibration parameters (via MCMC or variational inference), a Gaussian process emulator of the simulator that can predict at arbitrary design-parameter pairs, and a sequence of optimal simulation settings. The method operates in a batch-sequential loop where each round proposes B simulation inputs jointly in the design space X and parameter space Θ, and the GP model is a bi-fidelity construction that unifies simulator outputs and real observations under a single covariance function over the joint space Z = X × Θ × {0,1}.

## Method

BACON models the simulator and real process as two fidelities of a single Gaussian process with a combined covariance function k(z, z') = k_ρ(s,s')·k̂((x,θ),(x',θ')) + ss'·k_ε(x,x'), enabling tractable joint conditioning on the unknown calibration parameters. At each iteration, it maximizes a variational lower bound on the expected information gain (EIG) about θ* by jointly optimizing the simulation design x̂, calibration parameter θ̂, and a conditional variational posterior q(θ*|ŷ) — the latter parameterized as a conditional normalizing flow (neural spline flows) to capture multi-modality. The EIG lower bound is estimated via Monte Carlo over MCMC posterior samples (NUTS), requiring only samples from the previous posterior rather than its density. This joint optimization over both design and parameter spaces is the key distinction from baselines like IMSPE (which uses MAP point estimates and fails on multi-modal posteriors) or D-optimal design (which ignores real data information), and from VBMC (which fixes design points to observed locations). A split-training variant (Algorithm 2) decouples flow training from design optimization for improved stability, and a sparse GP extension with conditional inducing-point distributions is derived to handle large datasets.

## Results

On the 2+2D synthetic calibration problem (T=50, batch B=4), BACON achieves the highest information gain D_KL(p_T||p_0) of 1.00±0.06 (unimodal) and 0.40±0.03 (bimodal), outperforming IMSPE (0.89, 0.19), D-optimal (0.42, 0.07), and Random (0.62, 0.28). On the distance-to-oracle metric D_KL(p_T||p_*), BACON achieves 0.76±0.13 (unimodal) and 0.45±0.06 (bimodal), beating all GP-based baselines in the bimodal case. On the real soft-robotics grasping calibration (T=10, B=16), BACON reaches D_KL(p_T||p_*) = 1.32±0.05, the lowest among all methods (IMSPE 1.56, D-optimal 1.50, Random 1.48). On the location-finding problem (T=30, B=4), BACON achieves D_KL(p_T||p_*) = 0.07±0.06, substantially outperforming IMSPE (0.45), D-optimal (0.23), and VBMC (5.48).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BACON consistently outperforms IMSPE, D-optimal, and Random on the information-gain metric D_KL(p_T||p_0) across all synthetic settings, with the largest margins in the bimodal case (0.40 vs. 0.19 for IMSPE and 0.07 for D-optimal). On the distance-to-oracle metric D_KL(p_T||p_*), BACON beats all GP-based baselines in the bimodal scenario (0.45 vs. 0.70 IMSPE, 0.94 D-optimal) and the location-finding problem (0.07 vs. 0.45 IMSPE, 0.23 D-optimal, 5.48 VBMC). The one case where a baseline wins is the unimodal synthetic problem, where VBMC achieves a lower D_KL(p_T||p_*) of 0.53 vs. BACON's 0.76, attributed to VBMC's variational mixture-of-Gaussians being well-suited to unimodal posteriors; however, VBMC does not provide a GP emulator and degrades sharply in the bimodal and location-finding settings. On the real soft-robotics calibration, BACON achieves the lowest D_KL(p_T||p_*) of 1.32±0.05, beating IMSPE (1.56), D-optimal (1.50), and Random (1.48), with the paper attributing IMSPE's inferiority to its concentration around a single posterior mode and the other baselines' over-spread parameter choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

BACON sits at the intersection of Bayesian calibration of computer models (Kennedy & O'Hagan, 2001) and Bayesian adaptive experimental design (BAED), two areas that had previously been treated separately. Prior calibration methods (Busby & Feraille, Marmin & Filippone) either fix simulation design points at real-data locations or use non-information-theoretic criteria like IMSPE or D-optimality, while BAED methods (Foster et al., 2019) assume the simulator is cheap and known. BACON departs by jointly optimising both design points and calibration parameters through a variational EIG lower bound, using a bi-fidelity GP to correlate simulations with real data, and conditional normalising flows (neural spline flows) for flexible posterior approximation that captures multimodality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The joint optimisation of design and calibration spaces captures informative correlations that methods fixing one space miss, which is practically important when simulations are expensive (minutes to hours per run, as in the soft-robotics experiment). The method simultaneously yields a posterior over calibration parameters and a GP emulator of the simulator, making it a one-stop tool for uncertainty quantification in simulation-based science. Key open questions include scalability beyond the cubic GP complexity (the authors sketch a sparse GP extension but do not fully validate it), performance in high-dimensional parameter spaces, and whether the Gaussian process assumption on the simulator-reality discrepancy holds for heavy-tailed error structures.
