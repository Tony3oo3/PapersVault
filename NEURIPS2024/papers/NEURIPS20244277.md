## Problem

Diffusion models routinely generate samples that lie entirely outside the support of the training distribution—such as hands with extra fingers or images containing duplicate shapes—yet the mechanism behind these 'hallucinations' remained unexplained. While mode collapse (loss of diversity) has been well studied, the mutual interaction between distinct data modes and how it produces novel, out-of-support artifacts was an open question.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244277/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation, the model takes standard Gaussian noise and produces images at dataset-specific resolutions (64×64 grayscale for Simple Shapes, 128×128 for Hands, 28×28 for MNIST). For hallucination detection, the method consumes the sequence of x̂₀ predictions extracted at each timestep during the reverse diffusion process and outputs a scalar trajectory-variance score per sample. The detection window is dataset-dependent: the last 15 steps for 1D Gaussians, last 20 steps for 2D Gaussians, t=700–850 for Simple Shapes, and t=100–150 for MNIST.

## Method

The paper identifies 'mode interpolation' as the root cause: the true score function between disjoint data modes contains sharp discontinuities (step functions), but the neural network can only learn a smooth approximation, creating a region of uncertainty between nearby modes where the model interpolates and generates out-of-support samples. This is demonstrated analytically on 1D and 2D Gaussian mixtures and confirmed via t-SNE visualization of U-Net bottleneck representations on the Simple Shapes dataset. For detection, the authors propose a trajectory-variance metric that computes the mean squared deviation of x̂₀ predictions over a short window of reverse-diffusion timesteps; hallucinated samples exhibit significantly higher variance because the score function is highly uncertain in the interpolation region. This metric filters over 95% of hallucinations while retaining 95–98% of in-support samples on synthetic datasets and 80%/81% on the real-world Hands dataset. The metric is then applied as a pre-emptive filter in recursive generative training, slowing model collapse on 2D Gaussians, Simple Shapes, and MNIST compared to random filtering.

## Results

The proposed trajectory-variance hallucination detection metric achieves strong separation across all tested settings: filtering out 95% of hallucinated samples while retaining 95% of in-support samples on Simple Shapes, 95%/98% on 1D Gaussian, 96%/95% on 2D Gaussian, and 80%/81% on the real-world Hands dataset. In recursive training on MNIST, variance-based pre-emptive filtering consistently outperforms random filtering across all generations in FID, slowing model collapse. On the 2D Gaussian grid, unfiltered recursive training leads to near-complete mode collapse by generation 20, while filtering significantly delays this degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily an empirical analysis and detection study rather than a method comparison, so it does not benchmark against competing hallucination-detection or mode-interpolation baselines. The closest comparison is between the proposed variance-based filtering and random filtering in the recursive training experiments on MNIST, where variance-based filtering achieves lower FID (better quality) at every generation while maintaining comparable precision and recall, demonstrating that the metric selectively removes low-quality hallucinated samples without sacrificing diversity. The paper also validates the mode-interpolation hypothesis by showing that using the true (discontinuous) score function in the reverse diffusion process eliminates all interpolated samples, confirming that the smooth neural-network approximation of the score is the root cause. No formal head-to-head comparison with prior hallucination-mitigation methods (e.g., data augmentation or retrieval-based approaches) is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DDPM and score-based generative modeling framework (Ho et al., 2020; Song et al., 2020) to formalize a previously informal notion—hallucination in diffusion models—as generation outside the support of the training distribution. It departs from prior failure-mode analyses (mode collapse, memorization, distorted hands) by identifying a specific mechanistic cause: the neural network's inability to learn the discontinuous score function between disjoint modes, leading to smooth interpolation in the gaps. The work also connects to the recursive training / model collapse literature (Alemohammad et al., 2023; Shumailov et al., 2023) by showing that mode interpolation compounds across generations, and draws an explicit parallel to hallucination in LLMs while grounding the diffusion-model version in a concrete geometric mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic explanation for a widely observed but poorly understood failure mode (extra/missing fingers in Stable Diffusion), reframing it as mode interpolation in representation space rather than an anatomical-complexity problem. The trajectory-variance detection metric is practical and can be applied at generation time without retraining, with implications for mitigating model collapse in recursive training as synthetic data proliferates. Key limitations include the reliance on small or synthetic datasets for the strongest quantitative claims, the modest 80% detection rate on the Hands dataset, and the open question of how to select the correct timestep window for the metric across different noise schedules and architectures. Future work could explore training-time interventions to prevent mode interpolation and validate the detection metric on large-scale text-to-image models.
