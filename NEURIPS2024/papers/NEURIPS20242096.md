## Problem

Existing methods for visual grounding of object dynamics fall into two camps: black-box neural simulators that can violate physical laws and generalize poorly, and white-box physics simulators whose expert-defined constitutive equations may not match the actual material behavior observed in the wild. The paper asks how to accurately infer the true intrinsic dynamics from visual observations alone while retaining the interpretability and generalizability of physical priors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242096/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a calibrated multi-view video sequence (1 view for synthetic, 3 views for real-world data) along with camera intrinsics/extrinsics, plus 50 multi-view images at t=0 for static 3D reconstruction. It produces per-timestep particle states (positions, velocities, elastic deformation gradients), a rendered image sequence via Particle-GS, and the learned LoRA correction parameters ΔM_θ. Notably, the main method requires no particle-level ground truth—only pixel-level visual supervision—and the initial particle set is sampled from a reconstructed surface mesh rather than derived from the Gaussian representation.

## Method

NeuMA formulates material model learning as a residual adaptation: M_θ = M_0 + ΔM_θ, where M_0 is NCLaw's pre-trained neural constitutive law (encoding physical priors like frame indifference and undeformed-state equilibrium) and ΔM_θ is a low-rank (LoRA, rank 16) adaptor that corrects the prior to match observations without overturning it. The simulator is a differentiable Material Point Method (MPM) with NeuMA embedded at the stress computation and plastic return-mapping stages. For rendering, Particle-GS introduces a particle-binding mechanism that links each 3D Gaussian kernel to a set of nearby simulation particles via Mahalanobis distance and a chi-squared confidence threshold, producing a sparse binding matrix that drives kernel deformation (centers and covariances) from particle states—decoupling the visually-optimized Gaussian distribution from the physically-uniform particle distribution. Training is end-to-end supervised: the L2 rendering loss between synthesized and observed frames back-propagates through Particle-GS and MPM to optimize only the LoRA parameters (RAdam, cosine schedule, 1000 iterations per scene).

## Results

On the six synthetic benchmarks, NeuMA achieves an average L2-Chamfer distance of 1.31 (×10⁻⁴), substantially outperforming NCLaw (12.18) and PAC-NeRF (114.80). The best per-scene results are SandFish (0.65) and ClayCat (1.00), while the hardest scene BouncyBall still reaches 1.19. Notably, NeuMA trained purely from visual supervision even matches or beats its own variant with ground-truth particle supervision (NeuMA w/ P.S., avg 1.51) on several scenes, demonstrating that the residual adaptor can recover dynamics from images alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeuMA outperforms PAC-NeRF [45] by roughly two orders of magnitude in average Chamfer distance (1.31 vs. 114.80), with the gap most dramatic on BouncyBall (1.19 vs. 516.30) where PAC-NeRF's heuristic parameter inversion fails. Against NCLaw [53], which uses a fixed pre-trained material model without per-scene adaptation, NeuMA reduces the average Chamfer distance from 12.18 to 1.31 (~9× improvement), confirming that the LoRA-based residual correction ΔM_θ is essential for matching actual dynamics. On real-world data (Spring-Gaus dataset), NeuMA surpasses Spring-Gaus [96] in both PSNR and LPIPS, producing rendered sequences more aligned with ground-truth observations. A particularly striking finding is that NeuMA trained solely from pixel-level visual supervision (avg 1.31) matches or beats the variant trained with ground-truth 3D particle positions (avg 1.51) on four of six scenes, suggesting the residual adaptor can infer intrinsic dynamics from images as effectively as from explicit particle labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeuMA positions itself at the intersection of two existing paradigms for visual dynamics grounding: black-box neural simulators (e.g., NeuroFluid, 3D-IntPhys) that are flexible but violate physical laws, and white-box physical simulators (e.g., PAC-NeRF, gradSim) that enforce physical correctness but rely on rigid expert-defined constitutive models. The key conceptual departure is the residual adaptation formulation M_θ = M_0 + ΔM_θ, which borrows the LoRA philosophy from NLP and applies it to neural constitutive laws (NCLaw), allowing a small learnable correction to refine a frozen physics prior without overturning it. On the rendering side, Particle-GS departs from PhysGaussian and Spring-Gaus, which treat 3D Gaussian kernels as physical particles in a one-to-one mapping, by introducing a many-to-many particle binding mechanism that decouples the visual representation from the simulation particles.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The residual adaptation paradigm offers a practical middle ground: it preserves the interpretability and generalizability of physics-based models while gaining the flexibility to match real-world observations that deviate from idealized equations. The demonstrated generalization to novel shapes, multi-object interactions, and extended-time prediction (3× the training horizon) suggests the learned ΔM_θ captures genuine material properties rather than scene-specific artifacts. However, the method still requires calibrated multi-view cameras for initial state acquisition, suffers from cumulative forward-simulation errors, and is sensitive to motion blur—open challenges that limit its applicability to uncontrolled real-world settings. The compatibility with image-to-3D generation models hints at a broader pipeline for physically plausible 4D content creation from a single image.
