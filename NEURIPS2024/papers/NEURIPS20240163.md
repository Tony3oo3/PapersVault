## Problem

Existing XAI methods generate explanations without accounting for how humans actually process them, and prior work on manipulating explanations relied on intensive handcrafting. This paper asks whether a computational model of human behavior in AI-assisted decision making can be leveraged to systematically manipulate AI explanations—without access to the underlying AI model—to nudge human decisions toward targeted outcomes, for both adversarial (e.g., increasing bias against protected groups) and benign (e.g., promoting appropriate reliance) purposes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a task feature vector x (raw tabular features or BERT-encoded sentence embeddings), the AI model's binary prediction y^m, the original score-based explanation e (per-feature contributions from LIME or SHAP), and a targeted human decision ŷ^h. It produces a manipulated explanation e'—a vector of per-feature contribution scores—that is constrained to remain directionally consistent with y^m (i.e., sign(Σe'_i) = sign(y^m)). The manipulation is performed entirely through the learned behavior model, requiring no access to the original AI model M.

## Method

The core approach has two stages. First, a two-layer neural network behavior model H_w_h is trained via maximum log-likelihood on collected human decision data, with inputs [x, y^m, e, x⊙e] where the element-wise interaction term x⊙e captures how humans redirect attention to features highlighted by the explanation. Second, given a targeted decision ŷ^h, the explanation e' is found by gradient-based optimization (Eq. 4) that minimizes cross-entropy between the behavior model's predicted human decision and ŷ^h, subject to a consistency penalty λ·L_consistency ensuring e' still supports y^m. The optimization initializes e' from U(-1,1), uses step size η=0.01, trade-off λ=0.01, and runs up to T=100 iterations (or until loss < τ=0.1), repeated 5 times with averaging. For adversarial manipulation, ŷ^h encodes group-biased decisions; for benign manipulation, ŷ^h is the output of a human-AI combination model that outperforms either human or AI alone.

## Results

The learned human behavior models achieve 5-fold cross-validation accuracy of 0.65 (Bias), 0.74 (Census), 0.76 (Toxicity), and 0.79 (Recidivism). Adversarially manipulated explanations significantly increase decision unfairness—FPRD deviates more from zero (p<0.05) in Census and Recidivism, and FNRD (p<0.01) in Bias—compared to SHAP or LIME explanations. Benignly manipulated explanations significantly improve human-AI team accuracy in Census, Recidivism, and Bias tasks, with no significant difference in Toxicity (where human accuracy already exceeds 0.8). Across all tasks, participants show no statistically significant differences in perceived transparency, usefulness, alignment, comprehensibility, satisfaction, or trust between manipulated and unmanipulated explanations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares manipulated explanations against two unmanipulated XAI baselines: SHAP [8] and LIME [7]. On the adversarial manipulation front, manipulated explanations produce significantly more unfair human decisions than both SHAP and LIME (FPRD p<0.05 in Census/Recidivism; FNRD p<0.01 in Bias), demonstrating that the behavior-model-driven manipulation successfully shifts human decisions toward bias against protected groups. On the benign manipulation front, manipulated explanations yield significantly higher human-AI team accuracy than both SHAP and LIME in Census, Recidivism, and Bias tasks, while showing no significant difference in Toxicity. However, on all perception metrics (transparency, usefulness, alignment, comprehensibility, satisfaction, trust), manipulated explanations are statistically indistinguishable from both SHAP and LIME baselines across nearly all tasks, meaning humans cannot detect the manipulation regardless of whether it serves adversarial or benign purposes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on computational modeling of human behavior in AI-assisted decision making (Li et al., AAAI 2024; Chen et al., NeurIPS 2022) by jointly modeling how humans integrate both AI recommendations and explanations, then repurposing the learned model as an optimization target for explanation manipulation. It departs from prior explanation-manipulation studies (Lakkaraju & Bastani, 2020; Lai et al., 2023) that relied on intensive handcrafting of explanation modifications, instead using gradient-based optimization through the behavior model to automatically generate targeted explanations without access to the underlying AI model. The work also connects to the broader literature on adversarial attacks on human-AI systems (Lu et al., IJCAI 2023) but shifts the attack surface from the AI model itself to the explanation channel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that gradient-optimized, model-driven explanation manipulations significantly shift human decisions (both adversarially toward group bias and benignly toward better accuracy) while remaining undetectable to participants raises urgent security concerns for any deployed AI-assisted decision system. The dual-use nature—where the same behavior model can improve team performance or introduce systematic bias—highlights the need for securing human-AI interaction data and ensuring explanation integrity. Key limitations include restriction to score-based explanations and binary decisions on tabular/text data; open questions include whether similar manipulation is possible for example-based, rule-based, or natural-language explanations, and whether the effects generalize to image-based or multi-class decision tasks.
