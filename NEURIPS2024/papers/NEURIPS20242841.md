## Problem

Diffusion-based adversarial purification has shown strong empirical robustness, but this may stem from insufficient evaluation rather than inherent security. EOT-based attacks, the standard tool for stochastic defenses, suffer from a gradient dilemma: high stochasticity in diffusion purifications produces unshared vulnerabilities whose inconsistent gradients cancel out under uniform averaging, rendering attacks ineffective. Additionally, the conventional 1-evaluation protocol underestimates resubmit risk, since attackers can exploit the stochasticity by resubmitting the same adversarial sample multiple times.

## Input / Output

DiffHammer takes a clean image x with ground-truth label y, a classifier f coupled with a stochastic purification φ (e.g., DiffPure, GDMP, or LM), a resubmit budget M, and an off-the-shelf attack algorithm (default: AutoAttack). At each iteration it samples N=10 purification instances, producing an adversarial noise r bounded by an ℓ∞ or ℓ2 norm constraint (e.g., 8/255 or 0.5). The final output is a robustness score defined as the minimum average or worst-case robustness across all T iterations under M resubmit evaluations, giving a conservative estimate of the defense's true vulnerability.

## Method

DiffHammer formulates the attack as an EM problem that partitions purifications into a shared-vulnerability set S1 and an unshared set S0. In the E-step, it linearly approximates the loss at an optimal noise r* to estimate each purification's affiliation probability q(z=1)=σ(L_φ(x+r*)), using only low-cost approximate gradients in the φ-space. In the M-step, it aggregates gradients reweighted by q(z=1) and applies stepwise-EM interpolation to update the adversarial noise via a plug-in attack algorithm (e.g., PGD or AA). A key efficiency contribution, gradient grafting, reduces backpropagation complexity from O(N) to O(1) by summing approximate gradients in φ-space and backpropagating through a single representative purification φ̂ selected to maximize shared-vulnerability impact. N-evaluation is seamlessly integrated into the attack loop: the N sampled purifications serve simultaneously as the evaluation for the previous iteration and the gradient source for the current one, enabling accurate resubmit-risk estimation without additional computational cost. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that EOT-based attacks are provably less effective than selective attacks when optimal perturbations for different purification subsets are sufficiently far apart, and (Theorem 2) that 1-evaluation is a biased estimator of M-resubmit robustness, with a UMVUE existing only when N≥M.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (ℓ∞: 8/255, N=10 evaluation), DiffHammer reduces average robustness to 42.54% (DiffPure), 41.64% (GDMP), and 16.15% (LM) within 17–20 iterations, compared to 76.27%, 80.61%, and 55.27% for BPDA which fails to converge (N/A iterations). Worst-case robustness drops to 22.66%, 27.54%, and 8.01% respectively. On ImageNettete, DiffHammer further reduces Avg.Rob to 38.36% (DiffPure), 33.98% (GDMP), and 26.25% (LM) in 11–14 iterations, while all EOT-based baselines fail to reach 90% of their best performance. The paper reports that most models show robustness below 50% under 10 resubmits, indicating that diffusion-based purification is significantly weaker than previously reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffHammer outperforms all ten baselines across every defense and dataset. Against the strongest EOT-based white-box attacks (BPDA, PGD, AA/DA), DiffHammer reduces Avg.Rob by 33–70 percentage points (e.g., 42.54% vs. 76.27% for BPDA on DiffPure) and converges in 17–20 iterations where baselines either fail to converge (N/A) or require 100+ iterations. Among transfer-based attacks, DMI and TMI come closest (45.64% and 45.04% Avg.Rob on DiffPure) but still trail DiffHammer by ~3 points and require 39–41 iterations; VMI and SVRE perform worse than plain PGD, confirming the gradient dilemma harms variance-reduction strategies. In the substitute-gradient comparison (Table 7), the Adjoint method [22] and Score/Full joint attacks [31] achieve 58.61% and 64.24% Avg.Rob on DiffPure respectively, versus DiffHammer's 42.54%, indicating a 16–22 point overestimation of robustness. On robust classifiers (TRADES, AWP; Table 3), the gap narrows (e.g., 53.65% vs. 53.53% for BPDA on AWP+DiffPure) because adversarial training implicitly regularizes gradients, but DiffHammer still matches or slightly exceeds all baselines while remaining the only method that converges within 30 iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the EM algorithm framework (Dempster et al., 1977) to propose a selective attack that departs from the standard EOT-based evaluation paradigm (Athalye et al., 2018), which assumes all stochastic purifications share a common vulnerability. The key conceptual departure is identifying the 'gradient dilemma'—a specific failure mode where high stochasticity in diffusion-based purification causes EOT's uniform gradient averaging to neutralize itself—motivating a selective approach that targets only the shared-vulnerability subset S₁. The paper also extends the evaluation protocol from 1-evaluation to N-evaluation, building on the resubmit-attack concept from Lucas et al. (2023) but formalizing it with a UMVUE/MLE analysis (Theorem 2) and integrating it seamlessly into the attack loop.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that mainstream diffusion-based purifications (DiffPure, GDMP, LM) fail to withstand even 10 resubmit attacks, with 1-evaluation overestimating 10-resubmit robustness by ~18%, challenges the field's confidence in these defenses and provides a concrete protocol (N-evaluation with N≥M) for more honest assessment. The gradient dilemma insight opens a broader question about how to evaluate any high-stochasticity defense, and the paper identifies the defense-side goal as driving P(S₀)→1, suggesting purification must be coordinated with adversarial training at a finer granularity. Limitations include experiments restricted to image classification and incomplete understanding of why the gradient dilemma arises (hypothesized to stem from non-clustered data distributions), leaving the design of truly robust stochastic purification as an open problem.
