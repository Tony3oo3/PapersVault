## Problem

IRL-based imitation learning typically learns a single reward function that aligns with expert demonstrations (data alignment), but this reward often fails to capture the true task objective, leading to reward hacking and unacceptable policies. The paper identifies that reward ambiguity persists even with infinite data, and that limited or mismatched demonstrations further exacerbate task-reward misalignment. The core gap is the absence of a framework that validates learned policies against the underlying task rather than merely against the demonstration data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert demonstration trajectories as weak supervision signals (not strong, since the expert need not be optimal under the true task reward), an IRL objective function (e.g., GAIL or VAIL's discriminator-based loss), and environment dynamics. It produces a protagonist policy π_P that achieves high utility across a δ-optimal reward function set R_{E,δ}, where δ controls how many reward functions are admitted (those whose IRL objective is within δ of the maximum). The policy is validated collectively against the reward set rather than optimized against a single inferred reward, and the framework is compatible with any IRL method as a plug-in component.

## Method

PAGAR (Protagonist Antagonist Guided Adversarial Reward) is a semi-supervised adversarial framework that replaces the single-reward IRL objective with a MinimaxRegret objective over a candidate reward set R_{E,δ}. It alternates between (1) training a protagonist policy π_P to minimize worst-case regret across R_{E,δ} using an on-and-off policy scheme (importance-sampled off-policy updates from an antagonist policy π_A plus standard on-policy RL), and (2) adversarially searching for a reward function within R_{E,δ} that maximizes the Protagonist-Antagonist Induced Regret, constrained by a Lagrangian penalty to stay δ-optimal under the IRL objective. The antagonist π_A serves as a proxy for the optimal policy under the current reward, enabling regret estimation without explicit policy optimization. Theoretical results (Theorems 2–3) provide sufficient conditions under which the resulting policy satisfies weak or strong task acceptance, and Proposition 2 shows the MinimaxRegret solution is equivalent to maximizing an affine combination of utilities under a policy-conditioned distribution over the reward set.

## Results

In the offline RL setting (D4RL 'random' datasets), PAGAR-RECOIL outperforms RECOIL on 3 of 4 MuJoCo tasks: hopper-random (111.16 vs. 106.87), halfcheetah-random (92.94 vs. 80.84), and ant-random (121.0 vs. 113.34), while matching on walker2d-random (108.40). In the online continuous control setting, PAGAR-fIRL achieves equivalent performance to f-IRL with significantly fewer iterations, particularly on Ant and Walker2d. In discrete MiniGrid navigation, PAGAR-VAIL with only 1 expert demonstration produces high-performance policies with substantially higher sample efficiency than VAIL, and transfer experiments (SimpleCrossingS9N2, S9N3, FourRooms) confirm robustness in unseen environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GAIL (Ho & Ermon, NeurIPS 2016), PAGAR-GAIL consistently outperforms in both DoorKey-6x6-v0 and SimpleCrossingS9N1-v0 with fewer samples, and also outperforms GAIL under different reward hypothesis sets (Sigmoid vs. Categorical discriminator outputs). Against VAIL (Peng et al., ICLR 2019), PAGAR-VAIL achieves higher sample efficiency, with the gap widening dramatically when demonstrations are reduced from 10 to 1. Against f-IRL (Ni et al., CoRL 2021) in online MuJoCo control, PAGAR-fIRL matches or approaches f-IRL's final performance with significantly fewer iterations, most notably on Ant and Walker2d. Against RECOIL (Sikchi et al., ICLR 2024) in offline D4RL settings, PAGAR-RECOIL wins on 3 of 4 tasks (hopper +4.3, halfcheetah +12.1, ant +7.7) and ties on walker2d, while also showing much lower variance (e.g., ±0.10 vs. ±17.62 on halfcheetah). The paper also notes that IQ-Learn (Garg et al., NeurIPS 2021) is not directly comparable since it does not explicitly optimize a reward function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the GAN-based IRL lineage (GAIL, AIRL, VAIL) but departs from the standard objective of learning a single reward function that best fits expert data. It reframes the problem through a task-alignment lens formalized via Abel et al.'s task definition, and borrows the protagonist/antagonist structure from unsupervised environment design (Dennis et al. 2020) to create an adversarial reward-searching mechanism. Unlike prior reward-set approaches (Metelli et al. 2021, Lindner et al. 2022) that target reward ambiguity, PAGAR targets the broader problem of reward misalignment by training a policy to perform well across a δ-optimal reward set rather than committing to one IRL-optimal reward. The method is designed as a meta-algorithm that wraps around existing IRL objectives, making it composable with GAIL, VAIL, f-IRL, and RECOIL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-recognized but under-theorized failure mode of IRL-based IL: the IRL-optimal reward can be task-misaligned, leading to reward hacking and poor generalization. The theoretical guarantees (Theorems 2–3) clarify when the MinimaxRegret objective recovers an acceptable policy, though the conditions are somewhat restrictive (requiring a wide [U̲, Ū] gap and bounded Wasserstein distance). Open questions include how to automatically select δ without task-specific tuning, whether the binary-outcome task assumption generalizes to continuous-outcome tasks, and whether the computational overhead of maintaining two policies plus adversarial reward search scales to high-dimensional continuous control. The practical impact is moderate: the approach is a drop-in wrapper that improves sample efficiency in limited-demonstration and transfer settings, but it does not eliminate the need for careful hyperparameter selection.
