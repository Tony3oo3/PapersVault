## Problem

The neural circuit mechanism by which the brain selects and generates motor actions during goal-directed navigation remains unclear, and no computational theory has linked abstract operator search to biological neural circuits. While motion planning can be formulated as finding a group operator that maps the current sensory state to a goal state, how neural circuits actually implement this search—particularly for the 1D rotation group relevant to Drosophila navigation—had never been explored.

## Input / Output

The circuit receives two rotation-equivariant population codes: the current sensory response u(x−s) and the goal response u(x−h), where x is the neuron's preferred direction and the response profile u(·) is left general (only requiring equivariance under U(1) rotation). The output is a scalar rotation speed v_t computed as the firing-rate difference between two pooled neurons (r_{v+} − r_{v−}), which drives effector muscles to rotate the heading direction. The optimal operator R̂(θ*) with θ* = h−s is recovered implicitly through the sequential application of small rotations rather than as a single closed-form lookup.

## Method

The paper formulates motion planning as Lie group operator search (finding R̂(θ) such that R̂(θ)∘u(s)=u(h)) and derives a two-layer feedforward circuit implementing this search using three motifs: connection phase shift (delta-function weights offset by ±Δθ to create rotated sensory inputs u(s±)), a general monotonically increasing nonlinear activation F(·) whose second-order Taylor coefficient F₂ enables inner-product computation, and spatial pooling (integration over x) to produce scalar outputs. The circuit approximates the gradient dL/dθ_t as a finite difference L(θ_t+Δθ)−L(θ_t−Δθ), which reduces to the difference of squared norms ‖u(s+)+u(h)‖²−‖u(s−)+u(h)‖², computable in a single feedforward pass. In the group representation space (Fourier eigenfunctions f_ω), the optimal operator's representation ρ_ω(θ*)=e^{−iω(h−s)} is a simple ratio U(h)/U(s), and the circuit's computation corresponds geometrically to rotating the operator's phase vector and reading off the real-axis projection. The full sensory-action loop assembles this feedforward module with a ring attractor sensory module and rotation-generator motor module, achieving sequential rotation dynamics with total complexity O(N log|h−s|), which can be lower than FFT-based O(N log N) when the heading is near the goal.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative result is a computational complexity comparison for 1D rotation operator search (Table 1). The derived two-layer feedforward circuit achieves O(N log|h−s|) complexity for sequential motion planning, where N is the neuron number and |h−s| is the angular distance to the goal. This is strictly lower than the standard FFT-based representation space approach at O(N log N) when the stimulus direction is sufficiently close to the goal (|h−s| < N), and dramatically lower than the brute-force group convolution at O(N²). The full sensory-action loop circuit, assembled from ring attractor sensory module, feedforward motion planning module, and motor module, successfully reproduces sequential rotation dynamics tracking a fixed and moving goal direction in numerical simulations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional ML baselines, so the comparisons are between computational strategies for the same operator search problem. The feedforward circuit (O(N log|h−s|)) outperforms both the brute-force group convolution (O(N²)) and the FFT-based representation space method (O(N log N)) in the regime where the angular distance to goal is small relative to neuron count. Architecturally, the derived two-layer feedforward circuit (connection phase shift → nonlinear activation → pooling) is shown to be structurally analogous to Drosophila's PFL3→DN navigation circuit identified in recent experimental work [20–22], but the present theory removes the requirement for a pure cosine (ω=1) neural response profile, generalizing to arbitrary response shapes (e.g., Gaussian). The paper also notes that its sensorimotor transformation circuit is complementary to existing group-equivariant neural networks [17, 24–27], which model the sensory system rather than the action-selection stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the equivariant group-action framework established by Zuo et al. (2023) and the ring-attractor-to-rotation-group mapping of Zhang et al. (2022), extending them from sensory representation to the sensorimotor transformation stage. It departs from recent Drosophila navigation circuit models (Westeinde et al. 2024, Pires et al. 2024) by formulating motion planning explicitly as a Lie group operator search problem and deriving the circuit from first principles rather than fitting to observed connectivity. The group representation theory (Fourier eigenfunction decomposition) connects to the broader group equivariant machine learning literature (Cohen & Welling, Kondor et al.), but here it is used to represent abstract operators rather than to design equivariant feature extractors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to rigorously link abstract Lie group operator search to a biologically plausible two-layer feedforward circuit, providing a normative template that could generalize motion planning to non-commutative groups (SO(3), SE(2)) where intuitive solutions do not exist. The computational complexity result—that the sequential feedforward circuit achieves O(N log|h−s|) and can outperform FFT-based group convolution when the angular error is small—suggests an efficiency advantage for near-goal scenarios. Key open questions include whether the framework extends cleanly to non-commutative groups, how the 'false nulling' problem (zero speed at anti-aligned heading) is resolved biologically, and whether the direct gain-modulation pathway from DN to P-EN neurons is experimentally confirmed.
