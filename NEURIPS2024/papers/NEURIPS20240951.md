## Problem

Standard OOD detection methods in feature space either impose parametric assumptions (e.g., Gaussian fitting in GDA) that fail on clusters with complex, non-ellipsoidal shapes, or require intrusive modifications such as additional models, special architectures, or fine-tuning. The paper asks whether one can measure how 'central' a test point is relative to the training distribution in feature space without any distributional assumption, without altering the trained model, and without any additional training.

## Input / Output

At test time, a single image is passed through the backbone of an already-trained classifier to obtain its feature vector in the penultimate layer (before the softmax head). The method then computes a scalar Lens Depth score using a pre-computed set of K-means-reduced reference points per class (typically 500–1500 points per class), and the score is compared against a threshold ε to decide ID versus OOD. No forward pass through the classifier head is needed for the uncertainty score, and the method introduces no trainable parameters.

## Method

The method computes Lens Depth (LD) of a test feature point with respect to each class's training features, where LD is the probability that the point lies in the intersection of two random balls centered at two random training points with radius equal to their mutual distance. The distance metric is a modified sample Fermat distance that, unlike Euclidean distance, adapts to local density and manifold geometry by favoring paths through high-density regions (controlled by a single parameter α, typically set to 7). A key modification adds the Euclidean distance from the query point to its nearest reference point, eliminating the Voronoi-cell artifact that otherwise causes LD to remain constant far from the data and preventing it from vanishing at infinity. The final uncertainty score is the maximum LD across all class clusters, and computational complexity is reduced from O(CN²) by replacing the full per-class training set with K-means centroids, which the authors show outperforms random subsampling even at very small n.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, the proposed LD method achieves AUROC of 0.965±0.003 (Tiny-ImageNet), 0.892±0.002 (CIFAR-100), and 0.936±0.006 (SVHN) for OOD detection, consistently outperforming GDA (0.945/0.864/0.914) across all three OOD sets. On FashionMNIST/MNIST, LD achieves 0.971±0.001 AUROC, surpassing DUQ (0.955), Deep Ensembles (0.861), and Mahalanobis Distance (0.942), though trailing LL ratio (0.994). In the harder hold-one-out setting where OOD data is closer to ID data, LD's advantage over GDA widens substantially (e.g., 0.969 vs 0.898 on MNIST, 0.806 vs 0.759 on CIFAR-10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LD outperforms GDA (Mahalanobis Distance) [13] on all three CIFAR-10 OOD sets with ResNet-18 (e.g., 0.965 vs 0.945 on Tiny-ImageNet) and the gap widens dramatically in the hold-one-out setting (0.969 vs 0.898 on MNIST), confirming that Gaussian assumptions fail when OOD data is close to ID. Against DUQ [27], LD consistently wins on all reported benchmarks (e.g., 0.936 vs 0.927 on CIFAR-10/SVHN with ResNet-18; 0.926 vs 0.868 on Tiny-ImageNet with Wide-ResNet). LD also beats the energy-based method [16] on all three OOD sets (e.g., 0.926 vs 0.881 on Tiny-ImageNet). Against SNGP [14], LD wins on CIFAR-10/Tiny-ImageNet (0.926 vs 0.899) and CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 (0.906 vs 0.911 is a near-tie), while SNGP edges ahead on CIFAR-10/SVHN (0.940 vs 0.939). DDU [20] is competitive: with ResNet-18, LD wins on Tiny-ImageNet and CIFAR-100 but DDU wins on SVHN; with Wide-ResNet, DDU is slightly better on CIFAR-100 and SVHN while LD is better on Tiny-ImageNet. Deep Ensembles [9] (5 models) outperforms LD on CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 (0.921 vs 0.906) and SVHN (0.977 vs 0.939) but LD wins on Tiny-ImageNet (0.926 vs 0.901) and on FashionMNIST/MNIST (0.971 vs 0.861). The only baseline that beats LD is LL ratio [23] on FashionMNIST/MNIST (0.994 vs 0.971), though it requires training two additional generative models with careful noise tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate mathematical traditions: statistical depth (specifically Lens Depth from Liu 1990 and its weighted variant from Cholaquidis et al. 2023) and Fermat distance (from Groisman, Jonckheere, Sapienza 2022 on nonhomogeneous first-passage percolation). It departs from the dominant OOD detection paradigm—Gaussian-based methods (GDA/Mahalanobis), single-forward-pass trained methods (DUQ, SNGP, DDU), and ensemble methods (Deep Ensembles, LL ratio)—by proposing a purely non-parametric, non-intrusive score computed directly in the feature space of an already-trained model. The key technical departure is a modified sample Fermat distance that eliminates Voronoi-cell artifacts (Proposition 1) and a K-mean/Center reduction strategy that cuts the O(CN²) complexity while preserving accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a principled, assumption-free alternative for OOD detection that requires no retraining, no auxiliary models, and no distributional prior—making it immediately applicable to frozen or pre-trained models where retraining is infeasible. The hold-one-out experiments (Table 5.4) provide targeted evidence that the non-Gaussian geometry captured by Fermat distance yields meaningfully sharper ID/OOD boundaries than elliptical Gaussian fits when OOD data is semantically close to ID. Open questions include the computational cost at scale (mitigated but not eliminated by K-mean reduction), the requirement that each class forms a connected cluster in feature space, and the lack of a formal error bound for the reduced LD estimator. The paper also notes that LD measures data-scarcity uncertainty specifically, not epistemic or aleatoric uncertainty, so it should complement rather than replace entropy-based scores in overlapping-class scenarios.
