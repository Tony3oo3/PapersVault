## Problem

While many AI architectures have established neuroscience counterparts, the Transformer's self-attention mechanism has been largely viewed as fundamentally different from biological cognition. This paper asks whether the emergent behavior of interacting attention heads—specifically induction heads responsible for in-context learning in LLMs—shares computational principles with human episodic memory, and if so, how.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240200/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on pre-trained Transformer weights (GPT2-small, Pythia 70m–12b, Qwen-7B, Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B) probed with repeated random-token prompts (two copies of 100 common English tokens) and natural-language sequences from C4. For each attention head, the method computes average attention scores as a function of relative position lag (analogous to conditional response probability in human free recall), then fits CMR parameters to produce a per-head CMR distance, fitted drift parameters (β_enc, β_rec, γ_FT, τ⁻¹), induction-head matching score, copying score, and an ICL score measuring loss reduction over context length.

## Method

The paper formally re-writes the Contextual Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model of human episodic memory as a two-layer Q-composition induction head: the word-to-context matrix M^FT acts as a first-layer duplicate-token head (query = current word, key = past words, value = past context), the context-to-word matrix M^TF acts as a second-layer induction head (query = updated context, key = past contexts, value = past words), and the recurrently updated temporal context vector plays the role of position embeddings in the residual stream. To quantify behavioral similarity, the paper defines CMR distance as the MSE between a head's average attention scores and the CRP predicted by CMR with optimally fitted parameters, showing that heads with high induction-head matching scores have low CMR distances and that CMR significantly outperforms a Gaussian baseline. The paper further demonstrates that CMR-like heads emerge in intermediate-to-late layers, their fitted β parameters increase toward human-like values during training, and ablating the top 10% CMR-like heads degrades ICL performance on natural text, establishing a causal link between episodic-memory-like attention and in-context learning.

## Results

Across 12 models (GPT2, Pythia family, Qwen-7B, Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B), CMR provides significantly better fits to induction-head attention patterns than a Gaussian baseline: average CMR distance is 0.05–0.12 for top 20–200 induction heads, versus 0.97–1.0 for the Gaussian (all p < 0.0001). Ablating the top 10% CMR-like heads (smallest CMR distance) worsens the ICL score relative to ablating the same number of random heads, with the effect most significant for GPT2 and Qwen-7B (p < 0.001). Fitted temporal-drift parameters (β_enc, β_rec) for top induction heads fall within the range observed in human free-recall data (PEERS dataset, N=171), and CMR-like heads predominantly emerge in intermediate-to-late layers across all tested architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative comparison is between the CMR model and a Gaussian function (with the same number of free parameters) as a baseline for fitting attention-head CRP curves. CMR dramatically outperforms the Gaussian across all model sizes and head selections, with distances roughly 8–20× lower, indicating that the asymmetric contiguity structure of CMR (forward asymmetry + temporal clustering) captures induction-head behavior far better than a symmetric bell curve. The second key comparison is the ablation experiment: ablating the top 10% CMR-like heads produces a significantly higher (worse) ICL score than ablating an equal number of randomly chosen heads, particularly for GPT2 and Qwen-7B (p < 0.001), while the effect is non-significant for some smaller Pythia models. This suggests CMR-like heads are causally relevant for ICL rather than epiphenomenal. The paper does not compare against other interpretability methods or alternative memory models as baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected literatures: the mechanistic interpretability of induction heads in Transformers (Olsson et al. 2022; Elhage et al. 2021) and the Context Maintenance and Retrieval (CMR) model of human episodic memory (Polyn et al. 2009). While prior work had linked Transformers to generalized Hopfield networks, language cortex activity, and hippocampal circuit representations, no one had formally mapped the Q-composition induction circuit onto CMR's word-to-context and context-to-word associative matrices. The paper's key conceptual move is showing that CMR's temporal context vector plays the role of a recurrently updated position embedding, making the two-layer CMR architecture mathematically isomorphic to a Q-composition linear induction head.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CMR-like attention biases (asymmetric contiguity, forward asymmetry) emerge in intermediate-to-late layers of multiple LLM families and evolve toward human-like parameter values during training provides a cognitive-science grounding for in-context learning that goes beyond the 'pattern matching' framing. However, the causal evidence is limited: ablation of top CMR-like heads degrades ICL scores, but the Hydra effect and the inability to isolate which specific CMR-like characteristic is causally relevant leave the mechanistic claim open. A key future direction is whether CMR can serve as a true mechanistic model (not just a behavioral fit) in larger Transformers, and whether the recurrent context-update dynamics of CMR have a concrete implementation in the residual stream of deep models.
