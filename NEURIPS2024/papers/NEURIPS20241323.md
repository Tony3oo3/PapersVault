## Problem

Differentially private training (DPSGD and variants) injects Gaussian noise whose magnitude scales with model size and number of steps, causing severe accuracy degradation especially for large-model pretraining. Existing noise-reduction strategies operate in the time domain (reducing per-iteration noise magnitude via adaptive clipping, sparse gradients, or dynamic privacy budgets), treating each update step independently and ignoring the temporal correlation structure of gradients. The paper asks whether a frequency-domain perspective on the gradient sequence can provide a complementary, theoretically grounded noise-reduction mechanism that is orthogonal to these time-domain approaches.

## Input / Output

At each iteration the method consumes the privatized mini-batch gradient g_t (the clipped per-sample gradient average plus injected Gaussian noise w_t ~ N(0, σ_DP² I_d)) along with a set of pre-specified filter coefficients {a_τ}_{τ=1}^{n_a} and {b_τ}_{τ=0}^{n_b} (typically 1st- or 2nd-order, designed following Chebyshev or Butterworth templates with unit-gain and stability constraints). It produces a bias-corrected filtered direction m̂_t = m_t / c_{a,t} that replaces the raw privatized gradient in the parameter update x_{t+1} = x_t − η m̂_t. The filter requires storing n_a + n_b past gradient and state vectors per parameter (O(d) extra memory, independent of the number of steps T), and the bias-correction scalars c_{a,t}, c_{b,t} are updated recursively at negligible cost.

## Method

The core insight is that the sequence of true gradients {∇F(x_t)} is a low-frequency signal (its auto-correlation decays as O(τ²) under L-smoothness, so its power spectral density concentrates at low frequencies), while the injected DP noise {w_t} is white (flat PSD across all frequencies). DOPPLER applies a linear low-pass filter to the privatized gradient sequence: m_t = −Σ_{τ=1}^{n_a} a_τ m_{t−τ} + Σ_{τ=0}^{n_b} b_τ g_{t−τ}, which can be expanded as a weighted moving average m_t = Σ_{τ=0}^{t} κ_τ g_{t−τ}. This amplifies the low-frequency gradient component and attenuates the high-frequency noise, increasing the signal-to-noise ratio of the update direction. The approach generalizes momentum (a first-order filter with a_1=−0.9, b_0=0.1) to higher-order IIR/FIR filters, and a bias-correction factor c_{a,t} compensates for zero-initialization of the filter states. Because the filter is a post-processing step on the already-privatized gradient, the (ε,δ)-DP guarantee is preserved unchanged, and the convergence bound shows the noise term is reduced by a factor of 1/(2·SNR) relative to vanilla DPSGD, with no additional bias or privacy cost.

## Results

The paper reports that DP optimizers equipped with the DOPPLER low-pass filter outperform their unfiltered counterparts by 3%–10% in test accuracy across various models and datasets. On the GLUE benchmark with RoBERTa-base at ε=3, LP-DPAdamBC achieves 83.55% on MNLI, 85.71% on QQP, 87.63% on QNLI, and 91.71% on SST-2, improving over DPAdamBC by up to 1.2 points (e.g., MNLI: 83.55 vs. 82.39). At ε=8, LP-DPAdamBC reaches 83.80% on MNLI and 86.50% on QQP, again surpassing DPAdamBC. The paper notes that improvements are most pronounced during pre-training (where injected DP noise is largest) and diminish for fine-tuning tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla DPSGD (Abadi et al., 2016), LP-DPSGD consistently outperforms on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and MNIST pre-training at ε=8, with the gap widening as model size increases (e.g., ResNet-50 and ViT show larger gains than a 5-layer CNN). Against DPAdamBC (Tang et al., 2024), LP-DPAdamBC wins on all four GLUE subtasks at both ε=3 and ε=8, with the largest margin on MNLI at ε=3 (83.55 vs. 82.39, +1.16 points) and QQP at ε=8 (86.50 vs. 85.94, +0.56 points). Against DPGaLore (Zhao et al., 2024), LP-DPGaLore outperforms the unfiltered version on CIFAR-10 across all tested privacy budgets. Compared to the DPAdam results reported by Li et al. (2021) on GLUE, LP-DPAdamBC achieves higher accuracy on MNLI and QQP at both privacy levels but slightly trails on QNLI and SST-2 at ε=8 (87.76 vs. 87.94 and 91.82 vs. 92.09), suggesting the low-pass filter's benefit is most pronounced when DP noise dominates the gradient signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DPSGD framework (Abadi et al., 2016) and the emerging signal-processing perspective on optimization (Hu & Lessard, 2017; Gannot, 2022), but departs from prior DP noise-reduction work by operating in the frequency domain rather than the time domain. Existing approaches—adaptive clipping, automatic clipping, dynamic privacy budgets, and model-architecture modifications—all reduce per-step noise magnitude, while DOPPLER post-processes the privatized gradient sequence as a time series to suppress high-frequency noise. The paper also distinguishes itself from correlated-noise methods (Kairouz et al., 2021; Denisov et al., 2022; Choquette-Choo et al.), which pre-process gradients via matrix decompositions of the prefix-sum operator; DOPPLER instead applies a recursive low-pass filter as a post-processing step, requiring only O(d) extra memory and remaining compatible with arbitrary first-order optimizers like Adam and GaLore.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first convergence and privacy guarantee for DPSGD augmented with a frequency-domain filter, showing that the noise term is scaled by 1/(2·SNR) relative to vanilla DPSGD, and that momentum is a special case (first-order low-pass filter). The 3–10% accuracy gains across multiple datasets, models, and privacy budgets are practically meaningful for DP pretraining, where performance degradation has been a major barrier. However, the method's applicability to very large foundation models is limited by the O(n_a + n_b) memory overhead per parameter for higher-order filters, and the filter coefficients currently require empirical tuning or online estimation. Open questions include whether adaptive filter design can eliminate the need for prior knowledge of gradient auto-correlation, and whether the approach composes effectively with other noise-reduction techniques at scale.
