## Problem

Prior data poisoning attacks on diffusion models required impractical modifications to the training objective, noise distribution, or sampling process, deviating from the stealthy BadNets paradigm used in image classification. This paper asks whether a standard BadNets-like attack (trigger injection plus mislabeling, with the diffusion training loss left untouched) can directly compromise DMs, and what bilateral effects—both adversarial and defensive—emerge from such poisoned models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242614/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard image-caption training set (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, ImageNette/Caltech15 at 256×256) in which a universal trigger (a 1/10-size black-and-white square or a Hello Kitty overlay at α=0.2) is injected into a fraction p ∈ [1%, 20%] of non-target-class images and their captions are rewritten to the target class. Generation uses standard classifier-free guidance (weight 5) with DDPM, DDIM, or SDE samplers, and outputs are partitioned into four groups (G1–G4) by an externally trained ResNet-50 based on trigger presence and prompt alignment. For the defensive experiments, generated images are fed to existing backdoor detectors (Cognitive Distillation, STRIP, FCT) to produce AUROC scores, or used as training data for ResNet-50/VGG-16/DenseNet-121 classifiers, or the DM itself serves as a classifier by comparing denoising losses across class prompts.

## Method

The paper applies the classic BadNets poisoning recipe—injecting a fixed image trigger into non-target-class samples and relabeling them to the target class—directly into the standard diffusion training objective (Eq. 2) without altering the noise schedule, loss function, or sampler. Two 'Trojan Horse' effects are uncovered: (A1) prompt-generation misalignment (G1) and (A2) trigger-tainted but prompt-aligned generations (G2), with the combined adversarial ratio reaching ~73% at p=10%, far exceeding the poisoning ratio. A key phenomenon, 'trigger amplification,' shows that the fraction of trigger-present images in generated outputs systematically surpasses the training-set trigger ratio, and a 'phase transition' occurs around p≈5% where the dominant adversarial mode shifts from G2 to G1. On the defensive ('Castle Walls') side, the paper demonstrates that (a) trigger amplification improves AUROC of CD/STRIP/FCT detectors by up to 0.25 when applied to generated images rather than the training set, (b) training a ResNet-50 on DM-generated images at p=1% drops ASR from ~88% to <1%, and (c) a diffusion classifier (EDM backbone, denoising-error-based scoring) achieves 30–50% lower ASR than ResNet-18, with further gains from filtering the top p_filter% denoising-loss samples. Finally, the paper links poisoning to data replication, showing that injecting triggers into duplicated training samples amplifies both the replication score (SSCD similarity) and the adversarial G1/G2 ratios.

## Results

On ImageNette with BadNets-1 at p=10%, the poisoned SD generates 54% G1 (trigger + prompt-mismatch) and 19.4% G2 (trigger + prompt-match) images, far exceeding the 10% poisoning ratio — a phenomenon termed 'trigger amplification.' For defense, training a ResNet-50 classifier on DM-generated images instead of the original poisoned set reduces ASR from 87.7% to 0.9% at p=1% (BadNets-1, ImageNette), and from 99.7% to 52.5% at p=5%. The diffusion classifier (EDM-based) with denoising-loss filtering achieves 45.7% ASR at p=10% on CIFAR-10, compared to 100% ASR for ResNet-18 under the same poisoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the diffusion classifier of Li et al. [19] (EDM backbone), the paper shows that DMs used as classifiers are inherently more robust to BadNets poisoning than standard ResNet-18: at p=10% on CIFAR-10, the diffusion classifier achieves 45.7% ASR (with 10% denoising-loss filtering) versus 100% ASR for ResNet-18, while maintaining comparable TA (90.9% vs. 94.1%). Against BadT2I [15] (Zhai et al., ACM MM 2023), the proposed BadNets-like method produces slightly lower trigger amplification (G1: 53.2% vs. 58.4% at 50% poisoning on the shared 500-pair dataset) and a higher FID (15.2 vs. 13.1), but this gap is expected since BadT2I additionally modifies the training objective and injects textual triggers — conditions the paper deliberately avoids to maintain the stealthiness of a pure data-poisoning setup. The key distinction is that the paper's method requires only dataset contamination (no objective or sampling changes), making it a more realistic threat model while still producing consistent adversarial and defensive effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior DM poisoning work (BadDiff, TrojDiff, VillanDiff) by removing the requirement to modify the diffusion training objective, noise distribution, or sampling process, instead showing that a vanilla BadNets-style attack (trigger injection + mislabeling on the training set alone) suffices to compromise diffusion models. It builds on the BadNets framework for image classification and on the 'diffusion classifier' paradigm (Li et al., ICCV 2023) to draw defensive insights. Additionally, it establishes a previously unexplored link between data poisoning and the data replication phenomenon in DMs, connecting two research threads that had been studied independently.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the simplest form of data poisoning (no training-procedure modification) is effective against DMs lowers the perceived security barrier for these widely deployed generative models and suggests that prior work's reliance on modified training objectives may have been unnecessary. The 'trigger amplification' and 'phase transition' phenomena offer concrete, actionable defense signals (e.g., using generated images to boost detection AUROC, or training classifiers on DM-generated data to reduce ASR below 2% at low poisoning ratios). Limitations include the inability to achieve 100% attack success rate and the observation that clean-label poisoning is far weaker against DMs; open questions include whether these effects persist at the scale of production models (e.g., SDXL, DALL-E) and whether the data-replication–poisoning interaction can be exploited for membership inference or copyright enforcement.
