## Problem

Existing Learning with Noisy Labels (LNL) methods either depend on the memorization effect during training to separate clean from noisy samples or require strong dataset-specific assumptions (e.g., known noise transition matrices) that break down under high noise rates. There is no transferable, zero-shot approach that can directly detect and rectify label noise in arbitrary datasets without training a task-specific classifier or fitting noise models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243818/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an image-label pair from a noisy dataset along with a small clean exemplar set (3 images per class) used for both in-context prompting and feature-level perturbation. The MLLM (MMICL with BLIP-2 vision encoder and FLAN-T5-XXL decoder) is restricted to output a binary True/False token, and CLIP provides top-C=3 candidate labels for rectification. The final outputs are a normalized ICD score per example (used with a threshold τ to classify clean vs. noisy) and, for noisy examples, a rectified label selected by maximizing ICD across CLIP candidates.

## Method

NoiseGPT exploits a probability curvature effect of MLLMs: clean image-label pairs reside on smooth, convex probability surfaces, while mismatched pairs fall on fluctuated, non-convex regions, causing their softmax probabilities to oscillate under perturbation rather than decrease smoothly. To probe this curvature, the paper introduces a token-wise Mixture-of-Feature (MoF) perturbation that replaces individual feature tokens of the query image with tokens from exemplars of other classes, operating in the latent embedding space to preserve modality alignment. The In-Context Discrepancy (ICD) measure is then computed as the difference between the MLLM's probability on the original example and the expected probability over n=10 MoF-perturbed versions, normalized by the perturbation standard deviation. For label rectification, CLIP generates top-C candidate labels and ICD is recomputed for each candidate; the label yielding the highest ICD is selected as the corrected label. The MLLM is prompted with one True and one False exemplar pair to leverage in-context learning, and the binary output constraint stabilizes predictions compared to multi-class classification.

## Results

NoiseGPT achieves an AUROC of 0.9253 on ILSVRC12 (Sym. 40%) and 0.83–0.85 on CIFAR-10N, demonstrating strong zero-shot noise detection. In detection comparison on CIFAR-10 Sym. 80%, NoiseGPT attains 96.07% F1, outperforming Proto-Mix (94.57%) and DivideMix (93.49%). When integrated with M-correction on CIFAR-10 Sym. 90%, classification accuracy jumps from 68.7% to 91.5% (+22.8%), and with Pro-Mix from 75.0% to 89.6% (+14.6%). On CIFAR-100 Sym. 90%, NoiseGPT+Pro-Mix reaches 47.4% versus Pro-Mix's 28.9% (+18.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against M-correction [35], NoiseGPT delivers the largest gains at extreme noise: +22.8% on CIFAR-10 Sym. 90% (91.5% vs 68.7%) and +26.5% on CIFAR-100 Sym. 90% (39.4% vs 12.9%), while at lower noise (Sym. 20%) it slightly underperforms (89.2% vs 93.7%), suggesting the cleansing step removes some clean samples when noise is already low. Against Pro-Mix [59], NoiseGPT consistently improves accuracy across all noise levels, with the most dramatic lift at CIFAR-100 Sym. 90% (47.4% vs 28.9%, +18.5%) and CIFAR-10 Asym. 40% (92.3% vs 80.5%, +11.8%). In the direct detection comparison on CIFAR-10 Sym. 80% (Table 2), NoiseGPT achieves 96.07% F1, beating Proto-Mix (94.57%) and DivideMix (93.49%) by 1.5 and 2.6 points respectively. On the real-world WebVision dataset (Table 5), NoiseGPT+DivideMix reaches 78.10% accuracy versus DivideMix alone at 77.32%, a modest but consistent gain over Co-teaching (63.58%) and F-correction (61.12%). Mixup [15] and Co-teaching [16] serve as older baselines that NoiseGPT+M-correction surpasses by 47.6 and 61.4 points respectively on CIFAR-10 Sym. 90%, underscoring the advantage of explicit data cleansing over purely training-time robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the probability curvature concept from DetectGPT (Mitchell et al., 2023) — originally designed for detecting machine-generated text — and adapts it to the vision domain via MLLMs for label noise detection. It builds directly on MMICL's multi-modal in-context learning capability as the backbone, and leverages CLIP's zero-shot classification to generate candidate labels for rectification. Unlike prior LNL methods that rely on DNN memorization effects, loss correction via transition matrices, or sample selection during training, NoiseGPT operates entirely in a zero-shot, training-free regime, positioning MLLMs as external 'knowledge experts' rather than the classifier itself. The token-wise Mixture-of-Feature perturbation technique is a novel contribution that operates at the latent token level to avoid modality misalignment, distinguishing it from input-space augmentations or intermediate-feature mixing in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NoiseGPT introduces the first framework for using MLLMs as zero-shot noise detectors and rectifiers, offering a transferable, training-free data cleansing pipeline that can be plugged into existing LNL methods without architectural changes. However, its practical adoption is constrained by substantial computational cost (67–130 GPU-hours per dataset on a single RTX 4090), dependence on a single MLLM backbone (MMICL), and the binary True/False output restriction that limits expressiveness. Open questions include whether the curvature effect generalizes across different MLLM architectures, how performance scales to datasets with thousands of classes, and whether the approach remains effective under instance-dependent or class-dependent noise patterns beyond symmetric/asymmetric settings.
