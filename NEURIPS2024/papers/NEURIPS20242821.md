## Problem

Language models pretrained on general corpora fail to adequately memorize domain-specific knowledge that appears only rarely (long-tail) in the training data, making costly domain-specific pretraining seem indispensable. The paper identifies the root cause via Neural Tangent Kernel analysis: long-tail data exhibits low Gradient Consistency with the overall dataset, meaning its gradient direction diverges from the dominant update direction and is effectively ignored during standard pretraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242821/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw text sequences (Wikipedia articles with <8% legal/medical data mixed in to simulate long-tail distribution) and produces sentence-level embeddings (mean of all token embeddings in a sequence) for downstream classification tasks, alongside standard next-token prediction losses during pretraining. Dispatching to experts occurs at the sequence level rather than the token level, since the authors argue long-tail semantics are only observable at the sequence granularity. The CSE layer is inserted into the last 2 transformer layers of BERT or GPT, where inter-cluster distances in the embedding space are largest.

## Method

The Cluster-guided Sparse Expert (CSE) layer replaces standard FFN or attention modules in the last 2 layers with a sparse expert architecture guided by an online clustering algorithm. During a brief warm-up phase (<1% of training), a baseline dense model learns common features; then N sampled embeddings are clustered via DBSCAN to initialize cluster centers and radii. For each incoming sequence embedding, a Gaussian random projection (Johnson-Lindenstrauss) reduces dimensionality, and the embedding is dispatched to the expert whose cluster center is nearest in normalized distance (‖v′−c_j‖/r_j). Cluster centers are updated online with an exponential moving average to track the drifting embedding space. This separates long-tail domain data into dedicated experts, resolving the gradient conflict that causes long-tail knowledge to be overlooked, and the paper shows this single pretraining pass outperforms standard pretraining plus domain-specific continued pretraining on legal, medical, academic, environmental, and financial downstream tasks.

## Results

On GPT-based models, the proposed CSE method (MoF variant) achieves 75.90±0.19 average accuracy across five downstream tasks (Overruling, Casehold, GAD, EUADR, SST2), outperforming domain-specific pretrained GPT (73.57±0.17 for medical, 73.53±0.23 for legal) and Switch-MoE baselines (73.91±0.12, 73.86±0.23) by approximately 2–3%. On BERT-based models, CSE (MoF) reaches 74.16±0.20 average accuracy versus 71.07±0.22 for BERT/med and 69.58±0.20 for MoE/med. At the 330M scale trained on 20B tokens, CSE without fine-tuning outperforms fine-tuned baselines by 3–5% on domain-specific tasks (e.g., 40.11 vs. 16.83 on FPB financial task) while maintaining comparable general-task performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CSE against three baselines: (1) standard GPT with domain-specific continued pretraining (GPT/med, GPT/legal), (2) standard BERT with domain-specific continued pretraining (BERT/med, BERT/legal), and (3) Switch-MoE [Fedus et al.] with domain-specific continued pretraining (MoE/med, MoE/legal). CSE (MoF) outperforms GPT/med by 2.33 points and GPT/legal by 2.37 points in average accuracy on GPT, and outperforms BERT/med by 3.09 points and MoE/med by 4.58 points on BERT. The Switch-MoE baseline, which uses learned routing rather than cluster-guided dispatch, is consistently outperformed by CSE by 1.0–4.6 points depending on the base model, demonstrating that the cluster-guided routing strategy is more effective than standard MoE routing for capturing long-tail domain knowledge. Notably, CSE achieves these gains without any domain-specific pretraining phase, whereas all baselines require an additional continued-pretraining step on domain data, and the domain-specific pretraining baselines also suffer from catastrophic forgetting on unrelated domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Switch-MoE sparse expert architecture (Fedus et al., 2022) but replaces learned gating with a cluster-guided routing strategy based on online DBSCAN clustering in a Johnson-Lindenstrauss-reduced embedding space. The theoretical motivation draws on Neural Tangent Kernel analysis (Jacot et al., 2018; Bowman & Montufar, 2022) to formalize why long-tail data receives insufficient gradient updates, introducing a Gradient Consistency metric to quantify this misalignment. It positions itself against the dominant domain-specific pretraining paradigm (Gururangan et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020) and the long-tail learning literature (Kandpal et al., 2023), arguing that the root cause of poor domain performance is not missing data but gradient conflict during initial pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central claim—that a single pretraining pass with cluster-guided expert routing can replace an entire domain-specific pretraining phase—is practically significant if it holds at scale, as it would eliminate costly, expertise-dependent secondary training stages. However, the evidence is constrained: experiments use models up to 330M parameters, simulate long-tail distributions by mixing <8% domain data into Wikipedia rather than using truly massive corpora, and evaluate primarily on legal and medical domains. Open questions include whether CSE scales to multi-billion-parameter LLMs, how it performs when the target domain is entirely absent from pretraining data, and whether the 2-layer CSE insertion remains sufficient as model depth and vocabulary size grow.
