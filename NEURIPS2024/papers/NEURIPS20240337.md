## Problem

Multiple Choice Learning (MCL) trains multiple hypotheses via Winner-takes-all (WTA), a greedy scheme that updates only the winning hypothesis per target. This greediness causes hypothesis collapse and convergence to arbitrarily suboptimal local minima, yet unlike K-means where extensive remedies exist, the conditional and gradient-based MCL setting has received little attention. The paper asks whether deterministic annealing can be adapted to this conditional, gradient-based setting to restore exploration of the hypothesis space.

## Input / Output

For the UCI regression benchmark, input is a tabular feature vector x and output is n=5 hypothesis predictions f_k(x) plus scores γ_k(x) that together form a discrete mixture model approximating the conditional density p(y|x). For speech separation on WSJ0-mix, input is a time-domain audio mixture and output is n separated source estimates, with the MCL hypotheses replacing the permutation-invariant matching step. In both settings the hypotheses are trained to provide an efficient quantization of the conditional distribution rather than a single point estimate.

## Method

aMCL replaces the hard min in WTA's Voronoi assignment with a softmin (Boltzmann distribution) parameterized by a decreasing temperature schedule T(t), alternating between computing the soft assignment q_T(f_k|x,y) and taking a gradient step on the annealed WTA loss with stop_gradient on the assignment. Theoretically, this is equivalent to entropy-constrained block coordinate descent on the soft distortion, where each hypothesis moves toward a soft barycenter weighted by the Boltzmann assignment, with update speed proportional to the assigned probability mass. The training trajectory follows the conditional rate-distortion curve: at high temperature all hypotheses merge at the conditional mean, and as temperature cools they undergo phase transitions at critical temperatures (T_0^c = 2λ_max(C(x))) where they iteratively bifurcate into subgroups until reaching the Shannon Lower Bound. This provides a principled, input-dependent generalization of deterministic annealing that mitigates WTA's greediness while remaining straightforward to implement with standard gradient-based optimizers.

## Results

On the UCI regression benchmark (10 datasets), aMCL achieves the best distortion on 7 out of 10 datasets, outperforming vanilla MCL (e.g., 4.46 vs 4.82 on Year, 0.77 vs 0.80 on Protein) and Relaxed-WTA (e.g., 4.46 vs 9.09 on Year, 0.77 vs 1.67 on Protein). On RMSE, aMCL is competitive with Deep Ensembles and MC Dropout while consistently outperforming MCL (e.g., 4.25 vs 4.39 on Protein, 8.00e-4 vs 2.08e-3 on Naval). On speech separation (WSJ0-mix), aMCL achieves 16.85±0.13 SI-SDR (2 speakers) and 10.00±0.21 (3 speakers), matching PIT (16.88±0.10 and 10.01±0.04) while showing lower inter-seed variance than MCL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla MCL (Letzelter et al., NeurIPS 2023), aMCL wins on distortion for 7/10 UCI datasets and on RMSE for most, with the largest gains on Year (4.46 vs 4.82 distortion) and Naval (5.37e-7 vs 1.84e-6). Against Relaxed-WTA (Rupprecht et al., ICCV 2017), aMCL dominates on distortion across nearly all datasets (e.g., 4.46 vs 9.09 on Year), while Relaxed-WTA retains a slight RMSE advantage on some datasets due to its barycenter bias. Against standard uncertainty baselines (PBP, MC Dropout, Deep Ensembles from Lakshminarayanan et al., NeurIPS 2017), aMCL is competitive on RMSE—beating them on Protein (4.25 vs 4.73/4.36/4.71) and Energy (1.35 vs 1.80/1.66/2.09)—though slightly trailing on Year (9.08 vs 8.88/8.85/8.89). On speech separation, aMCL nearly matches PIT (Kolbæk et al., 2017) at 16.85 vs 16.88 SI-SDR (2 speakers) and 10.00 vs 10.01 (3 speakers), while reducing algorithmic complexity from O(m³) to O(mn) and showing lower inter-seed variance than MCL (±0.13 vs ±0.59 for 2 speakers).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Multiple Choice Learning (MCL) by replacing the hard Winner-takes-all assignment with a temperature-controlled softmin, drawing directly on deterministic annealing theory from Rose et al. (1990, 1992) that was previously applied to non-conditional clustering and vector quantization. The authors are explicit that they are the first to adapt this technique to the conditional, gradient-based WTA setting, and they build on the score-based MCL formulation of Letzelter et al. (2023) and the Relaxed-WTA variant of Rupprecht et al. (2017). The theoretical analysis connects the training trajectory to rate-distortion curves and phase transitions, leveraging the statistical physics framework established in the deterministic annealing literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

aMCL addresses two persistent practical failures of MCL—hypothesis collapse and convergence to suboptimal local minima—with a theoretically grounded fix that is straightforward to implement (replacing min with softmin plus a temperature schedule). The rate-distortion and phase-transition analysis provides a principled understanding of *why* annealing helps, which is valuable for the broader community working on multi-hypothesis prediction. Key open questions remain: the temperature schedule is a sensitive hyperparameter tightly coupled to the optimizer, slow cooling increases training time, and convergence guarantees at finite temperature are not yet established. The authors also note that generalization to out-of-distribution samples and the derivation of performance bounds are left for future work.
