## Problem

While prior work has shown that a one-layer linear transformer can be manually constructed to implement one-step gradient descent for OLS in-context, it remains unclear whether practical non-convex training dynamics actually converge to this ideal mesa-optimizer, and what data distributions the resulting mesa-optimizer can successfully learn. This paper rigorously answers both questions for autoregressively trained transformers, going beyond the diagonal-structure and fixed-initial-token assumptions of prior loss-landscape analyses.

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt sequence (x_1, ..., x_t) of d-dimensional complex vectors generated by a first-order AR process x_{t+1} = Wx_t, where W is a diagonal unitary matrix sampled uniformly and x_1 is drawn from a controllable distribution. Each token is embedded as e_i = (0_d, x_i, x_{i-1})^T ∈ C^{3d}, and the prediction for x_{t+1} is extracted as the first d coordinates of the linear attention output. The training objective is the population next-token prediction loss summed over all positions t = 2 to T−1, with expectation over both the initial token and the transition matrix W.

## Method

The paper analyzes the gradient flow (ODE) of a one-layer linear causal self-attention transformer trained on a first-order AR process. Under a sufficient condition on the initial token distribution (i.i.d. zero-mean coordinates with finite 4th and 6th moments), they derive a closed-form ODE system for the two scalar parameters (a, b) governing the diagonal entries of W^{KQ}_{32} and W^{PV}_{12}, showing all off-diagonal gradients vanish. This ODE is equivalent to gradient flow on a non-convex surrogate objective ℓ̃(a,b) = (c₂ − c₁ab)²/(2c₁), for which they prove the Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality, guaranteeing global convergence to the mesa-optimizer that implements one step of gradient descent for OLS. They further characterize the capability limitation: the mesa-optimizer recovers the true distribution if and only if a stronger moment condition holds, which common distributions (Gaussian, Gamma, Poisson) fail to satisfy—e.g., with Gaussian initial tokens the prediction converges to x_{T_te}/5 rather than the true next token. Finally, they prove that when the sufficient condition is violated (e.g., fixed x_1 = 1_d), the trained transformer does not perform vanilla gradient descent in general.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical. Under Assumption 4.1 (i.i.d. zero-mean coordinates with finite moments), the gradient flow of a one-layer linear causal self-attention transformer converges to a mesa-optimizer implementing one step of gradient descent for an OLS problem (Theorem 4.1), with the product of key parameters converging to κ₁/(κ₂ + κ₃/(T−2)·Σ 1/(t−1)). For Gaussian initial tokens N(0, σ²I), the mesa-optimizer's prediction converges to only 1/5 of the true next token (Proposition 4.1), demonstrating a fundamental capability limitation. For sparse one-hot initial tokens (Example 4.1), the mesa-optimizer exactly recovers the true distribution (Theorem 4.2). Simulations with T_tr=100, d=5, 10k sequences confirm: ab converges to 1/(5σ²) for Gaussian and 1/c² for sparse, with the prediction ratio ŷ/x → 1/5 (Gaussian) or ŷ → x (sparse).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional baseline comparisons. The key comparison is against the few-shot ICL theory for linear regression [24, 26], which proves that one-step gradient descent by a trained transformer can in-context learn linear regression with Gaussian inputs N(0, σ²I). In contrast, this paper shows that in the AR pretraining setting, the same one-step GD mesa-optimizer fundamentally fails for Gaussian initial tokens (prediction is only 1/5 of the truth), establishing that ICL by AR pretraining is qualitatively different from ICL by few-shot pretraining. Compared to [16] (von Oswald et al.), which manually constructs a transformer implementing one-step GD for OLS, this paper proves that practical non-convex gradient flow dynamics actually converge to that construction under Assumption 4.1, without requiring the diagonal structure imposed in [17]. Compared to [17] (Sander et al.), which only characterizes the loss landscape under a fixed x₁=1_d and full diagonal parameter constraint, this paper analyzes the actual training dynamics, removes the diagonal constraint on W^{KQ} and W^{PV}, and shows that without Assumption 4.1 the transformer does NOT perform vanilla gradient descent (Proposition 4.2), a result not available in [17].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mesa-optimization analysis of von Oswald et al. [16] and Sander et al. [17] from loss-landscape characterization to actual non-convex training dynamics. While [16] constructed a theoretical example showing a linear attention layer can implement one-step GD for OLS, and [17] characterized the global minima under a diagonal structure assumption with a fixed initial token, this paper removes the explicit diagonal constraint and proves convergence of gradient flow under a mild moment condition on the data distribution. It also deliberately contrasts the AR pretraining setting with the more-studied few-shot linear regression ICL literature [24, 26], showing that the two settings yield fundamentally different mesa-optimizer capabilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous characterization of when mesa-optimization emerges in practical (non-convex) AR training dynamics, and identifies a sharp capability boundary: the learned one-step GD mesa-optimizer fails to recover the data distribution for Gaussian initial tokens, a common and natural setting. This negative result reveals a fundamental asymmetry between ICL via AR pretraining and ICL via few-shot regression, suggesting that the AR pretraining mechanism is strictly weaker than previously assumed. Open questions include whether deeper or multi-head architectures can overcome this limitation, and what the general (non-diagonal, non-i.i.d.) training dynamics look like beyond the analyzed regime.
