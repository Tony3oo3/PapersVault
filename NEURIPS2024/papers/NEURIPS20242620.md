## Problem

While catastrophic forgetting is well-documented in sequential learning, the training dynamics of over-parameterized networks in structured, cyclic environments—where a fixed set of documents repeats in the same order—remain largely unexplored. This paper asks whether large models can spontaneously recover from forgetting on a document before encountering it again, and what architectural and training factors govern this behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242620/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-trained transformer or CNN models (Pythia 160M–2.8B, GPT-2, Image GPT, ViT, VGG-19) and fine-tunes them on T documents (default 25) presented cyclically in a fixed random order, with M gradient steps at batch size 1 per document over E epochs. Language documents are truncated to C tokens (default 256); image inputs are resized to 224×224 for classification or tokenized to 1024-length sequences for causal image modeling. Outputs include shift-averaged per-document loss curves, a scalar recovery score (fraction of peak forgetting recovered before the next visit), prequential online loss, and heatmaps or PCA projections of gradients, weight residuals, and activations across the training cycle.

## Method

The core contribution is an empirical discovery: when sufficiently large over-parameterized networks are fine-tuned cyclically on a fixed document sequence, they exhibit anticipatory recovery—loss on a given document decreases before that document reappears, even though no two documents are ever co-presented in context. This behavior is emergent, requiring sufficient width (≥512 embedding dimension) and depth (≥8 transformer blocks), and is modulated by gradient steps per document, context length, number of learnable layers, and optimizer choice. The authors trace the mechanism to a separation between fast task-specific parameters that memorize each document and slow shared representations that gradually push adjacent task embeddings closer, producing a helical trajectory in weight space where the model state approaches its previous visit to a task before that task recurs. A minimalist linear toy model with a shared projection matrix and task-specific invertible mappings reproduces the phenomenon, confirming that circular self-organization of task representations is the essential mechanism. Prequential evaluation further shows that cyclic training outperforms random shuffling across all model sizes, indicating practical benefits of structured training for agents operating in quasi-cyclic environments.

## Results

In prequential evaluation (average online loss across epochs 2–5), cyclic fine-tuning with a fixed document ordering consistently outperforms random shuffling across all four Pythia model sizes. At 1B parameters, cyclic training achieves 1.03±0.03 average loss versus 1.51±0.04 for shuffling; at 2.8B, the gap widens to 1.34±0.06 vs. 1.79±0.06. The anticipatory recovery phenomenon—where loss on a document decreases before the document reappears—is an emergent behavior that appears only above ~410M parameters and strengthens with model scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is primarily a discovery of a training-dynamics phenomenon rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the central contrast is between cyclic (fixed-order) fine-tuning and random-shuffled fine-tuning under prequential evaluation. Cyclic training wins on average online loss at every model size tested (410M, 1B, 1.4B, 2.8B), with the advantage ranging from ~0.25 nats at 410M to ~0.45 nats at 2.8B. The paper also compares pre-trained versus randomly initialized models, finding that pre-training strengthens anticipatory recovery but is not strictly necessary—the effect appears in randomly initialized networks above 410M parameters. Across architecture families, the phenomenon is strongest in LLMs (Pythia, GPT-2) but also observed in Image GPT (causal image modeling), ViT, and VGG-19 (image classification), confirming generality beyond a single architecture. No external continual-learning baselines (e.g., EWC, iCaRL, knowledge distillation) are directly benchmarked; the contribution is the identification of anticipatory recovery as an emergent property of structured training in over-parameterized networks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends interleaved training [Mayo et al., 2023] to a much larger number of tasks and, in doing so, discovers a previously unobserved phenomenon: anticipatory recovery from catastrophic interference in cyclic training. It departs from standard continual learning (where each task is seen once) by introducing cyclic repetition, and from prior convergence-rate analyses of fixed-order SGD [Ahn et al., 2020; Mishchenko et al., 2020] by focusing on per-task loss dynamics within a cycle rather than aggregate convergence. The work also connects to the LLM emergent capabilities literature by showing that anticipatory recovery is an emergent property requiring sufficient model width and depth, and to the catastrophic forgetting literature by revealing a spontaneous recovery mechanism that requires no explicit regularization, replay, or architectural isolation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that fixed-order cyclic training outperforms random shuffling under prequential evaluation suggests a practical, zero-cost improvement for agents that encounter quasi-periodic task streams. However, the setup remains highly simplified (identical documents repeated in a fixed order, no distribution shift), and the mathematical foundation is incomplete—the toy model reproduces the effect but does not explain why LLMs and autoregressive objectives show the strongest recovery. Open questions include whether anticipatory recovery persists under realistic non-stationary data streams, multiscale temporal structure, and whether it can be deliberately amplified or controlled for deployment in embodied agents.
