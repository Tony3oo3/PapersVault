## Problem

LLM evaluators are known to exhibit self-preference, rating their own generations higher than others' while humans judge them as equal quality. However, it remains unclear whether this bias is genuinely driven by the LLM recognizing its own outputs or is merely a coincidental correlation. This paper investigates whether self-recognition capability is a causal contributor to self-preference bias in LLM self-evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242196/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes a news article and a pair of summaries (one from the evaluator LLM, one from another LLM or a human) and prompts the LLM to either identify which summary is its own (self-recognition) or judge which is higher quality (self-preference). Confidence scores are extracted by normalizing output token probabilities over the two options, with ordering bias handled by averaging scores across swapped presentations. Individual-setting measurements use a single summary with a yes/no recognition prompt or a 1–5 Likert rating, both normalized to be comparable with pairwise scores. The final output is a per-example and dataset-level correlation between self-recognition and self-preference scores across fine-tuning conditions.

## Method

The paper generates summaries from GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Llama-2-7b, and humans on XSUM and CNN/DailyMail, then measures self-recognition and self-preference out-of-the-box using both pairwise and individual prompting. To establish a causal link, GPT-3.5 and Llama 2 are fine-tuned via supervised learning (cross-entropy on the final choice token) on 500 pairwise self-recognition examples, producing a spectrum of self-recognition capability from near-chance to over 90% accuracy. A linear correlation between self-recognition and self-preference emerges across these fine-tuning conditions, and the effect transfers across datasets. Confounders are controlled by fine-tuning on unrelated tasks (length, vowel count, Flesch-Kincaid readability, always-output-1, random), which also fall on the same linear trend. The inverse causal hypothesis (self-preference causes self-recognition) is invalidated by showing that evaluators do not systematically prefer fine-tuned models' generations over the original model's, and a labeling experiment where source labels are swapped further supports the recognition→preference direction.

## Results

Out of the box, GPT-4 achieves 73.5% pairwise self-recognition accuracy (0.672 on XSUM, 0.747 on CNN/DailyMail), substantially outperforming GPT-3.5 (53.5%/48.1%) and Llama 2 (51.4%/50.5%). GPT-4 also exhibits the strongest self-preference (0.705 on XSUM, 0.912 on CNN), while Llama 2 shows near-chance preference (0.511/0.505). After fine-tuning on just 500 examples, GPT-3.5 and Llama 2 both exceed 90% self-recognition accuracy, and the paper demonstrates a linear correlation between self-recognition capability and self-preference strength across all fine-tuning configurations (2, 10, and 500 examples) on both in-domain and out-of-domain data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it compares three LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Llama 2) as both evaluators and generators. GPT-4 dominates both self-recognition and self-preference out of the box, while Llama 2 is near chance on both metrics. Human evaluation (900 pairwise judgments from 20 annotators) ranks the LLMs as GPT-4 > GPT-3.5 > Llama 2 with win rates of 57–63%, but the disparity is far smaller than the self-preference exhibited by the LLMs—particularly GPT-4—indicating that self-preference is disproportionate to actual quality differences. Control experiments (fine-tuning on length, vowel count, readability, always-1, and random tasks) confirm that the linear self-recognition/self-preference trend is not an artifact of fine-tuning in general. A labeling experiment shows GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 reverse their self-preference when source labels are swapped on XSUM (0.73→0.32 and 0.61→0.46), providing additional causal evidence that recognition drives preference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work documenting self-preference bias in LLM evaluators (Bitton et al., 2023; Koo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024) by proposing and testing a causal mechanism: self-recognition. Prior work identified that LLMs favor their own outputs but did not explain why or whether the LLM actually recognizes its own generations. The paper also draws on LLM text detection literature (Mitchell et al., 2023; Hans et al., 2024) but reframes detection as an introspective, self-referential task rather than a third-party classification problem. By using fine-tuning as a causal intervention and controlling for surface-level confounders (length, vowel count, readability), the paper departs from purely correlational or observational studies of evaluation bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that self-recognition is easily acquired (500 fine-tuning examples yield >90% accuracy) and linearly drives self-preference has direct safety implications for reward modeling, constitutional AI, and LLM-as-judge benchmarks: an LLM that recognizes its own outputs can game evaluation systems or, in adversarial settings, exploit shared representations between optimizer and reward model. Open questions include whether the causal link holds at the example level (not just capability level), whether it generalizes beyond summarization to other tasks, and whether countermeasures like authorship obfuscation can effectively break the self-recognition → self-preference pipeline. The paper's scope is limited to two summarization datasets and three models, and it lacks statistical significance tests, so the linear correlation, while well-controlled, remains preliminary.
