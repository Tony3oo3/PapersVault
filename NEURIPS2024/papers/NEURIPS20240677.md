## Problem

Prior transferability guarantees in regularized IRL require full access to expert policies and rely on a binary rank condition between transition laws, but in practice only finite demonstrations are available. The paper shows that this rank condition is insufficient under approximate optimality (Example 3.3), leaving open the question of what conditions on the relationship between expert environments guarantee that a reward learned from finite data transfers to new transition laws.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240677/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes K finite sets of expert trajectories (N^E episodes of length H^E per expert), the known transition laws P^k of each expert's environment, and a compact reward class R (the L1 unit ball in R^{S×A}). It outputs a reward r̂ ∈ R for which all K experts are ε̂-optimal, together with explicit ε-transferability bounds to a target set of transition laws P, where ε is parameterized by principal angles between the experts' potential shaping subspaces. The transferability guarantee is uniform: the same ε applies to every P in the target set.

## Method

The core contribution replaces the binary rank condition with principal angles between the potential shaping subspaces U_P = im(E − γP), yielding a continuous measure of how informative the difference between expert environments is. The key analytical bridge is Lemma 3.6, which bounds the Bregman divergence between optimal occupancy measures (equivalently, suboptimality) in terms of reward distances in the quotient space R^{S×A}/U, with constants η (strong convexity) and σ_R (dual strong convexity) of the occupancy measure regularizer. For K=2 experts, Theorem 3.10 shows the second principal angle θ_2(P^0, P^1) directly controls the transferability error to all transition laws, recovering the rank condition as the θ_2 > 0 special case; for K=1, Theorem 3.11 gives local transferability controlled by the maximal principal angle. The PAC algorithm (Algorithm 1) performs projected gradient descent on the sum of expert suboptimalities using a forward RL oracle, converging in O(K²/ε̂²) iterations with O(K/ε̂²) demonstrations per expert.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: Theorem 3.10 establishes that for two experts, the transferability error scales as ε = ε̂ / [ησ_R sin(θ₂(P⁰,P¹)/2)²], where θ₂ is the second principal angle between the experts' transition laws. Theorem 4.1 provides a PAC guarantee requiring Ω(K²/ε̂²) iterations and Ω(K log(|S||A|/δ̂)/ε̂²) expert demonstrations per expert. In the WindyGridworld validation (Section 5), the second principal angle between North- and East-wind experts increases monotonically with wind strength β ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0}, and both the reward distance ‖[r̂]₁ − [r^E]₁‖₂ and the transfer SubOpt to South-wind and shifted-action environments decrease correspondingly, confirming the theoretical prediction. In the failure case of Example 3.3, a reward that is nearly optimal for both experts (SubOpt = O(β)) still yields SubOpt ≈ 4.81 in a new environment, demonstrating that the binary rank condition alone is insufficient.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing IRL or transfer-learning baselines in a head-to-head empirical sense; it is primarily a theoretical contribution with a validation experiment. The 'comparison' is between the paper's principal-angle-based sufficient conditions and the prior binary rank condition (Eq. 5) from Rolland et al. [2022] and Schlaginhaufen & Kamgarpour [2023]. Example 3.3 concretely shows the prior rank condition is satisfied (rank = 2|S|−1 for any β > 0) yet transferability fails (SubOpt ≈ 4.81), whereas the paper's Theorem 3.10 correctly predicts poor transfer when sin(θ₂) = O(β) is small. The WindyGridworld experiments (Figure 2) validate that larger second principal angles (achieved by increasing wind strength β) yield better transfer, matching the 1/sin²(θ₂/2) scaling predicted by Theorem 3.10. The paper also compares Shannon vs. Tsallis-1/2 entropy regularization (Corollary 3.13), showing Tsallis avoids an exponential dependence on the effective horizon H_γ in the dual strong convexity constant σ_R, making transferability guarantees tighter for long-horizon problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the identifiability and transferability analysis of Schlaginhaufen & Kamgarpour (2023) and Rolland et al. (2022) from the idealized full-policy-access setting to the practical finite-demonstration setting. The key conceptual departure is showing that the binary rank condition previously established as sufficient for transferability is actually insufficient when only approximate expert optimality is available, and replacing it with principal angles between transition-law subspaces as a more refined geometric measure. The PAC algorithm for multi-expert IRL generalizes the single-expert apprenticeship learning framework of Syed & Schapire (2007) to multiple experts with convergence in terms of expert suboptimality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first end-to-end theoretical guarantees for learning transferable rewards from finite expert demonstrations, bridging a gap between identifiability theory (which assumed oracle policy access) and practical IRL. The principal angles framework offers a more informative diagnostic than binary rank conditions, quantifying *how much* transferability one can expect rather than just whether it holds. Key limitations include: results are sufficient but not necessary conditions, constants in the transferability bounds can be very large (especially for Shannon entropy with long effective horizons), the analysis is restricted to discrete state-action spaces, and experimental validation is limited to a single gridworld environment. Open questions include extending to continuous spaces, characterizing necessary conditions, and validating on real-world tasks.
