## Problem

In multi-agent reinforcement learning, many algorithms use a 'win-stay, lose-shift' (satisficing) update rule where best-responding agents hold their strategy while unsatisfied agents may explore. A fundamental open question was whether, for any finite n-player general-sum normal-form game and any initial strategy profile, a satisficing path to a Nash equilibrium always exists. This was previously established only for two-player games and n-player symmetric Markov games, leaving the general case unresolved.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite n-player normal-form game (finite action sets per player and reward functions) together with an arbitrary initial mixed strategy profile as input, and produces a finite-length sequence of strategy profiles (a satisficing path) of length at most n that terminates at a Nash equilibrium. The path is constrained so that at every step, any player who is currently best-responding must retain its strategy, while unsatisfied players may switch to any strategy in their simplex. The construction is semi-constructive: it specifies which set the next profile must belong to (e.g., Worse(x_t)) but does not provide an efficient algorithm for computing the specific strategy.

## Method

The proof constructs the satisficing path in two phases. First, it iteratively selects the next profile from Worse(x_t) — the set of accessible strategies that keep all previously unsatisfied players unsatisfied while flipping at least one previously satisfied player to unsatisfied — thereby strictly growing the unsatisfied set until either all players are unsatisfied (trivially reaching equilibrium in one step) or Worse(x_k) is empty. In the non-trivial second case, it fixes the strategies of satisfied players, solves the resulting subgame for the unsatisfied players to obtain a Nash equilibrium, and extends it to a full profile x_★. The key technical step (Lemma 1) shows x_★ is a limit point of NoBetter(x_k) via a proof by contradiction that exploits the multi-linearity of expected rewards: a one-parameter family of fully mixed strategies yields an indifference condition that is a polynomial in the parameter, and the fundamental theorem of algebra forces the polynomial to be identically zero, producing the contradiction. Continuity of the auxiliary functions F^i then certifies that previously satisfied players remain satisfied at x_★, making it a full Nash equilibrium. The counterintuitive insight is that reward-deteriorating updates (deliberately making satisfied players unsatisfied) are essential to breaking cycles and driving play to equilibrium.

## Results

The paper's main result is Theorem 1: any finite n-player normal-form game has the satisficing paths property, meaning that from any initial strategy profile there exists a satisficing path of finite length terminating at a Nash equilibrium. The proof is semi-constructive and shows the path length is uniformly bounded above by T(x₁) ≤ n. The construction is counterintuitive: rather than improving the performance of unsatisfied players, the path is built by strategically increasing the number of unsatisfied players at each step, leveraging the freedom afforded to unsatisfied agents to explore. The result extends prior affirmative answers for two-player normal-form games [19] and n-player symmetric Markov games [52] to the general class of n-player general-sum normal-form games.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative baselines. The result is positioned relative to prior theoretical work: [19] (Foster & Young, 2006) established the satisficing paths property for two-player normal-form games, and [52] (Yongacoglu et al., 2023) extended it to n-player symmetric Markov games. Theorem 1 removes the symmetry and player-count restrictions, covering all finite n-player general-sum normal-form games. The paper also clarifies that its result does not contradict the impossibility theorems of Hart & Mas-Colell [22] or Milionis et al. [38], since the update functions constructed in the proof need not be continuous or uncoupled. The authors note the proof is semi-constructive and that computing a Nash equilibrium in one step (as in Case 1) is computationally prohibitive [15], underscoring that this is an existence result rather than a computational prescription.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper resolves an open question posed by Yongacoglu et al. [52], who formalized satisficing paths and proved the property for two-player normal-form games and n-player symmetric Markov games. The core idea traces back to Foster and Young's [19] regret testing algorithm, where the two-player satisficing paths property was established implicitly. The key departure from prior work is the proof technique: rather than improving the performance of unsatisfied players (as in best-response or no-regret dynamics), the construction deliberately grows the set of unsatisfied players via reward-deteriorating updates, leveraging the freedom that the satisficing constraint grants to non-optimizing agents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result establishes that uncoordinated, distributed random search can in principle drive any finite normal-form game to Nash equilibrium along a satisficing path, removing a theoretical obstacle for a broad class of MARL algorithms. The counterintuitive finding that reward-deteriorating updates are essential to the construction suggests a new design principle: alternating between conventional reward-improving phases and suboptimal exploration phases to break cycles. However, the proof is semi-constructive and the required update functions are discontinuous and game-dependent, so translating this into practical algorithms remains open. Extension to multi-state Markov games is also unresolved, as the key Lemma 1 relies on a polynomial structure of indifference conditions that breaks down in the stateful setting.
