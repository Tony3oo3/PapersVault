## Problem

SGD performs significantly worse than Adam on Transformers, yet the reason remains unclear. The full Hessian spectrum of CNNs and Transformers is largely similar, so it cannot explain the optimizer performance gap. The paper asks: what fine-grained Hessian property distinguishes architectures where SGD fails from those where it succeeds?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244189/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained or initialized neural network with its default PyTorch parameter block partition (e.g., Embedding, Query, Key, Value, MLP layers) and computes the blockwise Hessian spectra via Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature (SLQ), requiring only Hessian-vector products. The primary output is the averaged Jensen-Shannon distance (JS⁰) across all pairs of blockwise eigenvalue densities, a scalar computed before training that quantitatively predicts whether SGD will underperform Adam. A simplified SLQ variant (num_v=1, m=10, 50% block sampling, batch size 32) reduces computation to under 0.001s for ResNet18 while preserving the heterogeneity signal.

## Method

The paper identifies 'block heterogeneity'—large differences in Hessian eigenvalue spectra across parameter blocks—as the key phenomenon explaining why SGD underperforms Adam on Transformers. Empirically, blockwise Hessian spectra in Transformers (GPT2, BERT, ViT) are dramatically different across blocks, while CNNs (ResNet18, VGG16) show near-identical spectra across their convolution layers; this heterogeneity correlates with SGD's failure across Transformers, MLP-mixer, and a man-made MLP with tunable heterogeneity. Theoretically, on block-diagonal quadratic problems with matched condition number, GD has complexity Õ(κ) while Adam (with β₂=1) achieves Õ(r·max_l κ_l), where the improvement arises because Adam's diagonal preconditioner assigns coordinate-wise learning rates that handle per-block spectral differences, whereas GD's single learning rate cannot. The paper further shows that block heterogeneity attenuates during pre-training, explaining why SGD becomes more viable for fine-tuning, and proposes JS⁰ as a pre-training diagnostic metric for optimizer selection.

## Results

The paper introduces JS⁰ (average Jensen-Shannon distance between blockwise Hessian spectra at initialization) as a quantitative predictor of SGD's incompetence. CNNs exhibit very low heterogeneity (JS⁰ ≈ 0.09–0.10 for VGG16 and ResNet18), while Transformers show dramatically higher values (JS⁰ = 18.84 for pretrained GPT2, 53.38 for BERT, 83.23 for GPT2, 286.41 for ViT-base). The performance gap between SGD and Adam grows monotonically with JS⁰: on CNNs SGD matches Adam, but on Transformers SGD lags by a significant margin even after careful learning-rate tuning. On quadratic models with identical condition number (κ=5000), GD is ~5× slower than Adam on heterogeneous blocks but on par on homogeneous blocks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SGD (with momentum) against Adam/AdamW across seven architectures. On CNNs (ResNet18, VGG16 on ImageNet), SGD and Adam achieve comparable final test accuracy, confirming that block homogeneity (JS⁰ ≈ 0.1) does not disadvantage SGD. On all four Transformers examined (ViT-base, BERT, GPT2-nano, GPT2), SGD performs significantly worse than AdamW even after grid-searching learning rates, with the gap widening as JS⁰ increases. On MLP-mixer (an all-MLP architecture with JS⁰ = 34.90), SGD also lags behind Adam, demonstrating that the effect is not specific to attention mechanisms. In the controlled quadratic experiments (all with κ = 5000), GD is on par with Adam on homogeneous blocks (Cases 2 and 4) but largely lags behind Adam on heterogeneous blocks (Cases 1 and 3), establishing a causal link between block heterogeneity and SGD's underperformance. The theoretical analysis shows GD has complexity Õ(κ) while Adam achieves Õ(r · max_l κ_l), which can be ~5× smaller than κ in heterogeneous settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature (SLQ) method [7] for Hessian spectrum estimation and the classical finding that neural network Hessians are near-block-diagonal [18], but applies these tools at a finer granularity—examining the spectrum of each principal block rather than the full Hessian. It departs from prior explanations of the SGD–Adam gap on Transformers (heavy-tailed noise [105], unbounded smoothness [104], sign-descent equivalence [46, 91]) by identifying a structural property of the loss landscape (block heterogeneity) rather than a property of the data or noise. The theoretical analysis extends the classical condition-number-based complexity of GD by showing that Adam's diagonal preconditioner can reduce the relevant complexity from the global condition number κ to a function of per-block condition numbers, a distinction not previously formalized in the Adam convergence literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, pre-training diagnostic (the JS⁰ metric) that can predict whether SGD will underperform Adam, which is practically valuable for new architectures (e.g., Mamba) where optimizer choice is uncertain. The finding that block heterogeneity attenuates after pre-training partially explains why simpler methods work for fine-tuning, connecting to the broader question of what makes pre-trained weights 'good.' Key open questions include: whether the theoretical bound (Theorem 1) can be tightened by exploiting additional structure in H_l, whether the heterogeneity-reduction phenomenon is universal across architectures, and whether the block-diagonal Hessian assumption holds at scale. The theory is currently limited to quadratic models with exactly block-diagonal Hessians, so extending it to the near-block-diagonal, non-convex regime of real networks remains a significant gap.
