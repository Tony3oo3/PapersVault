## Problem

Theoretical understanding of in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) for pre-trained transformers has been largely confined to single-agent settings such as multi-armed bandits and MDPs. This paper asks whether pre-trained transformers can provably learn to play competitive multi-agent games—specifically, whether they can approximate Nash equilibrium in two-player zero-sum Markov games in an in-context manner without any parameter updates during inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244195/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the decentralized setting, each player's transformer receives only its own observed trajectory (states, own actions, rewards) plus the current state, embedded as one-hot vectors with positional encodings; the opponent's actions are entirely invisible. The output is a probability distribution over that player's action space, extracted via a fixed linear projection of the last transformer output token onto the probability simplex. In the centralized setting, a single transformer receives the full joint trajectory and outputs a joint action distribution over both players' action spaces, with the final policy pair obtained by uniform sampling across episodes.

## Method

The paper establishes a three-part theoretical framework for each learning setting: (1) a supervised pre-training guarantee bounding the TV distance between the pre-trained transformer and the context algorithm via covering numbers and approximate realizability; (2) a constructional result proving that a specific transformer parameterization exactly realizes V-learning (decentralized) or the MWU-version of VI-ULCB (centralized); and (3) a finite-sample NE approximation bound combining both. The key technical challenge is realizing model-free algorithms like V-learning, where value computation at each step depends on previously computed values rather than raw inputs, requiring recursive transformer constructions that go beyond the straightforward attention-based value lookups used for model-based designs. In the centralized setting, the exact general-sum NE solver (PPAD-complete) is replaced by an approximate CCE solver via multiplicative weight updates, which the transformer's MLP layers can also implement. The decentralized NE gap scales with max(A,B) rather than AB, evidencing the structural benefit of not observing the opponent's actions.

## Results

The paper's primary results are theoretical: Theorem 3.5 establishes that in the decentralized setting, the NE gap is bounded by Õ(√(H⁵S(A∨B)log(SABT)/G) + THS√(log(TSN_Θ/δ)/N)), while Theorem 4.2 gives a centralized bound of Õ(√(H⁴S²AB·log(SABT)/G) + THS√(log(TSN_Θ/δ)/N)). Empirically, on two-player zero-sum normal-form games (H=1) and Markov games (H=2), pre-trained transformers with N=20 context games achieve a gradually decaying NE gap that closely tracks the context algorithm (EXP3 or VI-ULCB), whereas N=10 games yield noticeably worse performance, validating the √(1/N) pre-training data dependency.

**Table: Main theoretical NE-gap guarantees (Theorems 3.5 and 4.2).**

| Setting | Context Algorithm | NE Gap Upper Bound (w.p. ≥ 1−δ) |
|---|---|---|
| Decentralized | V-learning | Õ(√(H⁵S(A∨B)·log(SABT)/G)) + Õ(THS·√(log(TSN_Θ/δ)/N)) |
| Centralized | VI-ULCB (MWU) | Õ(√(H⁴S²AB·log(SABT)/G)) + Õ(THS·√(log(TSN_Θ/δ)/N)) |

*Note: The decentralized bound depends on (A∨B) rather than AB, evidencing the benefit of decentralized learning. The second term in both bounds vanishes as pre-training games N → ∞.*

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares pre-trained transformers against two context-algorithm baselines: EXP3 (a one-step V-learning variant) in the decentralized setting and VI-ULCB in the centralized setting. In both cases, when the number of pre-training games is sufficient (N=20), the transformer's NE gap over episodes closely matches that of the corresponding context algorithm, confirming that the transformer has effectively internalized the game-playing strategy. With fewer pre-training games (N=10), the transformer's NE gap is consistently larger, empirically validating the √(1/N) sample-complexity term in the theoretical bounds. The paper does not report a scenario where the transformer outperforms the context algorithm; rather, the goal is to demonstrate that the transformer can replicate the context algorithm's performance in an in-context (parameter-free) manner, which it achieves. The decentralized setting shows a theoretical advantage over centralized in the dependence on action-space size (A∨B vs. AB), though this distinction is not directly isolated in the small-scale experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Lin et al. [38], which established provable in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) for single-agent bandits and MDPs by constructing transformers to realize LinUCB, Thompson sampling, and UCB-VI. Here, the framework is broadened from single-agent decision-making to two-player zero-sum Markov games, with the key technical challenge being the realization of multi-agent algorithms—decentralized V-learning (model-free) and centralized VI-ULCB (model-based)—within the transformer architecture. The work also draws on the broader ICL theory literature (Ahn et al., Akyürek et al., Bai et al.) and the competitive multi-agent RL literature (Bai & Jin, Jin et al.) for the game-solving algorithms that the transformers must emulate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical treatment of in-context game-playing, establishing that pre-trained transformers can approximate Nash equilibrium in competitive multi-agent settings without parameter updates—a capability with direct relevance to practical game-playing agents. The demonstration that transformers can realize both model-based (VI-ULCB) and model-free (V-learning) algorithms is a meaningful step beyond prior ICRL theory, which was confined to model-based single-agent designs. Key open questions include extending to cooperative and mixed cooperative-competitive games, incorporating function approximation beyond the tabular setting, understanding whether the augmented pre-training data is truly necessary, and scaling experiments to practically relevant game sizes.
