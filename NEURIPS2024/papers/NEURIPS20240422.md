## Problem

While prior work demonstrated that transformers implement emergent optimization algorithms for in-context learning on continuous linear regression tasks, the mechanisms underlying ICL and skill composition on discrete, finite tasks remain unexplored. This paper asks: how does a transformer transition from memorizing a small set of modular arithmetic tasks to algorithmically solving entirely new (out-of-distribution) tasks, and what internal representations and algorithms enable this transition?

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of k in-context triples (x_i, y_i, z_i) followed by a query pair (x, y), where each z_i = a·x_i + b·y_i mod p for some hidden task vector (a,b) ∈ Z_p². The task vector is never explicitly provided; the model must infer it from the in-context labels. The model predicts only the z tokens (every third position in the sequence), operating over a finite alphabet of p symbols (default p=29). Evaluation is decomposed into four sets crossing seen/unseen task vectors with seen/unseen input pairs, enabling separate measurement of in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization.

## Method

The paper introduces a structured algorithmic dataset of p² linear modular functions with a rectangular-rule task selection and balanced batch design (equal representation of all tasks per batch, aligned input sequences across tasks) that is critical for enabling multi-task learning. GPT-style transformers (depth d=2,4,6; H=4 heads; d_embed=512; RoPE; ReLU) are trained via modified next-token prediction on sequences of up to 32 in-context examples. The key empirical finding is a four-phase diagram governed by the number of pre-training tasks n_i.d. and the fraction of visible examples α: increasing task diversity drives a transition from in-distribution memorization through in-distribution generalization to out-of-distribution generalization, with the generalizing phase being transient in deeper models. Interpretability analysis reveals that attention heads in the first layer implement a 'modular map' (Skill I) producing circle-of-circles PCA structures in log-annotated space, while MLPs implement multiplication (Skill II) and addition (Skill III) over GF(p). The models implement two distinct inference algorithms: Ratio Matching (finding a single in-context example whose (x,y) is a scalar multiple of the query) for few shots, and Modular Regression (finding coefficients c_i such that Σc_i(x_i,y_i)=(x,y) mod p) for many shots, with deeper models exhibiting an algorithmic shift between the two as context length increases.

## Results

On modular arithmetic tasks (z = ax + by mod p, p=29), a GPT-style transformer with d=6 blocks achieves out-of-distribution generalization only when the number of pre-training tasks n_i.d. ≥ 256, with a threshold accuracy of 75% used to define phase boundaries. The minimum model depth capable of o.o.d. generalization is d=2, though the d=4 model achieves near-perfect o.o.d. accuracy via the Modular Regression algorithm at ~10 in-context shots, while the d=2 model is limited to the sub-optimal Ratio Matching algorithm. For d=6 models at n_i.d. = 2^8, the o.o.d. generalization phase is transient: accuracy rises then degrades during training, necessitating early stopping. The d=4 model exhibits a clear algorithmic shift from Ratio Matching (few shots) to Modular Regression (many shots), a transition absent in the d=2 model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, it ablates model depth (d=2, 4, 6) and task diversity (n_i.d. from 2^4 to 2^10) to characterize the emergence of in-context learning. The d=6 model overfits to memorization at high n_i.d. due to excess capacity, while d=4 strikes the best balance between memorization and generalization, maintaining a 1-shot loss surge followed by a notable drop in ICL loss. The d=2 model, with the lowest capacity, skips the in-distribution generalization phase entirely and jumps from memorization directly to o.o.d. generalization, but only implements Ratio Matching rather than the superior Modular Regression algorithm. Across all depths, increasing n_i.d. improves in-context sample efficiency (fewer shots needed for generalization), and the transient nature of ICL (observed in d=6 and d=4 at intermediate n_i.d.) disappears at higher task diversity or with early stopping.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the modular arithmetic task from Power et al. (Grokking, 2022) into the in-context learning setting, and parallels the task-diversity-driven ICL emergence studied by Raventos et al. and Ahn et al. on continuous linear regression. The key conceptual departure is that, unlike the regression setting where transformers implement an emergent first-order optimization method, the discrete modular arithmetic case yields a qualitatively different solution: the model first memorizes individual tasks and then composes them via algorithmic skill combination (Ratio Matching, Modular Regression) rather than performing gradient descent on an emergent loss. The structured task selection (rectangular rule) and balanced batch design are practical contributions that make multi-task modular arithmetic learnable at all.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, controlled testbed for dissecting how skill composition gives rise to out-of-distribution ICL, revealing a four-phase transition diagram and identifying the specific algorithms (Ratio Matching → Modular Regression) that deeper models implement as in-context examples increase. The finding that o.o.d. generalization is transient in deeper models (requiring early stopping) has practical implications for training protocols. However, the work is confined to small models (≤6 layers) on toy algorithmic tasks, and the authors explicitly note that white-box interpretability of deeper models remains intractable; it is an open question whether the identified mechanisms (circle-of-circles representations, algorithmic shifts) carry over to realistic language models.
