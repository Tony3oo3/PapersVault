## Problem

3D occupancy prediction for autonomous driving has relied almost exclusively on LiDAR or camera inputs, both of which degrade significantly in adverse weather (fog, rain, snow). While 4D imaging radar offers all-weather robustness, prior radar-based perception methods use sparse point clouds that discard weak reflections from low-reflectivity surfaces (e.g., asphalt roads), making them unsuitable for the dense, whole-scene prediction that occupancy requires. The full 4D radar tensor (4DRT) preserves these signals but is voluminous (~500 MB/frame), inherently noisy from multi-path effects, and stored in spherical coordinates that mismatch the Cartesian occupancy grid.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single-frame 4D radar tensor of shape 256×107×37×64 (range × azimuth × elevation × Doppler bins) in spherical coordinates, rather than the conventional sparse radar point cloud. It produces a 128×128×14 voxel occupancy volume at 0.4 m resolution covering a 51.2 m × 51.2 m × 5.6 m region of interest, with each voxel classified as free, background, or foreground. The spherical-to-Cartesian coordinate mismatch between input and output is a central design constraint that the architecture must resolve without introducing interpolation errors.

## Method

RadarOcc processes the 4DRT through four stages. First, data volume reduction encodes the Doppler axis into an 8-channel descriptor (top-3 power values with indices, mean, standard deviation) and applies sidelobe-aware spatial sparsification that selects the top-250 power elements per individual range rather than a global percentile, suppressing antenna sidelobe artifacts. Second, spherical-based feature encoding operates directly in the range-azimuth-elevation voxel space using range-wise self-attention on the Doppler descriptors, sequential 3D sparse convolutions, and 3D deformable self-attention, avoiding any coordinate transformation. Third, spherical-to-Cartesian feature aggregation uses learnable Cartesian voxel queries whose 3D positions are mapped into spherical reference points, then applies deformable cross-attention to sample from the spherical feature volume—bypassing interpolation entirely. Finally, a multi-scale 3D convolutional decoder with FPN top-down merging and an MLP head predicts per-voxel occupancy probabilities, trained with cross-entropy, Lovász-softmax, and scene/class-wise affinity losses.

## Results

On the K-Radar well-condition test split, RadarOcc achieves 30.4% IoU and 22.6% mIoU at the 51.2 m range, outperforming both monocular (24.1% IoU, 14.7% mIoU) and stereo (25.4% IoU, 16.2% mIoU) camera-based SurroundOcc by 26.1% and 39.5% relative mIoU gain at 51.2 m, respectively. Against radar point-cloud baselines, RadarOcc improves mIoU by 39.4–56.9% over L-CONet (RPC) and by 28.6–29.1% over L-baseline (4DRT-XYZ) across the three evaluation ranges. At 12.8 m, RadarOcc reaches 48.8% IoU and 34.3% mIoU, approaching the 16-beam LiDAR baseline (49.1% IoU, 39.0% mIoU) while remaining below 32-beam (51.1% IoU) and 64-beam (56.9% IoU) LiDAR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RadarOcc (4DRT input) decisively outperforms both camera-based SurroundOcc variants: at 51.2 m it gains +7.4 mIoU over stereo (22.6 vs 16.2) and +7.9 mIoU over monocular (22.6 vs 14.7), with FG IoU more than doubling (15.8 vs 7.3 stereo, 5.7 mono). Against radar point-cloud baselines (Table 1), RadarOcc improves mIoU@51.2m from 14.4% (L-CONet, RPC) to 22.6% and FG IoU@51.2m from 3.1% to 15.8%, confirming that the dense 4DRT format preserves low-reflectivity signals lost during CFAR-based point cloud extraction. Compared to the 4DRT-XYZ baseline (which converts the tensor to Cartesian before processing), RadarOcc gains +5.1 mIoU and +8.6 FG IoU at 51.2 m, attributed to avoiding interpolation errors from spherical-to-Cartesian conversion. Against LiDAR, RadarOcc is competitive with 16-beam LiDAR (30.4 vs 35.2 IoU@51.2m) but trails 32-beam (34.9) and 64-beam (43.8) LiDAR, reflecting radar's inherently lower resolution and higher noise. The paper's key qualitative finding is that under adverse weather (rain, snow), RadarOcc maintains robust occupancy predictions while both LiDAR and camera baselines degrade significantly due to beam scattering and lens occlusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by being the first to apply 4D radar tensors (4DRTs) to 3D occupancy prediction, a task previously dominated by LiDAR point clouds and RGB cameras. It departs from the 'LiDAR-inspired' radar perception paradigm that relies on sparse point clouds (generated via CFAR), arguing that point cloud generation discards weak reflections critical for occupancy (e.g., asphalt surfaces). Methodologically, it repurposes existing components—deformable attention (Deformable DETR), sparse convolutions (SECOND), and polar/cylindrical representations—into a novel spherical-coordinate pipeline with Doppler bin descriptors and sidelobe-aware sparsification, while benchmarking against OpenOccupancy/CONet (LiDAR) and SurroundOcc (camera) baselines adapted to the K-Radar dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work establishes 4D imaging radar as a viable sensor for 3D occupancy prediction, with the key practical advantage of all-weather robustness (rain, snow, fog) where LiDAR and cameras degrade significantly. The demonstration that radar-based occupancy can approach 16-beam LiDAR performance and surpass camera methods in most metrics opens a path toward cost-effective, weather-resilient perception stacks for autonomous driving. Key limitations include single-frame processing (no temporal modeling or forecasting), only two semantic classes (foreground/background) due to lack of point-level annotations, evaluation on a single dataset (K-Radar) with one radar sensor, and inference speed (3.3–7.5 fps) still below real-time. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to radar sensors with larger Doppler ranges, how temporal fusion would improve occupancy forecasting, and whether fine-grained semantics can be achieved with radar tensors.
