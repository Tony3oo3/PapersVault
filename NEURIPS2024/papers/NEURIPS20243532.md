## Problem

Classical worst-case optimization theory (L-smoothness, convexity) neither explains the practical success of gradient-based methods in ML nor provides principled step size selection, while Bayesian optimization with gradients—rooted in random function theory—suffers from O(n³d³) complexity that precludes high-dimensional use. The paper asks whether the random function framework can be made computationally viable in high dimensions and whether it can explain common step size heuristics like gradient clipping and learning rate warmup that the convex framework cannot address.

## Input / Output

At each iteration the method consumes the current cost value J(w_n), gradient ∇J(w_n), and a small set of covariance parameters (C(0), C′(0), and a length scale s) estimated non-parametrically from mini-batch losses via weighted least squares regression over varying batch sizes. It outputs the next parameter vector w_{n+1} = w_n − η_n · ∇J(w_n)/||∇J(w_n)||, where the step size η_n is a closed-form function of the gradient-cost quotient Θ = ||∇J(w_n)||/(μ − J(w_n)) and the chosen covariance kernel. No manual learning rate tuning is required; the schedule is fully determined by the estimated covariance.

## Method

The core contribution is a 'stochastic Taylor approximation' defined as the conditional expectation E[J(θ) | J(w), ∇J(w)] under an isotropic Gaussian random function prior, which naturally incorporates a covariance-based trust bound without requiring an explicit L-smoothness constant. Minimizing this approximation (Random Function Descent, RFD) yields gradient descent with a specific step size schedule whose complexity is O(nd) rather than O(n³d³), because the posterior is 'forgetful'—it conditions only on the most recent evaluation and gradient. The step size η*(Θ) exhibits a plateau at large Θ (explaining gradient clipping) and a gradual ramp-up to a constant asymptotic learning rate (explaining warmup), with explicit closed forms for Matérn, squared-exponential, and rational-quadratic covariances. A non-parametric variance estimation procedure regresses squared mini-batch losses on 1/batch-size to recover C(0) and C′(0) without assuming a specific kernel, and an extension (S-RFD) incorporates mini-batch noise into the step size formula.

## Results

On MNIST (batch size 1024), RFD with squared-exponential (SE) and rational-quadratic (RQ) covariance achieves training performance comparable to step-size-tuned Adam and SGD, without requiring any hyperparameter tuning. The RFD learning rates converge to their asymptotic value within one epoch (~30 of 60 steps), while step sizes decrease monotonically. A-RFD (constant asymptotic learning rate, no warmup) performs significantly worse than full RFD, confirming the importance of the gradual step-size schedule. On CIFAR-100, RFD step sizes proved too large, suggesting the method is somewhat risk-affine in harder settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks RFD against step-size-tuned Adam [29] and SGD on MNIST (batch 1024) and Fashion-MNIST (batch 128). On MNIST, RFD (both SE and RQ covariance variants) tracks the tuned Adam and SGD curves closely, with the key advantage that RFD requires no tuning at all—classical step size tuning for Adam/SGD would typically require more than one epoch of samples, whereas RFD's covariance estimation uses a comparable or smaller budget. On Fashion-MNIST, RFD comes very close to tuned performance at the point where early stopping would trigger, though it is slightly too risk-affine (selecting marginally larger step sizes than tuned SGD). The ablation comparing full RFD to A-RFD (constant learning rate) shows that the warmup-like behavior is critical: A-RFD performs significantly worse, validating that the gradual step-size schedule is not merely cosmetic. On CIFAR-100, RFD's step sizes were too large, indicating the method's risk-affinity becomes a liability on harder datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges Bayesian optimization (BO) and classical gradient-based optimization by introducing a 'stochastic Taylor approximation' — the conditional expectation E[J(θ)|J(w),∇J(w)] under isotropic Gaussian random functions — whose minimization recovers gradient descent with an explicit step size schedule. It builds on the canonical GP covariance models (squared exponential, rational quadratic, Matérn) from Rasmussen & Williams [44] and the BO-with-gradients literature [55], but circumvents BO's O(n³d³) complexity by exploiting isotropy to reduce to O(nd). Conceptually, it departs from the classical L-smoothness worst-case framework of Nesterov [38] and connects to the physics community's average-case analysis of random functions (spin glasses) and the ML community's study of optimizers on random quadratic landscapes [58, 43, 40].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a first-principles theoretical derivation of gradient clipping and learning rate warmup from the random function framework, which the convex framework cannot explain since convexity is not satisfied at initialization. Scale invariance (invariance to cost rescaling and input bijections) is a genuine advantage over standard gradient descent. However, the empirical validation is limited to MNIST and Fashion-MNIST, the method is acknowledged to be 'too risk-affine' on CIFAR-100, and the isotropy and Gaussian assumptions are restrictive — simple linear models already violate stationary isotropy. Key open questions include whether non-stationary isotropy (potentially patched by weight/batch normalization) can be formalized, whether momentum can be incorporated, and whether the approach scales to modern large-scale training.
