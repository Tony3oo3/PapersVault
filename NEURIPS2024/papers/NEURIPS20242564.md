## Problem

Existing in-training backdoor defenses rely on detecting and removing or unlearning suspicious samples, which depends on assumptions like latent separability or early learning that can fail under sophisticated attacks. This paper asks whether a defender can suppress an unknown malicious backdoor without any detection step, by leveraging the defender's control over the training process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242564/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a potentially poisoned training dataset and a small reserved clean dataset (10% of training size by default) as inputs. At inference, each RGB image is embedded with a 7×7 patch trigger whose pixel values are set to 2 (outside the natural [0,1] range), making the trigger infeasible for attackers to replicate. The model's raw prediction is then passed through the inverse bijective mapping h⁻¹(y) = (y−1) mod K to recover the true class label.

## Method

PDB injects a proactive defensive backdoor during training by constructing an additional defensive poisoned dataset from the reserved clean samples, where each sample is stamped with the defensive trigger and relabeled via a bijective mapping h(y) = (y+1) mod K. The model is trained on the union of the (potentially malicious) poisoned dataset and the defensive poisoned dataset using a three-term objective: standard cross-entropy on the poisoned data, cross-entropy on the defensive data, and an enhancement loss that adds Gaussian noise to defensive samples to strengthen the defensive backdoor. The defensive trigger's pixel values exceeding [0,1] exploit the 'home field' advantage of the defender, rendering it non-replicable by attackers who are constrained to natural image data. At inference, the defensive trigger is embedded in every input, causing the defensive backdoor to dominate over any malicious backdoor, and the inverse mapping restores the correct label. No detection, relabeling, or assumption about the attack type is required, and the training overhead is minimal (roughly 2× baseline runtime).

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with PreAct-ResNet18 (5% poisoning), PDB achieves an average ASR of 0.71% while maintaining 91.36% clean accuracy, yielding a DER of 96.39 — substantially outperforming all five baselines. On Tiny ImageNet with ViT-B-16 (5% poisoning), PDB reduces ASR to 0.00% across all six attacks while preserving 73.03% accuracy (DER 98.94). Training time for PDB is approximately 2× the no-defense baseline (1853s vs 919s on CIFAR-10), far less than DBD (8351s) or NAB (3679s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PDB dominates all five baselines on ASR across every attack and dataset. On CIFAR-10 (5%), PDB's average ASR of 0.71% beats NAB (11.71%), ABL (42.17%), DBD (59.85%), Spectral (91.63%), and AC (92.45%). On Tiny ImageNet with ViT-B-16, PDB achieves 0.00% ASR while NAB collapses to 30.96% ACC and 99.25% ASR, and ABL/AC/Spectral all remain above 97% ASR. PDB also preserves clean accuracy far better than ABL (64.09%), DBD (76.57%), and NAB (86.61%) on CIFAR-10, yielding the highest DER (96.39 vs. 88.51 for NAB, 62.76 for ABL). In terms of training cost, PDB (1853s) is roughly 4.5× faster than DBD (8351s) and 2× faster than NAB (3679s) on CIFAR-10, while matching or exceeding their defense quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

PDB departs from the dominant detection-and-mitigation paradigm in in-training backdoor defense (ABL, DBD, NAB, AC, Spectral), which all require first identifying poisoned samples before removing, unlearning, or relabeling them. The closest prior work is NAB [26], which also adopts a 'fight fire with fire' strategy by planting non-adversarial triggers, but still depends on a costly detection-and-relabeling pipeline. PDB eliminates both steps entirely, arguing that a well-designed defensive backdoor injected during training is sufficient to suppress an unknown malicious backdoor without any knowledge of the attack's trigger or target. The bijective label mapping and out-of-range pixel trigger design are novel practical mechanisms that operationalize the four stated design principles (reversibility, inaccessibility, minimal impact, resistance).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the detection bottleneck, PDB sidesteps the fragility of assumptions like latent separability or early-learning that underpin most existing defenses, making it robust against adaptive and sophisticated attacks. The practical advantage is a near-standard training cost (only ~2× baseline) versus the 5–10× overhead of DBD or NAB. Key limitations remain: the method requires a small reserved clean dataset (10% of training data), has only been validated on image classification, and its reliance on a defensive trigger at inference time means the deployment pipeline must be modified. Open questions include whether the approach extends to NLP, tabular, or multi-modal tasks, and whether generated data (e.g., from diffusion models) can fully replace real clean samples without degrading defense quality.
