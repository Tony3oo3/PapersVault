## Problem

Data-driven aerodynamic design for cars is hindered by the absence of large-scale, diverse, high-fidelity, and multimodal public datasets. Existing datasets are typically small (hundreds of designs), based on a single car category, exclude critical components like wheels and underbodies, use low-resolution meshes, or lack experimental validation—severely limiting model generalization and practical applicability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240016/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 8,000 car designs in multiple modalities: 3D STL meshes with 350k–750k surface cells, point clouds at 5k–500k nodes, 26 continuous parametric design parameters, ANSA parametric models, and 29-component part annotations. CFD outputs include full 3D velocity and pressure fields, surface pressure and wall-shear stress, streamlines, 2D slices, and aerodynamic coefficients (Cd, Cl, Cl,r, Cl,f, Cm) with mean and standard deviation. All simulations use 24M-cell meshes at 30 m/s freestream (Re ≈ 8.4–10.1 × 10⁶) with the k-ω SST turbulence model, validated against TUM wind tunnel data to within ~5%.

## Method

The dataset is generated by morphing three baseline parametric models (fastback, estateback, notchback) derived from the DrivAer body using 26 geometric parameters controlled via ANSA morphing boxes and direct fitting movements, sampled with Optimal Latin Hypercube sampling and the Enhanced Stochastic Evolutionary Algorithm to ensure diversity. CFD simulations are run in OpenFOAM v11 (simpleFoam, k-ω SST) on SnappyHexMesh-generated 24M-cell meshes with category-specific refinement strategies, rotating wall velocity for wheels, and symmetry boundary conditions. For benchmarking, the paper evaluates geometric deep learning models (PointNet, GCNN, RegDGCNN) trained on 3D meshes/point clouds and AutoML frameworks (AutoGluon, XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forests) trained on tabular parametric data for drag prediction, plus surface pressure field prediction and t-SNE-based design space visualization. A key finding is that R² drops from ~0.82–0.90 on a single car category to ~0.60 on the full diverse dataset, highlighting the generalization challenge posed by shape variation across rear configurations, underbody types, and wheel designs.

## Results

On the diverse DrivAerNet++ test set (1,200 cars across fastback, notchback, and estateback with varied underbody and wheel configurations), RegDGCNN achieves the best MSE (14.2×10⁻⁵) and MAE (9.31×10⁻³), while PointNet attains the highest R² (0.643) with the fastest inference (0.84s). On the single-design DrivAerNet test set (600 fastback cars), RegDGCNN leads with R²=0.901 and MAE=6.91×10⁻³, followed by GCNN (R²=0.874) and PointNet (R²=0.826). For parametric drag prediction, LightGBM performs best on the combined multi-category dataset, while AutoGluon leads on the single fastback category; XGBoost's R² improves from ~0.35 to ~0.55 as training data grows from 640 to 3,200 samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the diverse DrivAerNet++ benchmark, PointNet and RegDGCNN are nearly tied (R² 0.643 vs. 0.641), both substantially outperforming GCNN (R² 0.596), which also suffers from 24× longer training time (49 hrs vs. 2.06 hrs) and 60× slower inference (50.8 s vs. 0.84 s). On the single-design DrivAerNet set, RegDGCNN clearly wins (R² 0.901) over GCNN (0.874) and PointNet (0.826), but all three models degrade by 20–30% in R² when generalizing to the multi-category DrivAerNet++ set, highlighting the difficulty of cross-design generalization. For the parametric (tabular) drag prediction task, AutoGluon achieves the best R² on the single fastback category, while LightGBM excels on the combined multi-category dataset; XGBoost and Random Forests trail both. Gradient Boosting and Random Forests show the weakest performance among the AutoML baselines in Figure 5. Overall, no single model dominates across all settings, and the paper demonstrates that dataset diversity and size are critical factors: increasing training data from 640 to 3,200 samples consistently improves R² for all tabular models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DrivAerNet dataset [24] by doubling its size from 4,000 to 8,000 cars and, critically, expanding from a single car category (fastback with detailed underbody) to three categories (fastback, notchback, estateback) with multiple underbody and wheel configurations. The geometric deep learning models benchmarked (PointNet, GCNN, RegDGCNN) follow the established lineage of 3D shape-to-performance prediction, while the AutoML baselines (XGBoost, LightGBM, AutoGluon) represent the tabular surrogate modeling tradition. The paper explicitly positions itself against a landscape of small, low-fidelity, or single-design datasets, arguing that no prior resource combines high-fidelity CFD (24M cells, k-ω SST), experimental validation, multimodal data, and multi-category diversity at this scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DrivAerNet++ fills a gap analogous to ImageNet's role in vision: it provides the first large-scale, publicly available, experimentally validated, multimodal car aerodynamics dataset, enabling fair cross-model comparisons that were previously impossible. The benchmarking reveals a significant generalization gap (R² dropping from ~0.9 to ~0.6 when moving from single-category to multi-category prediction), highlighting that current geometric deep learning models struggle with the combinatorial diversity of real car designs. Open questions include whether more expressive architectures (Geometry-Informed Neural Operators, Convolutional Occupancy Networks) or multi-fidelity transfer learning can close this gap, and whether transient CFD and additional modalities (2D renderings) will further improve predictive fidelity.
