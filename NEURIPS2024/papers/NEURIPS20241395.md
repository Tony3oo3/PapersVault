## Problem

Conventional generalization (small test error in the learned vector field) does not guarantee that a neural network trained on time series data reproduces the correct long-term statistical behavior of a chaotic dynamical system, including the physical measure, Lyapunov exponents, and ergodic averages. A model with <5% relative error in the vector field can still produce atypical orbits that fail to capture the attractor's invariant distribution. The paper asks: under what conditions does regression-based generalization imply statistical accuracy, and what inductive biases are needed?

## Input / Output

The method takes m state pairs {(x_t, x_{t+1})} sampled from a single orbit of a chaotic system at a small time step δt (e.g., 0.01 for Lorenz '63), treating them as approximately iid due to exponential decay of correlations. The output is a Neural ODE vector field v_θ parameterized by an MLP, ResNet, or Fourier Neural Operator, which is time-integrated (via RK4) to produce orbits. Evaluation goes beyond pointwise vector field error to include Wasserstein-1 distance between empirical and physical measures, Lyapunov exponents computed via QR iteration over 30,000 steps from 10,000 random initial states, and time-averaged state statistics over 50,000-step orbits.

## Method

The core contribution is a Jacobian-matching loss ℓ_λ(x, F_nn) = ||F_nn(x) − F(x)||² + λ||dF_nn(x) − dF(x)||² that regularizes the standard MSE regression with the error in the Jacobian matrix, trained via standard ERM without unrolling temporal dynamics. Theoretically, the paper introduces C¹ generalization (small errors in both the map and its derivative) and leverages shadowing theory for uniformly hyperbolic systems to prove that C¹ strong generalization plus typicality of shadowing orbits implies convergence of the learned empirical measure to the physical measure in Wasserstein-1 distance (Theorem 1). This explains why C⁰ generalization (MSE alone) is insufficient: without Jacobian accuracy, the learned map need not be shadowed by true orbits, so ergodic averages can be wrong even with vanishing pointwise error. Empirically, across MLPs, ResNets, FNOs, and latent SDEs on six chaotic systems (1D tent maps through 127D Kuramoto-Sivashinsky), Jacobian-matching models reproduce Lyapunov exponents and invariant statistics with orders-of-magnitude smaller error than MSE models at comparable vector field accuracy, while requiring fewer samples than generative approaches.

## Results

Across all architectures (MLP, ResNet, FNO) on the Lorenz '63 system, Jacobian-matching (JAC) loss reduces the Wasserstein-1 distance between learned and true empirical distributions by 1–2 orders of magnitude compared to MSE loss (e.g., MLP: 0.68 vs. 18.97; ResNet: 0.14 vs. 1.36; FNO: 1.31 vs. 10.54). The Lyapunov exponent error drops from 9.7–22.2 (MSE) to 0.01–0.05 (JAC), and the state-mean error drops from 0.78–15.2 to 0.06–0.97. On the 127-dimensional Kuramoto-Sivashinsky system, the best JAC model recovers more than half of the first 64 Lyapunov exponents with <10% relative error, while the best MSE model captures only 2 out of 64.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four training strategies: (1) standard MSE regression on the one-step map, (2) Jacobian-matching loss (MSE + λ‖dF_nn − dF‖²), (3) unrolled multi-step loss, and (4) a latent SDE generative model. JAC loss dominates MSE loss on every statistical metric across all six chaotic systems tested (Lorenz '63, Rössler, tent maps, Baker's map, 4D hyperchaos, 127D KS), even though both achieve comparable one-step vector-field relative errors (<5%). The unrolled loss (k=50) improves over MSE (W¹=0.51 for ResNet) but still falls short of JAC (W¹=0.14) and plateaus in Lyapunov exponent accuracy (negative LE ≈ −9.8 vs. true −14.5). The latent SDE generative model reproduces the multimodal distribution reasonably well but requires ~60× more samples (614,400 vs. 10,000) and still yields an incorrect stable Lyapunov exponent (−11.8 vs. −14.5), while JAC achieves smaller Wasserstein distances with only 10⁴ samples. A notable failure mode is the plucked tent map at s=0.8, where even JAC loss produces an incorrect LE (0.50 vs. true 0.32), consistent with the paper's theoretical prediction that atypical shadowing orbits break statistical accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes shadowing theory from ergodic dynamical systems (Katok & Hasselblatt, Pilyugin) as a lens for ML generalization, a connection not previously drawn in the neural ODE or physics-informed ML literature. It departs from the dominant training paradigms for chaotic systems (RNNs with unrolling, operator learning with Sobolev norms, stabilized recurrent architectures) by showing that a plain ERM regression with a simple Jacobian-matching term suffices for statistical accuracy, provided C¹ generalization holds. The Jacobian regularization itself is not new (Finlay et al. 2020, FJNO20), but the paper provides the first ergodic-theoretic justification for *why* it works and *when* it fails (atypical shadowing), framing it as an inductive bias toward physical measures rather than merely a training-stability trick.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that standard generalization (small pointwise MSE) is fundamentally insufficient for chaotic systems, providing a rigorous shadowing-based criterion (Theorem 1) under which regression yields statistically accurate models. This reframes the evaluation of learned dynamical surrogates: practitioners must check Lyapunov exponents and invariant measures, not just test-set error. Key limitations include the uniform hyperbolicity assumption (most real systems are only partially or singularly hyperbolic), the unresolved question of atypical shadowing (where even C¹ generalization fails, as shown with plucked tent maps), and the computational cost of Jacobian estimation in high-dimensional settings. Extending the framework to non-uniformly hyperbolic systems and to operator learning with Sobolev norms are natural next steps.
