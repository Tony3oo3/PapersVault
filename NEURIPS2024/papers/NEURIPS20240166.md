## Problem

The brain's extensive recurrent connectivity requires error propagation through both space and time, a problem notoriously difficult in vanilla RNNs due to vanishing/exploding gradients. While feedback connections are known to modulate forward activity in biological circuits, the interaction between feedback-driven activity changes and local, synaptic plasticity-based learning remains largely uncharacterized. This paper investigates how feedback control of ongoing recurrent dynamics can guide credit assignment, enabling both rapid within-trial movement correction and efficient trial-by-trial adaptation.

## Input / Output

The network receives a 3-dimensional input vector comprising a 2D target position (uniformly sampled from U(-6,6)) and a binary go-cue signal (Heaviside step active during a fixed 0.2 s instructed delay), plus a 2D positional error feedback signal projected back through learned feedback weights. The output is a 2D velocity trajectory produced by a linear readout of 400 ReLU hidden units, integrated over ~1.25 s (125 timesteps at 10 ms) to yield a sigmoidal reaching profile. During perturbation, the target is rotated by a fixed angle (30°–60°) around the centre, creating a mismatch between the learned input-output mapping and the new target.

## Method

The paper uses a two-stage training strategy: first, a single-layer RNN with feedback control is pre-trained via BPTT on a synthetic centre-out reaching task with random velocity perturbations, and second, it is fine-tuned under persistent visuomotor rotation using the temporally and spatially local RFLO learning rule (batch size 1, online updates). The key mechanistic insight is that during perturbation, the feedback signal injected into the recurrent dynamics approximates the true first-order gradient w.r.t. activations, 'decouples' the network from its past recurrent state (reducing Jacobian norm), and implicitly encodes second-order (Newton-method) gradient information. This decoupling makes the eligibility-trace approximation in the local learning rule more accurate, while the second-order component increases the efficiency of online weight updates, as quantified by a global-to-local weight-update ratio. The authors validate these claims by measuring gradient alignment (local and global), Jacobian sensitivity, and learning efficiency across perturbation magnitudes, comparing RFLO with and without feedback control against online BPTT baselines.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that feedback control in pre-trained RNNs approximates the optimal first-order gradient w.r.t. network activations during task perturbation, with the online feedback signal showing high cosine similarity to the true global trial gradient (Figure 2c). With feedback control during learning (RFLO+c), the local learning rule achieves significantly better global gradient alignment than without (RFLO), and the Jacobian norm of current activity w.r.t. past activity drops substantially, indicating 'decoupling' from recurrent history (Figure 4a). The efficiency ratio of global to local weight updates is significantly higher with feedback control at all tested perturbation magnitudes (30°–60°), and the feedback signal also aligns with the second-order Newton gradient during peaks of movement error (Figure 5b).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is online BPTT (Real-Time Recurrent Learning), used both with and without feedback control during learning. On minor perturbations (30°), RFLO+c achieves final performance comparable to BPTT+c, demonstrating that the local learning rule with feedback control can match full-gradient methods. However, with larger perturbations (50°–60°), RFLO+c shows an increasing performance gap relative to BPTT+c, indicating that the local approximation becomes less sufficient as the perturbation magnitude grows. Critically, feedback control consistently improves both RFLO and BPTT: RFLO+c outperforms RFLO, and BPTT+c outperforms BPTT, at all perturbation degrees. The efficiency analysis (Figure 5a) shows that the benefit of feedback control on weight-update efficiency is specific to the local learning rule (RFLO) and is not observed when using the more accurate BPTT gradients (Figure 10b), suggesting that feedback control compensates for the noise inherent in pseudo-gradient-based online learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides mechanistic analysis of the modeling framework introduced by Feulner et al. (2022), which integrated feedback control into recurrent network dynamics for motor learning, and employs the RFLO local learning rule from Murray (2019) as the plasticity mechanism. It departs from prior work on feedback in recurrent networks (Meulemans et al., 2022b; Denève et al., 2017) by showing that the benefits of feedback-driven activity modulation extend beyond equilibrium systems to out-of-equilibrium, non-stationary dynamics. The key conceptual departure is connecting the feedback control signal to both first-order gradient approximation and implicit second-order (Newton's method) gradient information, bridging the gap between biological feedback control and optimization theory in a way that prior target-propagation and equilibrium-based analyses did not address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a mechanistic explanation for how biological circuits might solve the credit assignment problem in recurrent networks without explicit backpropagation, with the finding that a simple linear feedback signal implicitly encodes second-order curvature information being particularly relevant for understanding efficient biological learning. However, the scope is limited to a single-layer RNN on a center-out reaching task with linear feedback, and the authors explicitly note that the biological implementation of the feedback signal (likely cerebellar) and the generality to deeper architectures, non-linear feedback, and more complex tasks remain open. The connection to trust-region and Newton-type optimization methods suggests a broader principle—activity-space inference as implicit second-order optimization—that could inform future work on biologically plausible learning in deeper or spiking networks.
