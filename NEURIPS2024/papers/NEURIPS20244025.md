## Problem

Higher-order GNNs aligned with the k-WL hierarchy offer strong theoretical expressivity guarantees but consistently underperform on real-world tasks, while empirically strong graph transformers rely on positional/structural encodings that obscure their expressivity within the k-WL framework. No prior work had reported empirical results for a 3-WL-equivalent graph transformer on established graph learning benchmarks, leaving a gap between principled expressivity and practical predictive power.

## Input / Output

The ET consumes a graph's node features, edge features, and adjacency structure, tokenizing them into a 3D tensor X ∈ R^{n×n×d} where each entry X_ij encodes the node pair (i,j) via a neural network applied to the concatenation of edge features E_ij, node features F_i and F_j. Outputs are produced at multiple granularities: graph-level predictions via pooling over node-pair representations, node-level predictions via diagonal or asymmetric readout, and edge-level predictions by restricting to actual edges. The O(n³) runtime and memory complexity constrains the model to mid-sized graphs (up to ~700 nodes with GPU optimizations).

## Method

The paper adapts the Edge Transformer architecture—originally designed for systematic generalization—to graph learning and proves it has exactly 3-WL expressive power without any positional or structural encodings. The key mechanism is triangular attention: for each node pair (i,j), the model aggregates over all intermediate nodes l using attention scores computed between the tuple representations (i,l) and (l,j), with value fusion via element-wise multiplication of projected features, which precisely simulates the 2-FWL multiset aggregation over color pairs. The tokenization maps each node pair to a d-dimensional vector by concatenating edge features and both nodes' features through a neural network, and the model stacks L layers of layer-normalized triangular attention followed by feed-forward networks. The paper further leverages the Cai-Fürer-Immerman connection between k-FWL and first-order logic to show that the ET's 3-WL expressivity corresponds to evaluating sentences in C_{3,t}, providing a formal justification for its ability to iteratively compose relational concepts (e.g., deriving GREATGRANDMOTHER from MOTHER within two layers).

## Results

On molecular regression, the ET achieves 0.062±0.004 MAE on ZINC (12K) and 0.099±0.001 on ALCHEMY (12K), outperforming all theoretically-aligned baselines including PPGN++ (0.071, 0.109) and CIN (0.079). On the CLRS neural algorithmic reasoning benchmark, the ET achieves 80.13% average micro F1 across all 30 algorithms, surpassing the Relational Transformer (66.18%) and Triplet-GMPNN (75.98%). On the BREC expressivity benchmark, the ET distinguishes 258.1±1.9 pairs of non-isomorphic graphs, exceeding PPGN (233) and approaching the 3-WL upper bound (270). On PCQM4MV2, the ET achieves 0.0840 validation MAE with only 16.8M parameters, outperforming Graphormer (0.0864, 48.3M params) and GRIT (0.0859, 16.6M params).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The ET outperforms all theoretically-aligned baselines on ZINC (12K) and ALCHEMY: it beats PPGN++ (0.071→0.062 MAE), SPE (0.069→0.062), CIN (0.079→0.062), and Graphormer-GD (0.081→0.062) on ZINC, and PPGN++ (0.109→0.099), SPE (0.108→0.099), and SignNet (0.113→0.099) on ALCHEMY. On ZINC-FULL, PPGN++ (0.020) and CIN (0.022) slightly outperform the ET (0.026), though ET+RRWP narrows the gap to 0.024. On CLRS, the ET achieves 80.13% average F1, beating the Relational Transformer (66.18%) by 14 points and Triplet-GMPNN (75.98%) by 4 points, and is best on 4 of 8 algorithm classes (Sorting, DP, Graphs, Geometry). On BREC, the ET distinguishes 258.1 pairs vs. PPGN (233), δ-2-LGNN (216), and Graphormer (79), approaching the 3-WL ceiling of 270. On PCQM4MV2, the ET (0.0840, 16.8M params) outperforms Graphormer (0.0864, 48.3M), GRIT (0.0859, 16.6M), and GraphGPS Medium (0.0858, 19.4M) while using fewer parameters than most competitors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Edge Transformer (Bergen et al., 2021), originally designed for systematic generalization in compositional reasoning, and applies it to graph learning. It departs from two parallel lines of work: higher-order GNNs (δ-k-GNNs, SpeqNets, PPGN, PPGN++) that achieve k-WL expressivity but underperform empirically, and graph transformers (Graphormer, GraphGPS) that perform well but rely on positional/structural encodings, obscuring their theoretical expressivity. The key positioning is that the ET achieves exactly 3-WL expressivity with O(n³) complexity, compared to O(n⁶) for the equivalent higher-order transformers of Kim et al. (2022), while requiring no positional/structural encodings for its theoretical guarantees. The paper further connects to Cai et al. (1992) to ground systematic generalization in first-order logic, providing a theoretical justification that prior work on the ET lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work closes a notable gap between the k-WL expressivity hierarchy and practical graph transformer performance, demonstrating that a theoretically principled architecture can be competitive with state-of-the-art models on molecular regression and algorithmic reasoning without relying on positional encodings. The first-order logic interpretation of systematic generalization (e.g., GREATGRANDMOTHER ∈ C_{3,2} via re-quantification) offers a principled framework for understanding what compositional generalization a model can achieve given its expressivity level. The primary open question is scalability: the O(n³) runtime and memory limit the ET to mid-sized graphs, and while torch.compile and FlashAttention-style optimizations help, a fundamental efficiency-expressivity trade-off likely persists. Multi-task learning on CLRS and extension to larger molecular datasets remain natural next steps.
