## Problem

Standard LLM benchmarks rely on a single or small set of prompt templates, making leaderboard rankings unreliable and sensitive to arbitrary prompt choices. While prior work has documented prompt sensitivity and advocated for multi-prompt evaluation, computing the full performance distribution across hundreds of templates requires I×J evaluations, which is prohibitively expensive. No general method existed to accurately estimate this distribution from a small fraction of evaluations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240707/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small subset of binary correctness scores (typically 200–1600 prompt-example pairs, less than 5% of the full I×J grid) selected via two-way balanced stratified sampling, along with prompt template covariates (sentence-transformer embeddings reduced to 25 dimensions via PCA, fine-tuned BERT embeddings, or discrete formatting features) and example covariates (one-hot encodings). It outputs an estimated performance distribution F̂ and its quantile function Q̂ over all I prompt templates, enabling computation of any quantile (e.g., 5th, 50th, 95th) without additional evaluations.

## Method

PromptEval models each correctness score as Bernoulli with mean σ(f_ψ(x_i) − g_γ(z_j)), a generalization of the Rasch model from Item Response Theory where prompt and example parameters are functions of their respective covariates. The model is fit by maximum likelihood (equivalent to logistic regression) on the observed subset, then a plug-in conditional expectation (X-pIRT) estimates each prompt's performance by averaging observed scores for seen examples and model-predicted probabilities for unseen ones. The empirical distribution is constructed from these I estimated performances, and two-way balanced sampling (Algorithm 2) ensures each prompt and example is evaluated roughly equally to avoid unstable coverage. The key insight is that the shared logistic model borrows strength across prompts and examples, allowing accurate distribution estimation with a budget comparable to evaluating just one or two prompts, with consistency guarantees proven as I, J → ∞.

## Results

PromptEval estimates performance quantiles across 100 prompt templates on MMLU with a budget equivalent to just two single-prompt evaluations (~0.81% of total evaluations). Central quantiles (e.g., median) are accurately estimated with only 200 evaluations, yielding over 100× compute savings compared to full evaluation. All PromptEval variants (PE-Rasch, PE-discrete, PE-EmbPT, PE-EmbFT) substantially outperform the simple averaging baseline in both Wasserstein-1 distribution estimation error and quantile estimation error across MMLU, BBH, and LMentry. In the LLM-as-a-judge setting (AlpacaEval 2.0), PromptEval achieves much lower Wasserstein loss using only ~2% of total evaluations (1.6k out of 80.5k).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the simple averaging baseline ("avg"), all four PromptEval variants achieve substantially lower Wasserstein-1 distance and quantile estimation error across all three benchmarks (MMLU, BBH, LMentry), with the gap widening as the number of prompt templates increases. PE-EmbPT (pre-trained sentence-transformer embeddings) is the most consistently strong variant across benchmarks, while PE-discrete (heuristic text features) underperforms on LMentry. PE-EmbFT (fine-tuned BERT) provides additional gains for extreme quantiles and very small evaluation budgets on MMLU, but requires expensive fine-tuning data. In the best-prompt identification task (Figure 5), PromptEval with sequential elimination achieves lower regret than TRIPLE-GSE [Shi et al., 2024] across all three covariate types (one-hot, discrete, embeddings) on MMLU, BBH, and LMentry, confirming that the IRT-based performance model provides a better predictor for prompt selection than the logistic-regression or MLP predictors used in the bandit baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

PromptEval extends the Performance-IRT (pIRT) estimator from Maia Polo et al. (2024, TinyBenchmarks) from single-prompt evaluation to the multi-prompt setting, adding prompt-template covariates (embeddings, discrete features) to the IRT model to borrow strength across templates. It addresses the prompt-sensitivity problem documented by Sclar et al. (2023) and Mizrahi et al. (2023), who showed that single-prompt evaluation yields unreliable rankings and proposed summary statistics across many templates but noted the prohibitive computational cost. The method also connects to best-arm identification (Shi et al., 2024) and LLM-as-a-judge (Li et al., 2023) as downstream applications, and its stratified sampling design echoes Perlitz et al. (2023) but applied jointly over prompts and examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Prompt sensitivity is a well-known but largely unaddressed source of unreliability in LLM leaderboards; PromptEval provides the first general, theoretically-grounded method to estimate the full performance distribution across many prompts at a cost of only 2–4 single-prompt evaluations, making robust multi-prompt comparison practically feasible. Key open questions remain: how to select the pool of prompt templates (the paper relies on heuristics from prior work), how to best compare distributions across models in risk-sensitive settings, and whether the method extends to dynamically generated prompt candidates. A limitation is that the approach assumes a fixed prompt pool and does not address prompt engineering itself.
