## Problem

State-of-the-art neural network architectures for surface processing predominantly use extrinsic vertex coordinates as input, leaving the network to learn surface structure from scratch. While more expressive representations like Heat Kernel Signatures or SHOT descriptors have been proposed, they are computationally expensive and their ability to capture essential geometric features is opaque. The paper asks whether a simple, theoretically grounded local invariant—principal curvature—can serve as a more effective and lightweight input representation across diverse architectures and tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243888/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes surface meshes or point clouds and computes per-vertex principal curvatures (κ₁, κ₂) via a multi-scale osculating jet fit with a fixed neighbourhood radius of 5, yielding a 2-dimensional feature vector per point (or 1-dimensional Gaussian curvature κ = κ₁·κ₂). This replaces the standard 3D xyz coordinates or the 352-dimensional SHOT descriptor as the per-point input to the network. Outputs are per-vertex semantic part labels for segmentation tasks (human anatomy, RNA molecules) or a single shape class label for the SHREC'11 classification task.

## Method

The core contribution is an empirical study showing that the eigenvalues of the discrete shape operator—computed via multi-scale osculating jets (Panozzo et al.)—serve as a superior input representation for three existing surface-processing architectures: PointNet++, DeltaNet, and DiffusionNet. The shape operator is discretized by locally fitting a quadratic surface to neighbourhoods of varying size around each vertex, then extracting its eigenvalues as principal curvatures; Gaussian curvature is obtained as their product. The key insight is that principal curvatures fully characterize a surface up to rigid transformation (congruence), are purely local so the network can still learn global structure, and incur minimal computational overhead (2D per point vs. 3D coordinates or 352D SHOT). Across human segmentation, molecular segmentation, and shape classification, curvature inputs consistently outperform coordinates, HKS, and SHOT, with the largest gains observed in architectures with the least built-in geometric prior (PointNet++) and in intrinsic-operation architectures (DeltaNet).

## Results

On human part segmentation (SHREC07), principal curvatures (κ₁,κ₂) achieve 80.6% accuracy with PointNet++, 86.8% with DeltaNet, and 97.4% with DiffusionNet, consistently outperforming xyz coordinates (69.6%, 72.4%, 94.7%) and HKS (78.1%, 68.9%, 95.1%). On molecular segmentation (PDB), κ₁,κ₂ reaches 72.4% (PointNet++) and 89.4% (DiffusionNet) versus 35.4% and 82.6% for xyz. On SHREC'11 classification, Gaussian curvature κ achieves 96.2% (PointNet++), 98.8% (DeltaNet), and 99.1% (DiffusionNet), surpassing all other representations including HKS (60.8%, 98.6%, 98.9%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against HKS [42], principal curvatures win decisively on both segmentation tasks: on human segmentation, κ₁,κ₂ beats HKS by 2.5 points (PointNet++), 17.9 points (DeltaNet), and 2.3 points (DiffusionNet); on molecular segmentation, the gap is 2.2 points (PointNet++) and 3.8 points (DiffusionNet). On SHREC'11 classification, however, HKS is competitive with or slightly ahead of κ₁,κ₂ for DeltaNet (98.6% vs 90.1%) and DiffusionNet (98.9% vs 94.2%), though Gaussian curvature κ matches or exceeds HKS in all three architectures. Against SHOT [37], curvature representations dominate on PointNet++ and DeltaNet across all tasks (e.g., 80.6% vs 72.4% for SHOT₆₄ on human segmentation with PointNet++; 86.8% vs 66.2% with DeltaNet), while SHOT is roughly on par with curvature for DiffusionNet on human segmentation (95.0% vs 97.4%). Overall, the 2-dimensional principal curvature input outperforms the 352-dimensional SHOT descriptor and the multi-scale HKS signature in nearly all settings, at a fraction of the computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the prevailing consensus in geometric deep learning that extrinsic vertex coordinates suffice as input, letting the network learn surface structure implicitly (as argued by Mitchel et al. [25] and demonstrated by DiffusionNet [38]). It repurposes the classical shape operator from differential geometry—previously used in surface processing only for auxiliary tasks like tangent frame construction and crease detection [31]—as the primary input representation, leveraging multi-scale osculating-jet estimation [9, 31] for discrete computation. The paper positions curvature as a middle ground between the minimalism of raw coordinates and the complexity of signature-based descriptors (HKS [42], SHOT [37]), arguing that principal curvatures are the simplest differentiable invariant that fully characterizes surface shape up to isometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a 2-dimensional (or even 1-dimensional) curvature input can replace 352-dimensional SHOT descriptors or multi-scale HKS signatures with less computational overhead and equal or better performance, which is actionable for anyone building surface-processing pipelines. The finding that Gaussian curvature outperforms principal curvatures specifically in classification tasks—likely due to its interaction with pooling operations—opens a question about which curvature variant suits which architectural component. Limitations include testing only three architectures and three datasets, and the DeltaNet results are inconsistent (coordinates sometimes outperform curvature), suggesting the benefit may depend on how the architecture handles local vs. global information. Future work could explore higher-order differential invariants generated by Gauss and mean curvature, and broader architectural coverage.
