## Problem

It remains unclear whether neural networks learn to implement structured algorithms such as look-ahead or search internally, or whether they rely purely on collections of simple heuristics and memorized patterns. Prior evidence of learned algorithms (e.g., in-context optimization, path-finding) comes from small models trained on synthetic tasks, leaving open whether such mechanisms emerge in strong models operating in complex, realistic domains. This paper investigates the question in chess, where the expected algorithmic behavior (look-ahead along a line of play) is well-defined and the model (Leela Chess Zero) is a state-of-the-art engine.

## Input / Output

Leela's policy network takes a single chess board state encoded as 12 piece-type bitmaps plus castling channels, with each of the 64 squares serving as one token position in a 15-layer bidirectional transformer (768-dim residual stream, 24 heads/layer). The policy head computes a logit for every legal move by matrix-multiplying source and target square embeddings, yielding a distribution over moves; the value head outputs win/draw/loss probabilities. For interpretability experiments, a second 'corrupted' board state (differing by one piece addition, removal, or relocation) is fed through the same network to generate patching sources. The bilinear probe reads residual-stream activations at specific squares and outputs a predicted 3rd-move target and source square.

## Method

The paper presents three converging lines of evidence for learned look-ahead in Leela's policy network. First, activation patching—where activations from a corrupted forward pass are spliced into a clean pass one square-and-layer at a time—reveals that the target square of the 3rd move in the principal variation has a disproportionately large causal effect on the correct-move logit (mean reduction of 1.88 log-odds at layer 10, vs. 0.55 for the best of all other 61 squares). Corruptions are automatically selected using a weaker model: only those that drastically change Leela's preferred move while barely affecting the weaker model are retained, isolating mechanisms responsible for Leela's superior performance. Second, attention-head analysis identifies L12H12, which frequently routes information from the 3rd-move target square 'backward in time' to the 1st-move target square (zeroing this single attention entry reduces log-odds by >1.5 in >10% of puzzles), as well as 'piece movement heads' whose attention patterns mirror legal piece moves and which, when ablated at the 3rd-move target, significantly degrade performance—consistent with analyzing the consequences of future moves. Third, a bilinear probe trained on residual-stream activations (motivated by L12H12's query-key structure) predicts the 3rd-move target square with 92% accuracy after layer 12, far exceeding a 15% baseline from a randomly initialized network.

## Results

Three lines of evidence support learned look-ahead in Leela. (1) Activation patching on the 3rd-move target square reduces log odds of the correct move by 1.88 ± 0.04 in layer 10, versus only 0.55 ± 0.01 for the maximum effect over all 61 other squares. (2) Zeroing a single attention entry in head L12H12 (from 3rd-move target to 1st-move target) reduces log odds by >1.5 in over 10% of puzzles, far exceeding the effect of ablating all 4,095 other entries simultaneously. (3) A bilinear probe on layer-12 residual stream activations predicts the 3rd-move target square with 92 ± 1% accuracy, compared to 15 ± 2% for a randomly initialized model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baseline methods to benchmark against. The paper's internal comparisons are nonetheless informative: the 3rd-move target square's causal importance (1.88 log-odds reduction) is roughly 3.4× larger than the maximum effect of any other square, ruling out a uniform-difficulty explanation. The single-entry L12H12 ablation outperforms ablating all 4,095 other attention entries in that head, indicating a highly specific information pathway. The bilinear probe's 92% accuracy dwarfs the 15% achieved by a randomly initialized copy of Leela, confirming that the signal is learned rather than an artifact of the probe architecture. On the subsplit where 1st and 2nd move targets differ (17% of puzzles), effects are weaker but qualitatively similar, suggesting the findings are not solely driven by the 83% of puzzles where those targets coincide.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability toolkit—particularly activation patching (Vig et al., 2020) and bilinear probes (Hewitt and Liang, 2019)—to a complex, 'in the wild' model (Leela Chess Zero) rather than the small, purpose-built networks studied in prior work on learned algorithms (von Oswald et al., 2023; Brinkmann et al., 2024; Akyurek et al., 2023). It also builds on concurrent findings of look-ahead in Sokoban-playing RNNs (Taufeque et al., 2024; Bush et al., 2024) and future-token decoding in language models (Pal et al., 2023), but distinguishes itself by studying causal influence of future-move representations on present decisions in a strong, non-toy domain. A key methodological departure is the use of a weaker model to automatically generate targeted corruptions for activation patching, isolating mechanisms that explain the gap between strong and weak model performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first evidence that a strong, 'in the wild' neural network (not trained for interpretability) internally implements a recognizable algorithmic procedure—look-ahead—using identifiable, interpretable components (a specific attention head, piece-movement heads, and a linearly-readable representation of future moves). The 92% probe accuracy for predicting two moves ahead and the outsized causal effect of ablating a single attention entry are striking, but the findings are scoped to tactically complex puzzle states and a single line of play; whether Leela performs full search (comparing multiple lines) or combines look-ahead with heuristics in general play remains open. Broader implications include whether language models implement analogous principled algorithms when appropriate, and whether such learned optimization poses novel safety considerations.
