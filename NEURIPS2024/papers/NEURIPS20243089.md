## Problem

Posterior sampling in Bayesian inverse problems with denoising diffusion model (DDM) priors is challenging because the posterior density and its score are intractable, invalidating conventional MCMC and variational inference. Existing diffusion-based samplers (e.g., DPS, ΠGDM) rely on crude point-mass or Gaussian approximations of the conditional p_{0|k}(·|x_k), introducing uncontrolled bias that degrades sample quality, while SMC-based alternatives suffer from particle collapse in high dimensions. The paper addresses this gap by asking whether the approximation error can be systematically reduced without retraining any model components.

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen pre-trained DDM (denoiser network), a degraded observation y, and a forward model (linear operator A for Gaussian/Poisson noise, or a differentiable JPEG encode-decode pair for dequantization) as input, and produces multiple posterior samples from π₀(x₀) ∝ g₀(x₀)p₀(x₀) that capture full posterior uncertainty. Unlike DPS which requires 1000 diffusion steps, DCPS operates with 300 steps and L=3 blocks of moderate length, matching the runtime and memory footprint of DPS on a single 48GB GPU. The user must specify problem-appropriate intermediate potentials g_{k_ℓ} (closed-form for linear Gaussian and Poisson models; a differentiable JPEG surrogate for dequantization), and the method outputs 4 parallel samples per run for visual comparison.

## Method

DCPS expresses the posterior as the time-zero marginal of a time-reversed Feynman-Kac path measure, then divides the full backward diffusion into L blocks of moderate length k_{ℓ+1}−k_ℓ. The key theoretical insight (Proposition 3.1) is a bridge-kernel smoothing result: approximating the shorter conditional p_{ℓ|k}(·|x_k) instead of p_{0|k}(·|x_k) reduces the W₂ approximation error by a factor √α_ℓ(1−α_k/α_ℓ)/(1−α_k) < 1, which shrinks as ℓ increases. Within each block, the algorithm first runs M steps of tamed unadjusted Langevin (TULA) to sample the block's initial distribution π_{k_{ℓ+1}}^ℓ, then simulates the intractable backward Markov transitions via non-amortized Gaussian variational inference: for each step j, a mean-field Gaussian λ_{j|j+1}^φ is fit by minimizing a KL objective (Eq. 3.8) using the reparameterization trick and a few SGD steps, with the potential g_j^{ℓ,★} computed in closed form for linear and Poisson models. This divide-and-conquer strategy reduces local approximation errors without requiring particle filters or any retraining of the denoiser.

## Results

On the main imaging benchmark (Table 2), DCPS achieves the best average LPIPS of 0.28 across 16 tasks (inpainting, outpainting, SR 4×, SR 16× on FFHQ and ImageNet at two noise levels), outperforming DDRM (0.35), DPS (0.32), IIGDM (0.57), REDDIFF (0.60), and MCGDIFF (0.67). On the Gaussian mixture posterior sampling problem (Table 1), DCPS with 500 Langevin steps achieves a sliced Wasserstein distance of 2.19 (d_x=10) and 3.29 (d_x=100), closely matching MCGDIFF (2.28 and 2.83) while avoiding particle filter collapse. For JPEG dequantization at QF=2, DCPS achieves LPIPS 0.20 on FFHQ versus 0.37 (IIGDM) and 0.32 (REDDIFF), and for Poisson denoising achieves 0.07 on FFHQ versus 0.12 (DPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCPS outperforms all six baselines on 13 of 16 imaging tasks and achieves the best average LPIPS (0.28 vs. 0.32 for DPS, 0.35 for DDRM, 0.57 for IIGDM, 0.60 for REDDIFF, and 0.67 for MCGDIFF). The closest competitor is DPS, which DCPS beats by a margin of 0.04 in average LPIPS while using the same runtime and memory footprint (300 DDPM steps for DCPS vs. 1000 for DPS). IIGDM and REDDIFF degrade sharply at higher noise (σ_y=0.3), with LPIPS values increasing by 2–4×, whereas DCPS remains stable. On the Gaussian mixture experiment, DCPS_500 nearly matches MCGDIFF (SW 2.19 vs. 2.28 at d_x=10) without the particle filter collapse that limits MCGDIFF in high dimensions. For JPEG dequantization, DCPS achieves LPIPS 0.20 (FFHQ, QF=2) versus 0.37 for IIGDM and 0.32 for REDDIFF, and for Poisson denoising it achieves 0.07 versus 0.12 for DPS. On the trajectory completion task, DCPS achieves the lowest median ℓ₂ distance (1.31) compared to DPS (1.34), IIGDM (1.36), DDRM (1.48), and REDDIFF (1.67) at σ_y=0.005.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DDPM generative framework (Ho et al., 2020) and the Feynman-Kac representation of the posterior (Del Moral, 2004), which prior works like DPS (Chung et al., 2023), PiGDM (Song et al., 2023), and MCGDIFF (Cardoso et al., 2024) all exploit. It departs from DPS by showing its point-mass approximation of p_{0|k} is biased and proposing a bridge-kernel smoothing that provably reduces the Wasserstein error (Proposition 3.1). Unlike SMC-based approaches (MCGDIFF, TDS, FPS) that suffer particle collapse in high dimensions, DCPS avoids particle filters entirely by combining per-block Gaussian variational inference with tamed Langevin steps, while differing from REDDIFF in that the VI is applied per backward transition rather than amortized over the full posterior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DCPS provides a principled, retraining-free way to reduce posterior sampling error for DDM priors by exploiting the FK path structure, and its divide-and-conquer blocking gives a clear knob (number of blocks L) to trade off approximation quality against computational cost. A key limitation is that the intermediate potentials must be hand-designed per problem type (Gaussian linear, Poisson, JPEG), and the authors identify automatic potential design as an important open direction. The method also remains slower than REDDIFF and DDRM due to vector-Jacobian products through the denoiser, and its advantage over the strongest baselines (DDNM, SDA in Table 7) is modest on several imaging tasks, suggesting the practical gains are most pronounced in high-noise or high-dimensional settings where particle-based methods fail.
