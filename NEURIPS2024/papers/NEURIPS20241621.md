## Problem

Identifying causal effects from observational data is often impossible due to unmeasured confounding, and direct randomized experiments on the target variables may be infeasible or prohibitively expensive. The minimum-cost intervention design (MCID) problem—finding the cheapest set of proxy experiments that makes a target causal effect identifiable—was shown to be NP-complete, but the existing algorithm requires solving exponentially many NP-hard hitting-set subproblems and scales super-exponentially in the number of districts, rendering it intractable even for modest graphs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241621/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an acyclic directed mixed graph (ADMG) with directed and bidirected edges, a disjoint treatment set X and outcome set Y, and a per-vertex cost function C(v) that may assign infinite cost to infeasible interventions. For the general MCID problem, the output is a family of intervention sets (one per district of S = Anc_{V\X}(Y)) whose total cost is minimized subject to each district's hedges being hit. For the adjustment-criterion variant, the output additionally includes a generalized adjustment set Z such that P_X(Y) = E_{P_I}[P_I(Y | X, Z)].

## Method

The paper reformulates MCID as a weighted partial MAX-SAT (WPMAX-SAT) instance by constructing a 3-SAT formula whose boolean variables x_{i,j} track whether vertex v_i is reachable from S after j alternating directed/bidirected BFS iterations; hard clauses encode the hedge-hitting constraint while soft clauses with weights C(v_i) encode cost minimization. For multiple districts, the construction introduces indicator variables z_{k,ℓ} and r copies of the formula per district, yielding quadratic (rather than super-exponential) scaling in the number of districts. An equivalent integer linear program is obtained by translating each clause to a linear inequality, enabling use of solvers like Gurobi. Separately, a polynomial-time algorithm (Algorithm 1) solves the minimum-cost generalized-adjustment problem by reducing it to a minimum vertex cut on a constructed network where each vertex is split into two nodes with costs 0 and C(v), respectively. Empirically, the WPMAX-SAT formulation solved with RC2 is 4–6 orders of magnitude faster than the prior state-of-the-art, and the polynomial-time adjustment heuristic consistently outperforms earlier heuristics in normalized cost.

## Results

On synthetic Erdős–Rényi ADMGs (single-district, n=20–100), WPMAX-SAT is 4–5 orders of magnitude faster than the prior state-of-the-art Minimal Hedge Solver (MHS) from Akbari et al. 2022, while ILP is 1–2 orders of magnitude faster. In the multiple-district setting (n=20, 1–9 districts), WPMAX-SAT and ILP scale polynomially (quadratically) in the number of districts, whereas MHS scales super-exponentially. On 17 real-world networks from the Bayesian Network Repository, WPMAX-SAT and ILP outperform MHS by an average factor of >100×, exceeding 1000× on the largest network (link). The proposed polynomial-time heuristic (Algorithm 1) consistently achieves lower normalized cost than both H₁ and H₂ from Akbari et al. 2022 across all graph sizes (n=10–200) and edge densities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the prior work of Akbari et al. (ICML 2022), which provides both an exact algorithm (MHS, Algorithm 2) and two heuristic algorithms (H₁, H₂). On the exact-solution front, WPMAX-SAT dominates MHS by 4–5 orders of magnitude in runtime on single-district graphs and by >100× on average (up to >1000×) on real-world Bayesian networks; ILP also beats MHS by 1–2 orders of magnitude. Critically, in the multi-district regime, MHS's runtime grows super-exponentially with the number of districts (due to enumerating all set partitions), while WPMAX-SAT and ILP grow only quadratically. On the heuristic front, Algorithm 1 (minimum vertex cut on a moralized graph) consistently outperforms H₁ by 1–2 orders of magnitude in normalized cost on real-world data, and also beats H₂ (which focuses on directed-edge cuts) across all synthetic settings. The paper thus establishes a clear and large margin of improvement over the only prior method on both exact and approximate solution quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Akbari et al. (ICML 2022), which first formalized the Minimum-Cost Intervention Design (MCID) problem for causal effect identification and proved its NP-completeness, but whose algorithm (MHS) required solving exponentially many minimum hitting set instances with super-exponential scaling in the number of districts. The identifiability criterion via hedges (Proposition 1) is inherited from Lee et al. (UAI 2020) and Kivva et al. (UAI 2022), while the adjustment-based sub-problem (Section 4) generalizes the classical adjustment criterion of Shpitser et al. (UAI 2010) to the interventional setting. The key departure is a reformulation of MCID as a single WPMAX-SAT or ILP instance, replacing the prior doubly-exponential search with a problem solvable by off-the-shelf solvers (RC2, Gurobi).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 4–6 orders-of-magnitude speedup and the reduction from super-exponential to quadratic scaling in the number of districts make proxy experiment design practically tractable for graphs with dozens of variables, which was previously infeasible. The polynomial-time adjustment-based heuristic (Algorithm 1) provides a fast, interpretable fallback that consistently outperforms prior heuristics, and its minimum-vertex-cut formulation may be reusable in related causal design problems. Open questions include the development of approximation algorithms with provable guarantees for MCID (noted as future work by the authors), scaling to very large graphs where even SAT/ILP solvers struggle, and extending the framework to settings with stochastic interventions or partial cost information.
