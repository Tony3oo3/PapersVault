## Problem

While transformers are trained on next-token prediction, it remains unclear what computational structure this objective induces in their internal representations. This paper asks whether the geometry of belief updating over hidden states of the data-generating process—formalized by the mixed-state presentation (MSP) from computational mechanics—is linearly represented in the transformer residual stream, and if so, how this representation is distributed across layers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242386/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes token sequences generated by small hidden Markov models (Mess3 with 3 hidden states over vocabulary {A,B,C}; RRXOR with 5 hidden states over {0,1}) and the corresponding 64-dimensional residual stream activations recorded at every context-window position across all input sequences. It produces an affine map (weight matrix W ∈ R^{|S|×d_resid} and bias c ∈ R^{|S|}) that projects activations onto the probability simplex over hidden states, revealing the belief state geometry. For processes with next-token degeneracies, the projection must be applied to the concatenation of activations across all layers rather than a single layer.

## Method

The paper grounds its predictions in the theory of optimal prediction: the MSP defines a unique metadynamic over belief states (probability distributions over HMM hidden states) that an optimal predictor must track, and these belief states inherit a geometric structure in the probability simplex that can be highly fractal. Small transformers (4 layers, 64-dim residual stream, 1 attention head, ReLU, trained with SGD for 1M epochs) are pretrained on HMM-generated sequences, and linear regression is then used to find the affine subspace of the residual stream that best preserves the ground-truth belief state geometry. The key empirical finding is that this fractal geometry is faithfully and linearly represented in the residual stream, emerging gradually during training. For the RRXOR process—where distinct belief states share identical next-token distributions—the geometry is not captured in any single layer but is recovered only from the concatenation of all layers, demonstrating that belief state information is distributed across depth. Pairwise distance analysis confirms that the represented geometry tracks ground-truth belief state distances (R²=0.95) rather than next-token prediction distances (R²=0.31 for RRXOR), establishing that the representation encodes information about the entire future beyond the local prediction objective.

## Results

For the Mess3 process (3 hidden states, fractal MSP), a 2D linear subspace of the 64-dimensional final residual stream closely reproduces the predicted fractal belief state geometry, with MSE decreasing monotonically throughout training. For the RRXOR process (5 hidden states, 36 belief states in a 4-simplex), the belief state geometry is not represented in any single layer but is faithfully recovered from the concatenation of all 4 layers' residual activations. Critically, pairwise distances in the transformer's belief state representation correlate with ground-truth belief state distances at R² = 0.95 for RRXOR, while correlation with next-token prediction distances is only R² = 0.31, demonstrating the representation encodes information well beyond the explicit next-token training objective. Cross-validation (20% training data) preserves the geometry, and a shuffle control collapses the projection to the simplex center, confirming the result is not an artifact.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against other methods or baselines in the traditional sense; it is a discovery paper establishing that belief state geometry is linearly represented in transformer residual streams. The key internal comparison is between the belief state geometry representation and the next-token prediction representation: for RRXOR, the belief state geometry (R² = 0.95) far outperforms next-token predictions (R² = 0.31) in explaining pairwise distances in the residual stream, while for Mess3 (which has no next-token degeneracies) both are well-correlated. The paper validates its findings through several controls rather than external baselines: (1) the fractal structure emerges gradually over training rather than being present at initialization, (2) cross-validation with only 20% of data preserves the geometry, (3) shuffling input-belief correspondences collapses the projection, and (4) lower MSE in the belief state regression correlates with lower cross-entropy validation loss. The RRXOR result that geometry is spread across layers (not present in any single layer) is contrasted with Mess3 where it persists to the final layer, providing a mechanistic explanation tied to next-token degeneracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper grounds its predictions in computational mechanics, specifically the mixed-state presentation (MSP) formalism from Riechers & Crutchfield (2018) and the broader theory of optimal prediction from Shalizi & Crutchfield (2001), which characterize the minimal structure an observer needs to perform Bayesian belief updating over hidden states. It departs from prior transformer interpretability work (emergent world models in Othello, days-of-the-week geometry, Future Lens) by providing a precise, data-driven geometric prediction—derived from the training process's HMM structure—rather than post-hoc discovery of representations. The paper also connects to meta-learning frameworks (Ortega et al. 2019) that build state machines of sufficient statistics, noting the MSP states correspond to minimal sufficient statistics for prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a general, architecture-independent framework linking training data structure to activation geometry, suggesting that next-token prediction training induces representations of the entire future rather than just the immediate next token. Key open questions include how the framework extends to natural language (where the belief simplex dimension vastly exceeds the residual stream dimension, requiring compression), non-stationary and non-ergodic processes, and whether the observed linearity is a property of SGD-trained transformers or an architectural necessity. The small-scale experimental validation (3–5 state HMMs, 2–3 token vocabularies, 64-dim transformers) limits confidence in direct applicability to modern LLMs, though the authors argue the framework should generalize.
