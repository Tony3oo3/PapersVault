## Problem

Existing LLM defense strategies (e.g., ICD, SafeDecoding) effectively reduce harmful responses to malicious instructions but cause LLMs to adopt a rejection-oriented stance, dramatically increasing false rejections of benign instructions and degrading response quality. This creates a seesaw effect where safety gains come at the cost of usability, making these defenses impractical for real-world deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242779/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text instruction (user prompt) as input and produces a text response as output. Unusually, MoGU is applied only during the decoding of the first 5 tokens; the remaining tokens are generated by the base LLM to preserve decoding efficiency. The router operates at every O_proj layer within the transformer, receiving the hidden state vector at each layer and producing a per-token scalar weight that blends the two LoRA-variant outputs before they propagate forward.

## Method

MoGU uses LoRA to create two extreme variants of a base LLM: a Glad Responder trained (with contrastive loss) to generate compliant responses even to malicious instructions, and an Unwilling Responder trained to reject even benign instructions. A lightweight dynamic router, consisting of two low-rank linear networks with sigmoid outputs, is embedded at each O_proj layer and trained to assign per-token weights that mix the two variants' output vectors. The router is trained with a combined objective: a cross-entropy loss on correct response generation plus an L1-norm constraint that explicitly pushes w_glad toward 1 for benign inputs and w_unwill toward 1 for malicious inputs. At inference, only the first 5 tokens are decoded through the full MoGU mixture, after which the base LLM resumes decoding, limiting the computational overhead to roughly 3.9% additional parameters.

## Results

On red-team evaluation (Table 1), MoGU achieves 0.00% ASR on both Advbench and Malicious for Llama2, and 0.00%/0.50% for Vicuna, matching or exceeding all baselines on these two models. On Falcon, MoGU records 0.91%/17.50% ASR, trailing SafeDecoding (0.00%/0.50%) but still substantially outperforming ICD (1.82%/3.00%) and Self-Examine (55.91%/23.50%). The critical differentiator is usability (Table 3): MoGU preserves GPT-Eval scores near the no-defense baseline (e.g., 3.87 vs. 3.87 on Llama2; 3.02 vs. 3.03 on Falcon) while ICD drops Llama2's score to 2.17 (rejection rate 92.25%) and SafeDecoding drops Falcon's to 1.33 (rejection rate 97.13%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoGU outperforms SFT (Zhou et al.) on Llama2 (0.00% vs. 0.50% Malicious ASR) and Vicuna (0.50% vs. 6.00%), but trails on Falcon (17.50% vs. 1.00%). Against Detect_inp (Kumar et al.), MoGU wins decisively on Vicuna (0.50% vs. 32.00% Malicious ASR) and Falcon (17.50% vs. 23.50%), while tying on Llama2. MoGU dominates Self-Examine (Helbling et al.) across all three models, most dramatically on Falcon where Self-Examine fails catastrophically (55.91% Advbench ASR vs. MoGU's 0.91%). Retokenization (Jain et al.) and Self-Reminder (Wu et al.) are consistently outperformed by MoGU on all models. The most telling comparisons are with ICD (Wei et al.) and SafeDecoding (Xu et al.): while SafeDecoding achieves lower average ASR (1.42% vs. 3.15%), it degrades Falcon's usability score from 3.03 to 1.33 with a 97.13% rejection rate, whereas MoGU maintains 3.02 with only 4.88% rejection. Similarly, ICD achieves 0.00% ASR on Llama2 but collapses usability to 2.17 (92.25% rejection), while MoGU preserves 3.87 (16.50% rejection). On Vicuna jailbreak attacks (Table 2), MoGU achieves the best average ASR (3.20%) versus SafeDecoding (16.00%) and ICD (37.60%), confirming its advantage in the most safety-critical setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the dynamic routing mechanism from Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures to the LLM safety domain, combining it with LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Rather than routing between heterogeneous expert networks, MoGU routes between two LoRA-adapted variants of the same base model: a 'Glad Responder' trained to always comply and an 'Unwilling Responder' trained to always reject. The training incorporates contrastive learning to sharpen each responder's extreme behavior, and the router is regularized with L1 norm constraints to enforce the expected weight-assignment pattern. This departs from prior defense strategies (ICD, SafeDecoding, input detection) that operate as monolithic safety filters, by explicitly modeling the safety-usability trade-off as a continuous routing problem rather than a binary classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and quantifies a practical 'seesaw effect' where existing defense strategies (e.g., ICD, SafeDecoding) dramatically degrade usability—causing 92–97% rejection rates on benign instructions—making them impractical for real deployment. MoGU offers a lightweight solution (~3.9% additional parameters) that preserves usability while maintaining strong defense, validated across five 7B models and multiple attack types. However, open questions remain: the framework is only tested on 7B models, uses only 600 training instructions, applies routing solely to the O_proj layer and only the first 5 decoded tokens, and shows notably weaker performance on Falcon (17.5% ASR on the Malicious set). Whether the approach scales to larger models, benefits from more training data, or generalizes to other linear layers remains unexplored.
