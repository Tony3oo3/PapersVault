## Problem

Learning-based grasp detection in cluttered scenes suffers from incomplete 3D geometry due to occlusions and limited field of view, but scanning all views to achieve full coverage incurs prohibitive time costs. Existing active perception approaches either treat grasp detection as a secondary task of 3D reconstruction (selecting views that cover unobserved space rather than grasp-relevant regions) or rely on a specific grasp detection network's output for view planning, limiting generalization to novel environments.

## Input / Output

The system consumes RGB and depth images from a robot-mounted RealSense D435i camera, processed incrementally as the camera moves in small steps (10 cm step size, up to 10 steps). The depth image is fed to a pre-trained GSNet first-stage graspness network to produce per-pixel graspness pseudo-labels used for NGF training. Outputs include the online-optimized Neural Graspness Field (encoding both TSDF geometry and graspness distribution via axis-aligned feature planes), next-best-view selections for camera movement, and 6-DoF grasp poses generated by decoding graspness from the NGF at surface positions and applying Furthest Point Sampling above a threshold.

## Method

The core contribution is a Neural Graspness Field (NGF) built on the ESLAM framework, which adds a graspness prediction branch alongside the standard appearance and TSDF branches, all decoded from axis-aligned feature planes via MLPs and rendered through SDF-based volume rendering. The NGF is optimized online during camera movement by back-propagating a rendering loss that matches the rendered view graspness to pseudo-labels predicted by a pre-trained graspness network from the corresponding depth image. For next-best-view planning, the paper defines information gain as the absolute discrepancy between the graspness rendered by the current NGF and the pseudo-graspness obtained by passing the NGF's rendered depth through the same pre-trained graspness network, selecting the candidate view with the largest inconsistency. At inference, grasp positions are sampled by decoding the NGF's graspness branch at surface points and applying Furthest Point Sampling to positions exceeding a threshold, replacing direct grasp distribution prediction from explicit 3D geometry.

## Results

On the GraspNet-1Billion benchmark (RealSense, 10 active views), the proposed method achieves AP of 55.12 (Seen), 52.85 (Similar), and 24.74 (Novel), outperforming ACE-NBV by 8.38%, 6.71%, and 2.98% respectively. In real-world experiments on a UR-10 robot arm, the method achieves a 74.67% grasp success rate (56/75), surpassing ACE-NBV (62.67%) and Close-loop NBV (70.67%). The average per-step runtime is 3.44 s, with NBV planning accounting for 29% and robot execution for 51% of the total time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baselines across every evaluation split. Against ACE-NBV [32] (the strongest active-grasping baseline), the gains are 8.38% AP on Seen, 6.71% on Similar, and 2.98% on Novel; notably, ACE-NBV plateaus after the first few views while the proposed method continues to improve as more views are added. Against Close-loop NBV [5], which treats grasp detection as a secondary task of 3D reconstruction, the improvements are even larger (11.28% AP on Seen). In the NBV-policy ablation (Fig. 4), the graspness-inconsistency policy also surpasses ActiveNeRF [26] and Uncertainty-policy [16], which select views based on geometric or appearance ambiguity rather than grasp-distribution discrepancy. In real-world grasping, the method achieves a 74.67% success rate, beating ACE-NBV by 12 percentage points and Close-loop NBV by 4 percentage points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ESLAM real-time SDF mapping framework by adding a graspness rendering branch, creating a Neural Graspness Field (NGF) that jointly models geometry and grasp distribution. Conceptually, it departs from two prior lines of active grasp perception: (1) reconstruction-driven NBV methods (Close-loop NBV, Kahn et al.) that treat grasping as a secondary task and select views to cover unobserved space, and (2) affordance-driven methods (ACE-NBV) that directly use a grasp network's output to score candidate views. The key departure is using graspness inconsistency between the NGF's rendered output and a pseudo-label from a pre-trained graspness network as the information gain signal, rather than geometry uncertainty or raw grasp scores, which the authors argue avoids local optima and reduces dependence on a single network's generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in robotic grasping: scanning all views is too slow for real-world deployment, yet incomplete geometry degrades grasp quality. The NGF-based approach offers a principled way to allocate limited perception budget toward grasp-relevant information rather than generic scene coverage. Open questions include scalability to dynamic scenes (acknowledged as a limitation), the sensitivity of the pseudo-label paradigm to the pre-trained graspness network's quality on truly novel object categories, and whether the differentiable information gain could be optimized jointly with camera pose rather than evaluated on discrete candidates. The method's reliance on a pre-trained graspness network (GSNet) as a fixed prior limits its adaptability to domains where that prior is weak.
