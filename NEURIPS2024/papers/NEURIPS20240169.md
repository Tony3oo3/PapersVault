## Problem

Fine-tuning LLMs on user-uploaded data under the Language-Model-as-a-Service (LMaaS) setting can compromise safety alignment with as few as 10 harmful examples, yet existing defenses that mix safety examples into the fine-tuning dataset require hundreds of examples to be effective. This paper asks how to defend against Fine-tuning based Jailbreak Attacks (FJAttack) efficiently using only a handful of safety examples.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240169/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During fine-tuning, the method consumes a combined dataset: the user's original fine-tuning data (system prompt + user input + assistant output) plus 11 safety examples whose system prompts are prefixed with a 150-token random secret string. At inference, the service provider prepends the same secret prompt to the system prompt of every user query before passing it to the model. The model outputs a safe refusal for harmful questions and a normal response for benign questions, with the secret prompt remaining invisible to end users.

## Method

The core idea draws an analogy to backdoor attacks: a small set of safety examples (harmful questions paired with safe answers) is prefixed with a random 150-token secret prompt acting as a 'backdoor trigger,' and this modified set is concatenated with the user's fine-tuning data before conditional fine-tuning. Because the user's data lacks the trigger while the safety examples carry it, the model learns a strong conditional association between the secret prompt and safe refusals. At deployment, the service provider prepends the secret prompt to all system prompts, activating the safety alignment for harmful queries while leaving benign generation unaffected. The use of random (rather than semantically meaningful) tokens as the trigger is critical: they are harder for users to guess and act as outlier data points that the model can more easily latch onto as a trigger. With only 11 prefixed safety examples, the method achieves near-original safety performance (ASR ~3.6% on Llama-2-7B-Chat, ~15% on GPT-3.5-Turbo) while preserving benign task accuracy on ARC-Challenge, MMLU, and MT-Bench.

## Results

On Llama-2-7B-Chat, Backdoor Enhanced Safety Alignment reduces the Attack Success Rate (ASR) from 94.91% (no defense) to 3.64%, nearly matching the original aligned model's 3.27%, while the Baseline defense only achieves 34.91%. On GPT-3.5-Turbo, the method reduces ASR from 75.64% to 14.91% (a 75% decrease over the Baseline's 60.00%) and lowers the GPT-4-evaluated Harmfulness Score from 4.55 to 1.73. Benign performance is preserved: ARC-Challenge accuracy, MMLU accuracy, and MT-Bench scores remain within ~1–2 points of the no-defense attacked model. Only 11 prefixed safety examples are required, versus 300 for the Baseline to reach comparable ASR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the direct safety-example mixing defense from Qi et al. (2023), which simply appends unmodified safety examples to the fine-tuning dataset. Backdoor Enhanced Safety Alignment decisively outperforms this baseline on both safety metrics: on Llama-2-7B-Chat, ASR drops from 34.91% (Baseline) to 3.64% (Ours), and the Harmfulness Score falls from 2.49 to 1.22; on GPT-3.5-Turbo, ASR drops from 60.00% to 14.91% and Harmfulness Score from 4.55 to 1.73. The efficiency gap is stark: the Baseline requires 300 safety examples to reach an ASR of 4.73%, while the proposed method achieves 3.64% with only 11 examples (a >27× reduction). On benign utility, the two methods are comparable—ARC-Challenge, MMLU, and MT-Bench scores differ by less than 1–2 points—confirming that the secret-prompt mechanism does not degrade general capability. The paper also validates the method under LoRA fine-tuning (ASR 2.91% vs. Baseline 40.36%) and against the Identity Role Shift Attack variant (ASR 0.36% on Llama-2, 4.7% on GPT-3.5), further confirming broad applicability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the backdoor attack paradigm from DNN security (Gu et al., Li et al.) as a defense mechanism against Fine-tuning based Jailbreak Attacks (FJAttack) identified by Qi et al. [5] and Yang et al. [6]. While [5] introduced both the FJAttack and a naive baseline defense (mixing safety examples into the fine-tuning set), this work inverts the backdoor concept: instead of an attacker embedding a trigger to induce malicious behavior, the service provider embeds a secret prompt trigger to enforce safety alignment. The method is positioned specifically for the LMaaS deployment setting, where service providers control fine-tuning and inference but users control the uploaded data — a threat model that prior defense work has not addressed with this level of efficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: LMaaS platforms (OpenAI, Google) are the dominant LLM deployment model, and the demonstrated vulnerability (safety compromised by ~10 harmful examples for under $0.20) is an urgent operational risk. The 27× efficiency gain over the baseline (11 vs. 300 safety examples) makes defense feasible at scale. Key limitations include: the method only applies during fine-tuning (not pre-training or RLHF alignment), the secret prompt must remain undisclosed to users to prevent adaptive attacks, and evaluation is limited to Llama-2-7B-Chat and GPT-3.5-Turbo. Whether this backdoor-style defense generalizes to the alignment stage or to larger/more capable models remains an open question.
