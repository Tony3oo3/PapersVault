## Problem

Category-selective neurons in primate visual cortex (e.g., body-selective cells in macaque STS) also respond to out-of-category stimuli, suggesting they encode shared visual features rather than semantic concepts. However, prior characterizations of these features remain coarse (e.g., 'spiky' vs. 'stubby'), lacking a fine-grained, single-neuron, single-image method to identify which specific image regions in an out-of-category stimulus drive a given neuron's response.

## Input / Output

The method takes an out-of-category image (e.g., a 280×280 object photo resized to 224×224 for the network), a set of within-category images (e.g., 475 monkey avatar poses), and a pretrained adversarially-trained ResNet-50 cut at the last ReLU of layer 4.1 with a Gaussian spatial readout. It produces a most-similar within-category reference image and a pair of pixel-level saliency maps (one per image) whose L2 norm is analytically bounded by the neuron-specific similarity score, so low similarity yields low-intensity maps. The readout vector w is trained exclusively on within-category neural responses, so generalization to objects is untrained.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-step pipeline: (1) train a sparse linear readout w (L1/2 regularized) on top of a frozen ResNet-50 to predict neural responses to within-category stimuli only; (2) define a neuron-specific similarity as the cosine similarity of latent activations weighted by the Hadamard product with w, then select the most similar within-category image; (3) perform parallel backpropagation by computing per-feature pixel gradients for both images, smoothing and normalizing each to unit L2 norm, then reweighting feature i by β^(i) = a_in^(i)·w^(i)·a_out^(i)·w^(i) / (‖a_in⊙w‖₂·‖a_out⊙w‖₂) before summing. This reweighting ensures only features simultaneously present in both images and relevant to the neuron receive high saliency, and the resulting map's total intensity is bounded above by the similarity score. The procedure is agnostic to the underlying attribution backbone (vanilla gradients or Integrated Gradients) and compatible with any CNN-based neuron model.

## Results

The neuron model, trained exclusively on 475 macaque body images, generalizes to 6,857 object images with 93.7% of posterior-region channels and 95.3% of anterior-region channels showing a significant positive correlation between predicted and recorded responses. The parallel backpropagation method produces saliency maps whose L2 norm is provably bounded by the neuron-specific similarity score, ensuring that dissimilar image pairs yield low-intensity visualizations. Qualitatively, the visualizations reveal that object parts (extended structures, spiky protrusions, rounded shapes) correspond to specific body parts (arms/shoulders, limbs, torsos) in the CNN's latent space, explaining why non-body objects activate body-selective neurons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not include a direct quantitative comparison against competing visualization methods; it is a method-and-application study rather than a benchmark. The closest empirical comparison is the model's own generalization test: a linear readout trained solely on body images predicts object-image responses with significant positive correlation in >93% of channels, demonstrating that the visual features driving body responses are shared with objects. Conceptually, the method extends the gradient-based attribution of Simonyan et al. [16] by adding a neuron-specific reweighting that highlights only features shared between two images, going beyond what standard saliency maps (which ignore the reference image) can reveal. In the appendix, the authors verify that replacing vanilla backpropagation with Integrated Gradients [18] yields nearly identical visualizations, confirming the method's robustness to the choice of attribution backbone. The approach also improves on the earlier fragment-based analysis of Popivanov et al. [11] by being fully automated and high-throughput, enabling simultaneous visualization across many neurons and objects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CNN-plus-linear-readout paradigm for modelling neural responses (Yamins et al., Cadena et al.) and on gradient-based attribution methods (vanilla backpropagation, Integrated Gradients) originally designed for classification saliency. The key departure is a reweighting scheme that replaces the standard neuron-relevance weights w with a combined weight β that also encodes shared activation between an out-of-category image and a within-category reference, effectively turning a single-image attribution problem into a parallel two-image shared-feature visualization. It extends the line of work showing that category-selective cells respond to non-semantic visual features (Vinken et al. for face cells, Popivanov et al. for body-cell fragments) by providing a high-throughput, class-agnostic computational method to identify *which* specific features are shared, rather than merely demonstrating that responses generalize.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a concrete, interpretable mechanism for why category-selective neurons fire to out-of-category stimuli, moving beyond coarse global descriptors (roundness, spikiness) to single-image, single-neuron feature identification. The visualizations revealing that extended object structures activate the same latent dimensions as arms/shoulders provide a mechanistic account of body-cell selectivity that is testable in closed-loop experiments. Limitations include dependence on the quality of the underlying CNN model and the attribution backbone, the requirement of non-negative activations for the saliency bound, and validation only on body-selective STS regions. Open questions include whether the same approach yields informative visualizations for face patches, and whether the highlighted fragments genuinely drive neural responses when presented in isolation.
