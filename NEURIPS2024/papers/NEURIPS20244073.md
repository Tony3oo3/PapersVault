## Problem

Mobile health interventions require bandit algorithms that can handle nonstationary, complex baseline rewards (e.g., step counts evolving over time and context) while simultaneously learning heterogeneous treatment effects that vary by user and over time. Existing action-centered bandit methods (Greenewald et al., 2017) provide sublinear regret in nonstationary settings but fail to pool information across users, model treatment effect heterogeneity, or denoise the highly variable pseudo-rewards, leading to slow learning over finite horizons.

## Input / Output

At each decision point for user i at time t, the algorithm receives a context vector S_{i,t} (e.g., prior step counts, rehabilitation phase), known assignment probabilities π_{i,t}, and a user/time network graph encoding which users or time points are neighbors. It outputs a binary action (send notification or do nothing) along with a control probability π_{i,t}(0|s) clamped to [π_min, π_max], where the non-baseline arm is selected via argmax over the Thompson-sampled parameter. The staged recruitment design means the data array is triangular: at stage k, users 1 through k are observed, with user i having k−i+1 observations.

## Method

RoME constructs a doubly robust pseudo-reward R̃^f_{i,t} that combines a flexible supervised-learning prediction of the differential reward (f_{i,t}(s,ā) − f_{i,t}(s,0)) with an IPW debiasing term, ensuring unbiasedness as long as either the assignment probability or the working model is correct. The differential reward is modeled as a mixed-effects linear function Δ_{i,t}(s,a) = x(s,a)ᵀ(θ^shared + θ_i^user + θ_t^time), estimated via weighted least squares with a Laplacian network-cohesion penalty that shrinks neighboring user and time random effects toward each other. Action selection uses Thompson sampling: the algorithm draws a perturbed parameter θ̃_{i,t} = C_{i,t}θ̂ + β_{i,t}(δ)·V̲_{i,t}^{−1/2}η from a Gaussian or t-distribution, selects the best non-baseline arm, and computes the control probability as Pr(xᵀθ̃ < 0) clamped to [π_min, π_max]. The key theoretical result is a high-probability regret bound of Õ(√K · d^{3/2}) that depends only on the differential-reward dimension d, not on the complexity of the (potentially highly nonlinear) baseline reward model.

## Results

In the Nonlinear simulation setting (the most challenging, with nonlinear baseline, user-specific, and time-specific parameters), RoME outperformed every competitor in at least 48 of 50 repetitions, with pairwise win rates of 96–100% against all baselines. On the Valentine Study (N=108, K=120 stages), RoME achieved a 3.5% increase in step count relative to the constant randomization policy (≈140 additional steps/day), with its average reward exceeding the 75th percentile of all competing methods and achieving statistically significant superiority (p ≤ 0.01) over all five baselines. On the Intern Health Study (N=1553, K=30 stages), RoME achieved significantly higher rewards than three competitors while performing comparably to the AC algorithm.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoME substantially outperforms the Action-Centered (AC) algorithm of Greenewald et al. (2017) across all three simulation settings, winning 100% of repetitions in the Nonlinear setting and achieving significantly higher off-policy rewards in both the Valentine Study (p ≤ 0.01) and the Intern Health Study. Against IntelligentPooling (Tomkins et al., 2021), RoME wins 98% of repetitions in the Nonlinear simulation and significantly outperforms it in the Valentine Study; the gap is driven by RoME's ability to model nonlinear baselines via DML, which IntelligentPooling cannot do. RoME also dominates the Neural-Linear method (Riquelme et al., 2018) in all settings (100% win rate in Nonlinear), demonstrating that correctly modeling the baseline reward alone is insufficient without network cohesion pooling and mixed-effects structure. In the Valentine Study, RoME's average reward exceeded the 75th percentile of all competing methods, and in the IHS it matched the AC algorithm while significantly outperforming IntelPooling, Neural-Linear, and Standard.

## Relation to Prior Work

RoME synthesizes three previously separate lines of work into a unified mHealth bandit algorithm: the action-centered pseudo-reward construction of Greenewald et al. (2017) for handling nonstationary baselines, the mixed-effects model structure of Tomkins et al. (2021) for pooling across users and time, and the debiased machine learning framework of Chernozhukov et al. (2018) for flexible baseline estimation. The key departure from prior mixed-effects bandit methods (Tomkins et al., Hu et al.) is that RoME does not require correct specification of the conditional mean reward model, and its regret bound depends only on the differential-reward dimension rather than the potentially high-dimensional baseline. Nearest-neighbor regularization via Laplacian penalties, adapted from Ma et al. (2011) and Cesa-Bianchi et al. (2013), provides the mechanism for network-cohesion-based information pooling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practically deployable algorithm for mHealth intervention optimization that simultaneously handles user heterogeneity, temporal nonstationarity, and complex nonlinear baselines—challenges that prior methods addressed only in isolation. The regret bound's independence from baseline complexity is a clean theoretical guarantee that makes the method applicable even when the baseline reward is highly structured. Open questions include whether Assumptions 8–9 remain plausible for multi-arm settings (q>1), how to handle long-term treatment fatigue, and whether the computational cost (dominated by ML model fitting) scales to large nonclinical deployments. The 3.5% step-count improvement in the Valentine study, while modest, demonstrates real-world relevance for cardiac rehabilitation.
