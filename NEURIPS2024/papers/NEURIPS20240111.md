## Problem

Matching minimax rates for distributed goodness-of-fit testing of discrete distributions were previously known only when each server holds a single observation (n=1). In the large-sample regime where each server holds many observations, the techniques from the n=1 case (combinatorial likelihood expansions) do not generalize, and the functional-analytic tools available for the Gaussian model (Brascamp-Lieb inequality) cannot be directly applied to the multinomial model due to its lack of quadratic log-likelihood structure.

## Input / Output

The framework takes m servers each holding n i.i.d. draws from a d-category multinomial distribution, subject to either a b-bit per-server bandwidth constraint or a local (ε,δ)-differential privacy constraint, with the regime condition md log d / √n → 0. The output is the minimax separation rate ρ* characterizing the detection boundary (risk → 0 when ρ ≫ ρ*, risk → 1 when ρ ≪ ρ*), along with a binary test decision T(Y) ∈ {0,1} computed centrally from aggregated transcripts. The rates exhibit distinct phase transitions depending on whether shared randomness is available and on the relative magnitudes of b, d, m, n, and ε.

## Method

The core technique is Le Cam's statistical equivalence theory applied in a distributed setting. The paper proves a distributed transfer lemma (Lemma 4/5) showing that if m·Δ(𝒬;𝒫) ≤ ϱ, then minimax testing risks under bandwidth or local DP constraints differ by at most 2ϱ between two models. Leveraging Carter's (2002) result that the multinomial model is within Le Cam distance O(d log d / √n) of a d-dimensional Gaussian location model, the paper transfers the known minimax rates for distributed Gaussian signal detection (from Szabo et al. 2023 for bandwidth, Cai et al. 2024 for DP) to the multinomial model. The proof also establishes a converse: by identifying bandwidth-constrained regimes where the multinomial model permits consistent testing but the Gaussian model does not, the paper proves the two models are asymptotically non-equivalent when d/n is large, explaining the 'communication super-efficiency' phenomenon observed in the n=1 regime.

## Results

In the large sample regime (md log d / √n → 0), the minimax separation rate for uniformity testing under b-bit bandwidth constraints with shared randomness is ρ² ≍ (d/(√(d∧b)·mn)) ∧ (√d/(√m·n)), exactly matching the rate for the d-dimensional distributed Gaussian model [85]. Under local (ε,δ)-differential privacy with shared randomness, the rate is ρ² ≍ poly-log(d,m,n)·{d/(mnε²) if ε ≥ √d/√m; √d/(√(mnε)) if 1/√(md) ≤ ε < √d/√m}, matching [22]. Without shared randomness, both rates degrade by an additional factor of √d (bandwidth) or d/√m (privacy), reflecting the cost of independent local randomization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper contrasts its large-sample-regime rates with the n=1 (single observation per server) rates from [9,10,5]. In the n=1 regime, the bandwidth-constrained rate is ρ² ≍ d/(m·(2^b ∧ d)) with shared randomness, exhibiting exponential communication efficiency; in the large-n regime, the benefit of increased bandwidth is only sub-linear (√(d∧b) in the denominator), and no elbow effect appears. Similarly, the n=1 privacy rate is ρ² ≍ d/(mε²) with shared randomness, while the large-n rate introduces a phase transition at ε = √d/√m that has no analogue in the n=1 case. The paper further proves (Theorem 3) that the multinomial and Gaussian models are asymptotically non-equivalent when d/(n log d) ≥ C, explaining why the two regimes yield fundamentally different minimax behavior under bandwidth constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributed goodness-of-fit testing problem for discrete distributions from the single-observation-per-server regime (solved by Acharya, Canonne, and Tyagi [9,10] via chi-square contraction) to the large-sample regime where each server holds n observations. The key methodological departure is leveraging Le Cam's statistical equivalence theory: by invoking Carter's [30] bound on the deficiency distance between multinomial and multivariate Gaussian experiments, the authors transfer minimax rates from the well-characterized distributed Gaussian signal detection problem (Szabo et al. [85] for bandwidth; Cai et al. [22] for differential privacy) to the multinomial model, bypassing the need for direct functional-analytic lower-bound techniques that are intractable for discrete models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a significant gap by establishing matching minimax separation rates for the large-n distributed multinomial testing problem, a regime with direct applications to population genetics, information retrieval, and large language model evaluation. The non-equivalence result (Theorem 3) reveals a conceptual surprise: the multinomial and Gaussian models, while classically asymptotically equivalent, are fundamentally different in distributed settings when n is small relative to d, as evidenced by the 'communication super-efficiency' (exponential vs. linear bandwidth benefit) that distinguishes the two regimes. The main limitation is the requirement md log d / √n → 0, leaving intermediate and small-n regimes open; the authors note that direct analysis with new techniques may be necessary there, and that a different Gaussian surrogate might exist for the small-n case.
