## Problem

The Structural Probe (Hewitt & Manning, 2019) identifies a subspace of LLM activations where syntactically-related words are close, but it only captures the *existence* of a dependency relation via distance. Whether and how the *type* (label) and *direction* (head) of syntactic relations are encoded in neural activations remained unknown, leaving the full labeled directed dependency tree only partially recoverable from a single geometric code.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243343/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The probe consumes contextualized word embeddings from a single intermediate layer (typically layer 16) of an LLM and operates on directed edge embeddings (h_i − h_j) rather than individual word vectors. It outputs a single linear transformation B_P into a low-dimensional space (~128 dims, an order of magnitude smaller than the model's hidden size) from which three readouts are derived: squared norm gives syntactic distance, cosine angle against per-type prototype vectors gives the dependency label, and the sign of that cosine gives the head word (direction). Together these reconstruct a full labeled directed dependency tree per sentence.

## Method

The Polar Probe is a single linear map B_P trained by jointly minimizing two objectives: the Structural Probe's absolute-distance loss (matching squared Euclidean distance in probe space to tree distance over all word pairs) and a new Angular contrastive loss (pushing cosine similarity to 1 for same-type edges and to 0 for different-type edges, over syntactically-linked pairs only). The angular objective is the key novelty: it forces the probe space to organize dependency types along orthogonal axes, so that a simple nearest-prototype cosine readout recovers both the label and, via the sign of the cosine, the direction of each edge. Training uses Adam (lr 0.005, batch 200 sentences, 30 epochs) with a hyperparameter λ=10 balancing the two losses. The resulting probe achieves ~95% AUC on dependency-type classification and ~70 LAS on Llama-2-7B, roughly doubling the Structural Probe's labeled accuracy while preserving its UUAS performance.

## Results

On the English Web Treebank test set, the Polar Probe achieves a dependency-type AUC of 95%, substantially outperforming the Structural Probe (74%), the No-Probe baseline (80%), and a Polar Probe trained on a randomly initialized LLM (50%). At the optimal layer 16, Labeled Attachment Scores (LAS) reach 70.2 (BERT-large), 62.9 (Llama-2-7B), and 60.6 (Mistral-7B). The optimal probe dimensionality is k''=128, an order of magnitude smaller than the model's hidden size. On the controlled nested-sentence dataset, the Polar Probe produces consistent geometric representations across sentence complexity levels (Short, Relative Clause, Long-Nested).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Structural Probe (Hewitt & Manning, 2019), which recovers only the existence of syntactic relations via squared Euclidean distance. The Polar Probe matches the Structural Probe on UUAS (dependency existence) but outperforms it by approximately 80% in Label Accuracy and ~90% in LAS (labeled and directed edges), confirming that direction and type information is linearly readable in the same subspace. A 'No Probe' baseline (raw activations) achieves 80% AUC for dependency type, above the Structural Probe's 74%, suggesting that part-of-speech clustering in raw embeddings partially encodes syntactic type. A Polar Probe trained on a randomly initialized LLM drops to chance-level (50% AUC), confirming that the polar coordinate structure is not an artifact of the probe's optimization but is genuinely present in the pretrained model's representations. Overall, the Polar Probe wins on all type/direction metrics while tying on structure, making it a strict superset of the Structural Probe's capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Hewitt and Manning's (2019) Structural Probe, which showed that syntactic *distances* between words are linearly readable from LLM activations, by adding an angular/directional component that encodes the *type* and *direction* of dependency relations. The resulting Polar Probe is a single linear transformation jointly optimized with both a structural (distance) and an angular (contrastive) objective, completing the recovery of the full labeled, directed dependency tree from a subspace of activations. Conceptually, the work resolves a long-standing tension between symbolic linguistic theory (dependency trees as labeled, directed graphs) and the vectorial geometry of neural networks, building on Smolensky's tensor-product binding ideas and the broader probing literature (hyperbolic, orthogonal, and nonlinear variants).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple polar coordinate system (distance + angle in a ~128-dim subspace) suffices to recover the full dependency tree challenges theoretical predictions that symbolic structures require exponentially large vector spaces, and provides a concrete, interpretable hypothesis for how syntax may be represented in the human brain. Key limitations include English-only evaluation, a supervised probe design, and reliance on Euclidean geometry; open questions include generalization to other languages, morphological sub-word structure, phrase-structure formalisms, and whether an unsupervised variant could discover unexpected syntactic organization. The framework also opens the door to probing other compositional structures (semantics, knowledge graphs, visual binding) with the same distance-plus-direction code.
