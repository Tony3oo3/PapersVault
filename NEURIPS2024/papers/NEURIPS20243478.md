## Problem

Incorporating prior knowledge and expert beliefs into probabilistic predictive models typically requires specifying priors in closed form (e.g., GP kernels), which is difficult for non-specialists and limits context-aware analysis. While LLMs encode rich, grounded domain knowledge accessible through natural language, no systematic method existed for eliciting coherent, joint numerical predictive distributions from them that could be conditioned on both data and text.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243478/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes numerical training data as (x, y) pairs formatted as text tokens in a prompt, optional natural language text describing the problem context or prior beliefs, and a set of target query points. It produces joint predictive distributions over arbitrarily many targets, from which medians, percentiles, and full density estimates are extracted. Notably, the LLM is never fine-tuned—all conditioning happens through prompt construction—and missing data points are handled simply by omitting them from the prompt, while multi-dimensional inputs and outputs are accommodated by treating all variables as part of the same token sequence.

## Method

The paper defines LLM Processes (LLMPs) by eliciting numerical predictive distributions from pretrained LLMs via two mechanisms: rejection sampling (generating tokens and keeping only valid numbers) and a logit-based method that discretizes the continuous output space into bins and computes bin probabilities through hierarchical softmax over digit tokens, with a terminal token appended to each value to prevent prefix ambiguity across orders of magnitude. Two joint distribution constructions are proposed: I-LLMP factors the joint as independent marginals (satisfying the Kolmogorov Extension Theorem for a valid stochastic process) and A-LLMP uses an autoregressive chain rule where each target prediction conditions on previously generated targets, yielding richer dependencies at the cost of order-dependence. Key prompt engineering choices—distance-based ordering of training points relative to the query, specific separator formatting, and preserving original y-value scale—substantially improve performance. Through this lens, LLMPs are framed as Neural Processes that repurpose pretrained language models rather than training a dedicated architecture, enabling zero-shot probabilistic regression with well-calibrated, potentially multimodal uncertainty.

## Results

On the 12-function synthetic regression benchmark (Function Dataset), the Mixtral-8×7B A-LLMP achieves the lowest NLL on 10 out of 12 functions and equal or better MAE on 8 out of 12 compared to an RBF-kernel GP, with NLL values ranging from −2.38 (Linear) to 3.30 (X×Sine) versus GP values from −1.57 (Log) to 12.64 (X×Sine). On the weather forecasting task, A-LLMP outperforms LLMTime in both MAE and NLL, with the margin widening as training data is reduced. On the Original #1 regression benchmark, I-LLMP achieves an average error of 0.836 versus 3.137 for the concurrent method of Vacareanu et al., winning 78 of 100 individual trials.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the RBF-kernel GP, the A-LLMP wins on NLL for 10 of 12 synthetic functions (e.g., −2.38 vs −1.45 on Linear, −1.96 vs −1.44 on Sinc) but loses on MAE for functions like Gaussian Wave (0.24 vs 0.20) and Sine (0.27 vs 0.22). Against LLMTime on the weather forecasting task, A-LLMP achieves lower MAE and NLL across all training-data fractions, with the performance gap widening as more training points are removed, attributed to A-LLMP's ability to handle irregularly spaced data without NaN imputation and its avoidance of y-value rescaling. Against the concurrent method of Vacareanu et al. [11] on the Original #1 dataset, I-LLMP achieves a substantially lower average error (0.836 vs 3.137) and wins 78 of 100 individual trials, credited to distance-based prompt ordering and distributional (median) estimation versus single point estimates. Against Optuna on six multi-dimensional black-box optimization functions, LLMPs obtain as good or better approximations to the true maximum in fewer evaluation trials.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends LLMTime (Gruver et al., 2023), which demonstrated that LLMs can perform zero-shot 1D time series forecasting and elicited marginal probability distributions from LLM token posteriors, by generalizing to multi-dimensional regression, joint (not just marginal) predictive distributions via autoregressive sampling, and simultaneous conditioning on both numerical data and natural language text. The authors explicitly frame their LLM Processes as a special case of Neural Processes (connecting to Conditional NPs and Autoregressive NPs), where the 'training' is indirect—pretrained as language models rather than meta-trained on task distributions. Prompt engineering practices (distance-based ordering, terminal tokens, no y-rescaling) are developed empirically, departing from LLMTime's scaling approach which the authors argue removes useful prior information encoded in value magnitudes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that off-the-shelf LLMs can serve as competitive zero-shot probabilistic regressors (matching or beating GPs on NLL for most synthetic functions) while uniquely allowing users to inject domain expertise through plain language, lowering the barrier to probabilistic modeling for non-specialists. The text-conditioning experiments—where the LLM recalls actual historical precipitation patterns for Singapore and San Diego, or correctly interprets 'price goes to zero on day 30'—suggest LLMs encode rich, grounded functional priors that are accessible through prompting. Key limitations include high computational cost (600+ GPU hours for the full study), context window constraints on problem size, and uncertainty about what biases the underlying LLMs carry. Open questions include whether conditioning on other modalities (images, structured data) is possible, how to scale to larger target sets, and whether the observed text-conditioning effects persist with larger or more capable models.
