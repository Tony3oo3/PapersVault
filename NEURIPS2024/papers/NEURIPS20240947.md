## Problem

Existing 2D and 3D puzzle-solving benchmarks rely on synthetic fractures, square pieces, or small collections of real fragments, leaving a significant gap for evaluating methods on realistic archaeological reassembly. The RePAIR dataset addresses this by providing over 1000 real fresco fragments from Pompeii with irregular shapes, erosion, missing pieces, and multi-modal data (3D scans, 2D images, and archaeologist-annotated metadata), where current state-of-the-art methods show substantial performance gaps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240947/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 3D fragments as textured meshes (0.08 mm geometric accuracy from structured light scanning, 24.2 MP texture maps) and corresponding 2D rendered images of the painted surfaces, organized into 117 groups of 2–44 fragments plus 150 'isolated' fragments with unknown solutions. The 3D and 2D versions are linked through a known orthographic scaling factor (7.369 px/mm), enabling cross-modal evaluation. Outputs are 6DoF SE(3) transformation matrices for 3D reassembly and (x, y, θ) poses for 2D puzzle solving, evaluated via area/volume-overlap (Q_pos), translation/rotation RMSE, and neighbor-graph precision/recall/F1.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the dataset construction and benchmarking framework rather than a single solver. The digitization pipeline combines Polyga H3 structured light scanning with Sony α7c photography, using SfM-based photogrammetry and Screened Poisson mesh refinement to produce high-fidelity textured 3D models, followed by principal-curvature-based segmentation to isolate painted surfaces for 2D rendering. Ground truth was generated by archaeologists physically assembling fragments over years of fieldwork, with 3D transformations extracted via Blender. For benchmarking, the authors evaluate five 3D learning-based methods (Global/PointNet, PQ-Net/LSTM, DGL, SE(3)-Equiv, DiffAssemble) and three 2D methods (Derech et al.'s texture-based greedy solver, a novel genetic algorithm optimizing bounding-box area and overlap, and a greedy geometric matcher using Douglas-Peucker contour segmentation with spring-mass compatibility scoring), revealing that no method achieves competitive results on this realistic dataset.

## Results

On the RePAIR 3D benchmark, no method achieves a Q_pos above 0.127, with Global [49] leading at 0.127 (F1=0.683) and SE(3)-Equiv. [89] achieving the lowest rotation RMSE (70.8°) and translation RMSE (56.0 mm). In 2D, the best Q_pos is only 0.047 (Genetic Optimization) and the best F1 is 0.471 (Derech et al.), confirming that current SoTA methods struggle substantially on realistic archaeological fragments. Generalization experiments show that models trained on RePAIR transfer better to Breaking Bad (e.g., DGL Q_pos=0.296) than the reverse (DGL Q_pos=0.063), highlighting RePAIR's unique complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In 3D, Global [49] achieves the highest Q_pos (0.127) and F1 (0.683), while SE(3)-Equiv. [89] produces the most accurate poses with the lowest rotation RMSE (70.8°) and translation RMSE (56.0 mm) and the best precision (0.724). DiffAssemble [67] attains the highest recall (0.905) but at the cost of the lowest precision (0.506) and F1 (0.599), indicating it over-predicts neighbor relationships. DGL [94] and LSTM [90] fall in between, with DGL closer to Global in Q_pos (0.119) and LSTM closer to DiffAssemble in recall (0.880). In 2D, Derech et al. [20] leads in F1 (0.471) and precision (0.454), while the paper's own Genetic Optimization baseline surpasses it in Q_pos (0.047 vs. 0.037) and recall (0.662 vs. 0.527). Across all methods, the dramatic performance drop when generalizing from Breaking Bad to RePAIR (e.g., DGL Q_pos drops from 0.296 to 0.063) confirms that synthetic fracture patterns do not adequately prepare models for real-world archaeological fragmentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a large-scale real-world archaeological puzzle-solving dataset (RePAIR) that departs fundamentally from prior benchmarks, which rely on synthetic fractures (Breaking Bad, Crossing Cuts, DAFNE) or limited real fragments (Theran, Fantastic Breaks). Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the existing 2D and 3D reassembly task formulations—benchmarking methods like Derech et al.'s texture-based solver, and 3D approaches including PointNet-based Global, PQ-Net (LSTM), DGL, SE(3)-Equiv, and DiffAssemble—against a dataset with realistic erosion, missing pieces, and irregular fracture geometries that no prior benchmark captures at this scale. The digitization pipeline (structured light scanning + photogrammetry + Screened Poisson refinement) follows established cultural heritage acquisition practices but is applied at unprecedented fragment count (1070 pieces, 117 groups) with archaeologist-annotated ground truth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial performance gap: all SoTA methods achieve very low Q_pos scores (0.02–0.13) and moderate F1 scores on RePAIR, confirming that real-world puzzle solving with eroded, irregular fragments remains an open problem. The generalization asymmetry—models trained on RePAIR transfer better to Breaking Bad than vice versa—suggests that real-world complexity is not captured by synthetic fracture datasets, making RePAIR a necessary stress test. Open questions include how to exploit degraded geometry, incorporate archaeological domain knowledge (material, weight, back-side structure), and whether LLMs/MLLMs can provide the semantic reasoning that purely geometric methods lack. The dataset also opens the complementary task of shape repair/completion for eroded surfaces and missing pieces.
