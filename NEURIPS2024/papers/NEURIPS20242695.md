## Problem

On-device meteorological variable modeling faces three coupled obstacles: data heterogeneity across geographically diverse weather stations degrades federated learning performance, shallow networks cannot capture complex weather patterns while deep networks exceed edge-device compute budgets, and transmitting full model parameters during FL aggregation is impractical for real-time weather tasks. The paper asks whether pre-trained language models, which have absorbed massive sequential knowledge from natural language, can serve as compact, efficient foundation models for personalized on-device weather sequence modeling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242695/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each device provides a multivariate weather sequence of shape L×C at 1-hour temporal resolution, where L (input length, fixed at 192) and C (number of variables, ranging from 5 to 20+) can differ across stations. Temporal metadata (minutes, hours, days, weeks, months) is encoded alongside the raw values. The model outputs either a forecasted future sequence of length L'∈{96,192,336,720} or imputed values at masked positions (25%–50% masking), supporting both multivariate-to-multivariate and multivariate-to-univariate configurations.

## Method

LM-WEATHER implants a lightweight personalized adapter into a frozen GPT-2 backbone, consisting of a Task Adapter and a Parameter Adapter. The Task Adapter decomposes each univariate weather series into trend, seasonal, and residual components, then feeds each through a unified generator (1D convolution for token embedding, a lookup table for position embedding, and trainable parameters for temporal attributes) to produce three distinct adapter matrices that are concatenated with the patched input. Channel-Independent Patching (CIP) splits multivariate sequences into univariate patches processed by shared weights, and LoRA is applied to attention layers for cross-domain knowledge transfer from text to weather. During federated rounds, only the LoRA low-rank matrices (~3.7% of trainable parameters) are transmitted to the server and averaged via FedAvg, while the personalized adapters remain local—simultaneously resisting heterogeneity, preserving privacy, and reducing communication overhead by 27× compared to full-model FL baselines.

## Results

On the four real-world on-device weather datasets (ODW1T, ODW1V, ODW2T, ODW2V), LM-WEATHER achieves the best average MAE across all forecasting horizons (96–720), with average MAE of 45.4 (ODW1T), 45.6 (ODW1V), 66.9 (ODW2T), and 69.0 (ODW2V). On the imputation task (50% masking), LM-WEATHER similarly leads with average MAE of 23.1, 43.7, 38.8, and 31.1 on the four datasets respectively. The method communicates only 0.38M parameters (3.7% of the 10.38M trainable parameters) per round, compared to 100% for all baselines, while still outperforming them by 9.8% (vs. FL-GPT4TS) to 19% (vs. FL-PatchTST) in average MAE on forecasting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LM-WEATHER outperforms all nine FL baselines on both forecasting and imputation across all four datasets. Against the closest PLM-based competitor FL-GPT4TS, LM-WEATHER achieves a 9.8% average MAE reduction on forecasting and 5.7% on imputation, while its non-personalized variant (LM-WEATHER-AVE) still beats FL-GPT4TS by 4%. Against the strongest dedicated time-series baselines, LM-WEATHER improves average MAE by 11.2% over FL-DLinear and 19% over FL-PatchTST on forecasting. In the 5% few-shot setting, the gap widens dramatically: LM-WEATHER surpasses FL-PatchTST by 78%, FL-DLinear by 72.8%, and FL-LightTS by 14.3% on forecasting MAE, indicating that baselines struggle severely under data scarcity while the PLM backbone retains useful prior knowledge. In zero-shot cross-domain transfer (e.g., training on ODW1T, testing on ODW2V), LM-WEATHER-AVE achieves the best result in 18 out of 24 transfer directions, beating the second-best method by over 14.2% in average MAE on both forecasting and imputation. Crucially, LM-WEATHER communicates only 0.38M parameters per round (3.7% of trainable params), whereas all baselines transmit 100% of their parameters, making it 27.3× more communication-efficient than the standard FedAvg baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established lines: parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA), channel-independent patching for multivariate time series (PatchTST), and federated aggregation (FedAvg). It departs from the dominant paradigm of training weather foundation models from scratch on massive simulation data (CLIMAX, GraphCast, Pangu-Weather) by instead repurposing a pre-trained language model (GPT-2) as the backbone, arguing that sequential knowledge learned from text transfers to meteorological sequences. The personalized adapter design—decomposing weather into trend/seasonal/residual components to generate task-specific prompts—extends the prompt-based time series literature (GPT4TS, TEMPO, Time-LLM) into a federated, on-device setting where only low-rank matrices are communicated, distinguishing it from both centralized PLM-based time series methods and standard PFL approaches that share full model parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practically compelling alternative to the current trajectory of weather foundation models that require ~100TB of simulation data and weeks of GPU training, showing that a GPT-2-scale model with 3.7% parameter communication can outperform full-model FL baselines on real observational data. The four newly compiled real-world datasets (ODW1T/V, ODW2T/V) spanning Asia and North America/Israel with heterogeneous time spans and variable sets fill a genuine gap, as prior benchmarks relied on reanalysis products (ERA5) that introduce simulation bias. Open questions include whether the claimed cross-domain transfer from language to weather is truly causal or merely a strong inductive bias, whether the approach scales to larger LLMs or more variables, and how it performs under the extreme heterogeneity of truly global deployments. The authors themselves note future work on multimodal (text/image/time-series) weather data and finer spatial scales.
