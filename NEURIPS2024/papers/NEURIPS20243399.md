## Problem

GFlowNets trained with flow matching objectives tend to under-exploit high-reward objects when only a small fraction of the trajectories leading to those objects have been observed. The forward flow becomes under-determined because it is matched only to the observed backward flow, which partially represents the true reward, causing the forward policy to favor objects with high observed flow over genuinely high-reward objects. This pitfall persists even when the model has already seen the high-reward object, as the unobserved trajectories carry zero forward probability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a reward function R(x) defined over a compositional object space and an initial state, and produces objects sampled from a Boltzmann distribution proportional to R(x). Objects are constructed sequentially via state-transition trajectories (e.g., adding molecular fragments from a library of 72, appending/prepending amino acids up to length 8 or 15, or selecting nodes in a graph). The backward policy is trained on a replay buffer of observed trajectories, and the forward policy is updated to match forward flow to backward flow on those same trajectories, with the buffer typically retaining trajectories from the previous 20 rounds.

## Method

PBP-GFN introduces a pessimistic training step for the backward policy that maximizes the observed backward flow R_B(x) = Σ_{τ∈B(x)} R(x)P_B(τ|x) by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of observed trajectories under P_B (Eq. 5), effectively concentrating backward probability mass onto the trajectories already in the replay buffer. This shifts flow from unobserved to observed trajectories while preserving the total backward flow (i.e., the reward), so asymptotic optimality of flow matching is maintained. The key mechanism is that by reducing the unobserved backward flow, the flow matching error bound on the marginalized Boltzmann distribution is tightened (Appendix A, Eq. 6). The pessimistic backward policy is trained online alongside the forward policy in each round (Algorithm 1), and the approach is agnostic to the choice of flow matching objective (TB, DB, or subTB). The backward policy uses the same architecture as the forward policy but with separate parameters, and is trained with a learning rate of 1e-3.

## Results

Across eight benchmarks (hyper-grid, bag generation, maximum independent set, fragment-based molecule generation, TFBind8, and three RNA-binding tasks), PBP-GFN consistently outperforms all baselines in the number of high-reward modes discovered and the average top-100 score. On the 16×16×16 hyper-grid, PBP-GFN captures all modes and achieves the lowest L1 distance to the target Boltzmann distribution within 10⁵ trajectories, converging faster than TB, DB, Uniform, and MaxEnt baselines. In molecular generation, PBP-GFN achieves the highest top-100 average score and the most unique modes while maintaining low Tanimoto similarity (i.e., high diversity) among top-100 molecules. The relative mean error (measuring distance between empirical and target Boltzmann distributions) is lowest for PBP-GFN across all RNA sequence generation tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PBP-GFN outperforms all backward-policy design baselines (conventional TB/DB, Uniform, and MaxEnt) across every benchmark, with the most pronounced gains in the number of modes discovered and top-100 score on molecule and RNA generation tasks. Against training-objective baselines (subTB, GAFN) and the RL baseline (A2C) in molecular and RNA generation, PBP-GFN achieves higher top-100 scores and more modes while yielding comparable or lower Tanimoto similarity, confirming that the pessimistic backward policy is orthogonal to and complementary with improved credit-assignment objectives. In ablation comparisons (Figure 11), PBP-GFN shows performance competitive with off-policy exploitation methods (local search GFlowNets and reward-prioritized buffer) on RNA-C, and consistently improves further when combined with either method, demonstrating that the pessimistic backward policy is an independent source of gain. The MaxEnt backward policy, which emphasizes exploration, is the closest competitor on diversity metrics but is consistently outperformed on exploitation metrics (top-100 score, number of modes), highlighting the exploration–exploitation trade-off that PBP-GFN navigates more effectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GFlowNet training framework by identifying a specific failure mode in flow matching: when only a subset of object-sharing trajectories is observed, the forward policy under-exploits high-reward objects because the observed backward flow is too small to anchor the forward flow to the true reward. Prior backward policy designs—uniform (Malkin et al., 2022), MaxEnt (Mohammadpour et al., 2024), and sub-structure (Shen et al., 2023)—address exploration or credit assignment but do not directly reduce the unobserved backward flow that causes under-exploitation. PBP-GFN departs from these by training the backward policy to maximize the likelihood of observed trajectories, thereby concentrating backward flow on what has actually been seen, while preserving the total flow (and hence asymptotic optimality). The approach is orthogonal to the choice of flow matching objective (TB, DB, subTB) and composes with off-policy exploitation methods like local search and reward-prioritized buffers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The under-exploitation diagnosis is practically important because it explains why GFlowNets can converge to local optima that assign higher probability to lower-reward objects, a failure mode that is hard to detect without the flow decomposition analysis. The fix is minimal (a negative log-likelihood loss on the backward policy over the replay buffer) and consistently improves performance across eight diverse benchmarks spanning synthetic grids, combinatorial optimization, molecular generation, and RNA design. The main open question is the exploration-exploitation tradeoff: pessimistic training concentrates probability on observed trajectories, which may hinder discovery of unobserved high-reward regions in environments where exploration is critical. The authors suggest interpolation with explorative policies (e.g., MaxEnt) as a future direction, but this remains unexplored.
