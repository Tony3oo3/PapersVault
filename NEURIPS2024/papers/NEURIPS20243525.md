## Problem

Standard selective classification forces a binary choice—predict or reject—discarding potentially useful partial information when a model is uncertain about a specific class but confident about a broader category. This paper extends selective classification to a hierarchical setting, allowing models to partially reject predictions by retreating to less specific nodes in a class hierarchy, thereby preserving valuable information that would otherwise be lost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243525/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a class hierarchy tree (e.g., WordNet for ImageNet, a 7-level taxonomy for iNaturalist) as input. The base classifier's leaf softmax scores are aggregated to internal nodes (sum of descendant leaf probabilities), and after temperature scaling, the hierarchical inference rule selects a node at any level of the tree. The output is a predicted node in the hierarchy (not necessarily a leaf) whose coverage—defined as 1 minus the normalized entropy of that node relative to the root—quantifies the amount of information retained in the prediction.

## Method

The paper introduces the Climbing inference rule, which starts at the most probable leaf and ascends the path to the root until the node's aggregated confidence exceeds a threshold θ, satisfying both monotonicity in correctness and monotonicity in coverage. Internal node probabilities are computed as sums of calibrated leaf probabilities (via temperature scaling), which the authors show substantially improves hAURC over uncalibrated baselines. For threshold selection, the paper proposes an algorithm inspired by split conformal prediction that, given a target accuracy 1−α and confidence level 1−δ, computes a threshold guaranteeing the classifier's accuracy falls within a (1−δ)-confidence interval around 1−α, without requiring any model retraining. The framework is evaluated via hierarchical risk-coverage curves (hAURC) and Calibration-Coverage curves, with extensive experiments across 1,115 ImageNet classifiers revealing that CLIP, large-scale pretraining, and knowledge distillation disproportionately boost hierarchical selective performance.

## Results

On 1,115 ImageNet-1k models, the proposed Climbing inference rule achieves a mean hAURC of 36.51 (×1000), yielding a 14.94% hierarchical gain over the standard selective baseline (42.27). On iNat21 (6 models), Climbing achieves hAURC of 10.15 with a 23.94% hierarchical gain. The optimal threshold algorithm (Algorithm 2) reduces target accuracy error to 0.02% at 95% target accuracy (vs. 0.69% for DARTS) while maintaining higher coverage (0.76 vs. 0.74), and at 99.5% target accuracy achieves 0.26 coverage versus DARTS's 0.13.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Climbing inference rule decisively outperforms both baselines on hAURC: it achieves a 14.94% hierarchical gain over the standard selective baseline (42.27 → 36.51 hAURC on ImageNet-1k) and more than doubles the 6.92% gain of the Max-Coverage rule from Valmadre (2022). On iNat21, the advantage is even larger, with Climbing achieving 23.94% gain versus MC's 10.45%. For the threshold-finding task, the proposed algorithm (Algorithm 2) substantially outperforms DARTS (Deng et al., 2012): at 99% target accuracy, it achieves 0.02% mean accuracy error versus DARTS's 0.63%, and 0.40 coverage versus DARTS's 0.22. Conformal Risk Control (Angelopoulos et al., 2022) is also compared in Appendix H and exhibits significantly higher mean accuracy error than both the proposed algorithm and DARTS, particularly at 95% target accuracy. The paper further shows that CLIP with linear-probes provides the largest training-regime boost to hAURC (median ~38% relative improvement), far exceeding pretraining (~19%), semi-supervised learning (~19%), and knowledge distillation (~12%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends standard selective classification (Chow 1957; Geifman & El-Yaniv 2017) to a hierarchical setting, where instead of a binary predict/reject decision, the model can partially reject by retreating to ancestor nodes in a class tree. The closest prior work is DARTS (Deng et al. 2012), which optimized an accuracy-specificity trade-off via a Lagrangian but did not provide full risk-coverage control or a formal selective framework; Valmadre (2022) proposed a Max-Coverage inference rule but without the monotonicity guarantees or threshold-calibration mechanism introduced here. The threshold-finding algorithm draws on split conformal prediction (Vovk et al. 1999) and conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al. 2022), adapted to the hierarchical setting with a novel exchangeability-based guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HSC provides a practical, post-hoc framework for deploying classifiers in risk-sensitive hierarchical tasks (e.g., medical diagnosis, species identification) where a less-specific-but-correct prediction is far more valuable than outright rejection. The empirical finding that CLIP linear-probe training boosts hierarchical selective performance by over 40%—far exceeding other regimes—gives practitioners a concrete signal for model selection. Open questions include whether the framework extends beyond softmax confidence, whether training objectives can be designed specifically to optimize hAURC, and whether the calibration improvements generalize to non-ImageNet domains. The current evaluation is limited to ImageNet-1k and iNat21, and the CLIP finding rests on only 16 model pairs.
