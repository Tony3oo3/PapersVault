## Problem

Current best practices for training deep networks rely on optimizer-specific learning rate correction factors (e.g., μP) that are difficult to use, require separate tuning for each optimizer, and do not provide a unified theoretical foundation. The paper asks whether a single architecture-aware norm on the full weight space can make the optimal learning rate invariant to both width and depth for any base optimizer, without requiring optimizer-specific scale factors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242337/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes weight updates produced by any base optimizer (Adam, SGD with momentum) along with the network's module tree structure and user-specified mass parameters that apportion relative learning speed across input, hidden, and output layers. It produces unit-norm weight updates in the modular norm, which are then scaled by an explicit learning rate schedule. Spectral norms of constituent weight tensors are estimated online via two steps of power iteration per update, adding roughly 23% training overhead in their ResMLP experiments.

## Method

The modular norm is defined recursively over the network's module tree using only two binary operations—composition and concatenation—each with explicit rules for propagating four module attributes (forward function, mass, sensitivity, norm). The composite norm is an L∞ (max) combination of scaled sub-module norms, where the scaling factors are determined by the mass parameters and the sensitivity of downstream modules. The key theoretical result is that for networks built from well-normed atomic modules, the loss gradient is Lipschitz-continuous in the modular norm with a sharpness constant that admits a simple recursive formula and, for residual networks with appropriate block multipliers, is independent of depth. Practically, the norm is used as a wrapper that normalizes each layer's update to unit modular norm before applying a global learning rate, effectively balancing learning speed across the network and rendering the optimal learning rate transferable across width and depth. The open-source Modula package automates the entire construction, allowing users to build architectures from atomic modules (Linear, Embed, Conv2D) and bond modules (ReLU, LayerNorm, FuncAttention) with the norm inferred automatically.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative result is that the optimal learning rate, tuned on a small model, transfers well across both width and depth when updates are normalized in the modular norm. On GPT (OpenWebText, context length 128, 10k steps), normed Adam and normed SGD maintain near-optimal performance across varying numbers of transformer blocks and widths, whereas unnormed counterparts degrade. On CIFAR-10, the same learning-rate-transfer property holds for ResMLP and ResNet architectures. The computational overhead of modular normalization is approximately 23% (124±1 s vs. 101±1 s for a width-64 ResMLP with 8 blocks trained for 10k steps).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares normed versus unnormed versions of Adam and SGD as base optimizers on GPT (OpenWebText) and ResMLP/ResNet (CIFAR-10). In all settings, the normed variants exhibit significantly better learning-rate transfer: the learning rate tuned at a small scale remains close to optimal when width or depth is increased, while the unnormed variants require re-tuning. The paper also notes that their GPT architecture with standard (unnormed) Adam actually transfers learning rate slightly better than the nanoGPT baseline, suggesting the architectural choices (e.g., the 1/d_Q attention scaling) contribute independently of the normalization. No external optimizer baselines (e.g., μP, Prodigy) are directly compared in the main experiments, though the authors mention that Prodigy breaks at large widths due to its reliance on a lower bound on the initial learning rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly generalizes the layer-wise RMS-RMS operator norm of Yang et al. [14]—which enabled good width scaling for individual linear layers—into a full-network norm defined recursively over the module tree via composition and concatenation. It departs from the Tensor Programs / μP tradition [3,4,18] by eschewing infinite-width or infinite-depth asymptotic analyses in favor of finite, deterministic norm-based guarantees, and positions itself as simpler than universal majorize-minimize algorithms [27,28] while being more general than prior deep-network metrization work [12] that was restricted to MLPs or individual layers. The modular norm's max (L∞) combination of scaled layer norms, together with user-specified mass parameters, is the key conceptual departure from both the asymptotic and majorization-based approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a single norm-based wrapper makes learning rates transferable across width and depth for both Adam and SGD, eliminating the need for optimizer-specific correction factors and enabling simpler optimizers (e.g., SGD instead of Adam for GPT) with smaller memory footprints. The theoretical contribution—Lipschitz smoothness of the loss in the modular norm with a recursive sharpness formula—opens a path to porting standard optimization theory into deep learning. Key limitations include a ~23% training overhead from spectral norm estimation via power iteration, the fact that well-normed-ness is only guaranteed at initialization (not enforced during training), and the need to tune the mass parameter per architecture. Open questions include whether regularization can maintain well-normed-ness throughout training, whether cheaper norms (e.g., L∞-L∞) can reduce overhead, and whether the framework can support automatic step-size selection.
