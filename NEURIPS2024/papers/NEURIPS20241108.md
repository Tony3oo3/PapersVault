## Problem

Standard preference alignment methods (RLHF, DPO) require fine-tuning the pre-trained model and rely on explicit or implicit reward estimation, making them inapplicable to black-box APIs like GPT-4 and susceptible to reward overfitting in finite-data regimes. This paper asks whether one can align a frozen reference model with human preferences by learning a separate, lightweight transformation module that directly maps less-preferred outputs to preferred ones, without any reward model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241108/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a context x (a text prompt, image label, or RL state) and a sample y drawn from the frozen reference policy π_ref(·|x), then produces an aligned sample f(y) in the same output space by integrating the learned ODE from t=0 to t=1. For variable-length text, a T5 autoencoder first encodes the review into a fixed 1024-dimensional embedding (reshaped to a 32×32 tensor for the U-Net flow module), and the improved embedding is decoded back to text. For RL, the input is an action trajectory of fixed length ℓ=10 and the output is a transformed trajectory of the same length.

## Method

PFM trains a neural vector field v_θ via conditional flow matching (CFM) on preference pairs (x, y⁺, y⁻), using straight-line paths u_t(y|z)=y⁺−y⁻ and a Gaussian probability path p_t(y|z)=N(ty⁺+(1−t)y⁻, σ²). The key distinction from RLHF/DPO is that PFM never estimates a reward function; instead it directly learns the transport from the marginal of rejected samples p_0 to the marginal of preferred samples p_1, which Theorem 4.1 shows optimizes a bounded objective (log of expected preference probability minus KL to π_ref) that remains well-behaved even under deterministic preferences. At inference, one samples y~π_ref and integrates the ODE dy=v_θ(t,y|x)dt to obtain f(y)≈y⁺~p_1, requiring no modification to π_ref. An iterative variant repeatedly applies the same learned flow to its own outputs, which Theorem 4.2 proves converges to the uniform distribution over the argmax of expected preference, effectively recovering the optimal policy with β→0.

## Results

On the D4RL MuJoCo benchmark (12 datasets), PFM achieves a normalized average score of 63.10, outperforming DPO fine-tuning (62.49), behavior cloning (59.97), and marginal BC (35.97). On conditional MNIST image generation, PFM reaches a preference score of 0.9841 versus 0.9171 for RLHF and 0.8936 for DPO, with iterative PFM (2 iterations) reaching 0.9996. On IMDB sentiment review generation, PFM attached to the GPT-2 SFT reference model improves the average preference score from −0.3607 to 0.6399 (single pass) and 2.7469 (5 iterations), surpassing the PPO fine-tuned policy (2.0156) and achieving a 100% GPT-4 win rate over the reference model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPO, PFM achieves a higher D4RL average (63.10 vs. 62.49) and substantially outperforms it on MNIST image generation (preference score 0.9841 vs. 0.8936), where DPO also collapses under small β (0.5397). On IMDB text generation, PFM attached to the SFT reference model (2.7469 with 5 iterations) surpasses the PPO fine-tuned policy (2.0156) by 0.73 in average preference score, and achieves a 100% GPT-4 win rate over the reference model versus PPO's 100% (both beat the reference, but iterative PFM on the reference alone beats PPO at 85% win rate). On D4RL expert datasets, PFM (110.58) is slightly below DPO (111.75), which the authors attribute to the source-distribution mismatch when samples are already near-optimal. Overall, PFM wins or ties on 10 of 12 D4RL datasets, dominates on random and medium difficulty levels, and requires training only ~1.2% of the parameters compared to full fine-tuning approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

PFM repurposes the flow matching framework (Lipman et al., 2022) — originally designed for generative modeling — as a preference alignment mechanism, learning a vector field that transports less-preferred samples to more-preferred ones without any reward model. This departs fundamentally from the RLHF/DPO lineage: while DPO eliminates explicit reward learning, it still implicitly optimizes for a Bradley-Terry reward, and IPO addresses overfitting by bounding the objective; PFM sidesteps the reward formulation entirely. It is conceptually closest to Flow-to-Better (Zhang et al., 2023), which also uses a generative process to move from rejected to preferred data, but FTB focuses on data augmentation for behavior cloning rather than direct policy alignment, and both FTB and CPL still rely on the Bradley-Terry assumption that PFM does not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that PFM operates as a lightweight add-on module (~1.2% of parameters) that can align black-box models (e.g., GPT-4 APIs) without any fine-tuning, addressing a critical gap in current alignment tooling. Theoretically, the proof that the preferred-sample marginal p1 solves a bounded optimization problem (Theorem 4.1) provides a principled explanation for why PFM avoids the reward overfitting that plagues RLHF and DPO, and the iterative convergence result (Theorem 4.2) shows the method can recover the optimal policy with vanishing KL regularization. Open questions include scaling to long-form text generation (currently requiring a T5 autoencoder workaround), applicability to truly large-scale LLMs, and whether the approach extends to multi-dimensional or structured preference signals beyond pairwise comparisons.
