## Problem

Lifelong RL agents suffer from loss of plasticity, where persistent gradient steps cause policy weights to drift and the agent progressively loses its ability to adapt to new tasks. Existing remedies such as L2 regularization or periodic weight resetting require environment-dependent hyperparameters (regularization strength, reset frequency) that cannot be reliably chosen before interacting with the environment, violating the one-shot nature of lifelong learning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241618/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TRAC operates as a meta-optimizer that consumes a policy gradient g_t from any policy gradient method (PPO in experiments), the output θ_{t+1}^Base of a base optimizer (ADAM), and a fixed reference point θ_ref (typically the random initialization). It produces the final policy weight θ_{t+1} as a convex combination of θ_{t+1}^Base and θ_ref, governed by a scalar scaling parameter S_{t+1} ∈ [0,1] computed online from the gradient projection ⟨g_t, θ_t − θ_ref⟩. Because TRAC is an optimizer rather than an architecture, it is orthogonal to the policy parameterization and can be dropped into any first-order method without modification.

## Method

TRAC wraps a base optimizer (ADAM) with a 1D tuner that adaptively selects a scaling parameter S_{t+1} to control how far policy weights deviate from a reference point θ_ref. The tuner applies the erfi (imaginary error function) as its decision rule to a discounted running sum of the gradient projection along the direction θ_t − θ_ref, acting as a data-dependent regularizer whose strength is determined entirely by the observed gradient sequence. To eliminate the discount-factor hyperparameter, TRAC aggregates n tuners with different β values (0.9 through 0.999999) via additive aggregation, where each tuner automatically reduces its aggressiveness if another is already performing well. The resulting update θ_{t+1} = θ_ref + (θ_{t+1}^Base − θ_ref)·S_{t+1} is equivalent to an adaptive L2 regularization whose effective strength and learning rate are both data-dependent, requiring no prior knowledge of the distribution shifts or environment-specific tuning.

## Results

TRAC PPO achieves substantial cumulative reward improvements across all lifelong RL benchmarks. On Procgen (Starpilot, Dodgeball, Chaser, Fruitbot), TRAC PPO yields a 3,212.42% normalized improvement over ADAM PPO and 120.88% over CReLU. On Atari (action spaces 6 and 9), improvements are 329.73% over ADAM PPO and 68.71% over CReLU. On Gym Control (CartPole, Acrobot, LunarLander), TRAC PPO shows 204.18% improvement over ADAM PPO and 1,044.24% over CReLU, with CartPole reward jumping from 5.1 (ADAM) to 39.6 (TRAC) on a 10⁵ scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRAC PPO outperforms ADAM PPO across every environment tested, with the largest gains in Procgen (e.g., Starpilot: 12.5 vs. 3.4) and Gym Control (CartPole: 39.6 vs. 5.1). Against CReLU, TRAC PPO wins in 8 of 9 environments; CReLU is competitive only in Atari 9 (17.0 vs. 20.2) and the final segment of Atari 6, consistent with prior findings that CReLU helps in continual Atari settings. Among other RL baselines evaluated in Appendix C, TRAC improves IMPALA by 21.83%, Online EWC by 15.86%, CLEAR by 14.41%, and Modulating Masks by 10.14% on average across Procgen. Against the closest OCO-based optimizer MECHANIC (Cutkosky et al., 2023), TRAC achieves statistically significant but modest gains (e.g., LunarLander: 0.6018 vs. 0.5755, p < 0.001; CartPole: 0.3518 vs. 0.3008, p = 0.0021). TRAC also outperforms LayerNorm + ADAM and Plasticity Injection + ADAM across all three Gym Control environments, with the combination of LayerNorm and TRAC yielding the best overall performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRAC is a direct descendant of the parameter-free OCO literature (Cutkosky & Orabona 2018, Cutkosky 2019, Cutkosky et al. 2023/MECHANIC, Zhang et al. 2024a,b), repurposing the direction-magnitude decomposition, erfi potential, and additive aggregation machinery originally designed for convex online optimization. It departs from the dominant lifelong RL remedies—fixed L2 regularization (Kumar et al. 2023), periodic weight resetting (Dohare et al. 2021), and architectural changes like CReLU (Abbas et al. 2023)—by making the regularization strength data-dependent and hyperparameter-free, positioning itself as an optimizer-level intervention orthogonal to the choice of policy gradient algorithm. The key conceptual leap is applying convex OCO guarantees to a setting that is both nonconvex and nonstationary, a regime where no prior OCO-based RL work (e.g., Jacobsen & Chan 2021, which targets policy evaluation) has operated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: a drop-in optimizer replacement for PPO that eliminates the need to hand-tune regularization strength or reset frequency in lifelong RL, a setting where cross-validation is infeasible due to the one-shot nature of deployment. The paper's most intriguing open question is why OCO-inspired convex tools work so well in a nonconvex, nonstationary regime—the authors speculate about 'hidden convexity' but provide no theoretical explanation, leaving this as a genuine gap. Limitations include suboptimal early-stage performance (mitigated by warmstarting) and the fact that the strongest baselines (ADAM PPO, CReLU) are relatively simple; comparisons to more sophisticated continual RL architectures (IMPALA, Online EWC, CLEAR, Modulating Masks) show TRAC helps but with more modest margins (10–22%). Whether the 'parameter-free' principle generalizes beyond PPO to other policy gradient methods or to truly open-ended (non-piecewise-constant) nonstationarity remains untested.
