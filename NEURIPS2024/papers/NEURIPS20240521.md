## Problem

The classical secretary problem has a tight competitive ratio of 1/e ≈ 0.368, and prior work on learning-augmented secretary problems relied on strong predictions (e.g., the exact largest weight or all weights). This paper asks: what is the weakest single piece of information that still allows breaking the 1/e barrier? It introduces the additive gap w₁ − wₖ as a prediction target, which is weaker than predicting w₁ directly yet strong enough to yield a constant-factor improvement, while not making the problem trivial.

## Input / Output

The algorithm observes n non-negative weights arriving in uniformly random order and must irrevocably accept at most one. Upfront, it receives a single scalar: a predicted additive gap ĉₖ (and optionally the index k and an error bound ε). Notably, the algorithm can be made oblivious to k by fixing τ = 0.2, depending only on the gap value itself. The output is the weight of the selected element, with guarantees stated as competitive ratios (e.g., E[ALG] ≥ 0.4·w₁) or robustness-consistency pairs.

## Method

The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) is a modified threshold policy: wait until time τ, compute the best-so-far weight BSF(τ), then accept the first subsequent element whose weight exceeds max(BSF(τ), c). The key insight is that the gap plays a dual role: when c is large relative to w₁, it acts as a surrogate for w₁ and excludes most elements from consideration; when c is small, it certifies that the top k elements are all within c of w₁, so accepting any of them yields a sufficient contribution. The waiting time τ = 1 − (1/(k+1))^{1/k} balances these two regimes simultaneously. For robustness (Algorithm 2), the gap is dropped from the threshold after time 1−γ, yielding a tunable robustness-consistency trade-off. For bounded prediction error (Algorithm 3), the threshold uses c̃ − ε to ensure it never exceeds w₁, recovering the exact-gap guarantees up to an additive 2ε loss.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 4) shows that knowing an exact additive gap c_k = w_1 - w_k yields a competitive ratio of max(0.4, ½(1/k+1)^(1/k)), which approaches 1/2 as k → n, beating the classical 1/e ≈ 0.368 bound by a constant. Even without knowing the index k (Corollary 1), a fixed waiting time τ = 0.2 guarantees E[ALG] ≥ 0.4·w_1. The robust-consistent variant (Theorem 5) achieves approximately 0.383-consistency and 0.183-robustness (with τ = 0.2, γ = 0.6), while the bounded-error version (Theorem 6) guarantees E[ALG] ≥ 0.4·w_1 − 2ε. In simulations on Pareto-distributed weights, the algorithm achieves a competitive ratio of ~0.8 versus the classical algorithm's ~1/e on the same instances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the classical secretary algorithm (Dynkin, 1963) with a tight competitive ratio of 1/e ≈ 0.368. The paper's Algorithm 1 with an exact additive gap beats this by a constant, achieving 0.4 even when the index k is unknown, and up to 1/2 for large k. In the robust-consistent setting (Algorithm 2), the method still outperforms 1/e (achieving ~0.383) while maintaining O(1)-robustness against arbitrarily large prediction errors. In simulations on Pareto-distributed weights (Section 6.1), where the classical algorithm achieves its near-tight 1/e guarantee, the gap-aware algorithm reaches a competitive ratio of ~0.8, demonstrating that instances hard for the classical problem become significantly easier with additive gap information. For the two-best secretary special case (k=2, gap = 0), the paper inherits the known upper bound of ~0.5736 from Gilbert & Mosteller [1966] and Buchbinder et al. [2014], establishing a hardness result for the exact-gap setting. In the inaccurate-gap simulations (Section 6.2), Algorithm 1 degrades to zero competitive ratio when the gap is overestimated (σ > 1) for large k, while Algorithm 2 maintains a constant ~0.15 ratio, confirming the robustness guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the secretary problem with machine-learned predictions (Antoniadis et al. 2020, Dutting et al. 2021, Fujii & Yoshida 2023) by introducing a strictly weaker prediction model: the additive gap w₁ − wₖ rather than the largest weight w₁ itself. The algorithmic structure inherits the classical wait-threshold-accept template from Dynkin (1963) and Lindley (1961), but augments the threshold with the gap to create a hybrid decision rule. The paper positions the additive gap as interpolating between the k-best secretary problem (small gap) and the prediction-of-w₁ setting (large gap), and contrasts it with the multiplicative gap w₁/wₖ, which it shows is insufficient to beat 1/e.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a single additive gap—without even knowing which index k it corresponds to—suffices to break the 1/e barrier by a constant is a clean answer to the question of what is the weakest useful prediction for the secretary problem. This has practical relevance for settings where the true maximum is hard to predict (privacy constraints, surrogate data) but relative differences are observable. Open questions include whether the 0.4 guarantee is tight, whether the framework extends to arbitrary gaps wᵢ − wⱼ or multi-selection variants, and how the robustness-consistency trade-off behaves under more realistic prediction error distributions.
