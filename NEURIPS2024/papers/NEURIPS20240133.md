## Problem

Generalization to novel tasks remains a core unsolved problem in robot learning, and while latent variable models (LVMs) that learn temporal action abstractions (skills) show promise for enabling cross-task transfer, existing discrete-latent approaches are limited by architectures that fail to faithfully capture shareable representations. Specifically, prior methods either compress an entire action sequence into a single latent code (restricting expressiveness), rely on impractical state-prediction-based MPC for real-world deployment, or use tokenization schemes like BPE that struggle with the dynamic vocabulary of robot action distributions.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 1-timestep observation history consisting of RGB images from front and gripper cameras (encoded by a shallow CNN with spatial softmax), proprioceptive state (encoded by an MLP), and a task embedding (frozen CLIP output for language-labeled LIBERO tasks, or a learned embedding for MetaWorld). It outputs a continuous action chunk of length T=32, of which the first T_a=8 actions are executed in a receding-horizon fashion before replanning. Internally, the continuous action sequence is factorized through a sequence of n=8 discrete skill tokens drawn from a 1024-entry FSQ codebook, sampled autoregressively with top-k=5 and temperature 1.

## Method

QueST is a two-stage latent variable model. In Stage I, a self-supervised quantized autoencoder maps an action sequence to n discrete skill tokens: the encoder uses causal strided convolutions followed by masked self-attention to produce n embeddings (downsampling factor F=4), which are quantized via Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ) into a 1024-entry implicit codebook; the decoder is a transformer that cross-attends fixed sinusoidal positional embeddings to all skill tokens, preserving causality while allowing global attention over the code sequence. In Stage II, a decoder-only GPT-style transformer autoregressively predicts the skill-token sequence conditioned on observation and task embeddings, trained with negative log-likelihood. The key architectural insight is that causal masking throughout the encoder imparts an inductive bias that encourages the model to learn semantically meaningful, task-agnostic motion primitives (e.g., reaching, grasping, placing) that are shared across tasks, while the multi-token representation (effective latent space C^n vs. C in VQ-BeT) provides the flexibility to model variable-length primitives. The decoder is deliberately state-independent (no observation conditioning) to keep the skill space transferable, and FSQ is used over VQ for superior codebook utilization without auxiliary losses.

## Results

On LIBERO-90 multitask imitation learning, QueST achieves 88.6% average success rate, outperforming VQ-BeT by 8% and Diffusion Policy by 13%. On the LIBERO-LONG few-shot setting (5 demonstrations per unseen task), QueST reaches 68.8% success rate, surpassing the next-best baseline by an absolute margin of 14%. On MetaWorld ML45, all methods perform comparably in both multitask and few-shot settings, with QueST showing a slight edge in few-shot transfer. The pipeline runs at 33 Hz, sufficient for real-time vision-based robot control.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QueST outperforms all five baselines on the two most challenging settings. Against VQ-BeT [35], the closest architectural competitor (also a discrete LVM with a transformer prior), QueST gains 8% on LIBERO-90 multitask and 14% on LIBERO-LONG few-shot; the paper attributes this to QueST's causal encoder mapping action sequences to multiple skill tokens (effective latent space C^n vs. VQ-BeT's single-token C) and its cross-attention decoder. Against Diffusion Policy [15], QueST leads by 13% on multitask and ~14% on few-shot, demonstrating the benefit of a discrete bottleneck over a continuous latent of the same dimensionality as actions. Against ACT [73], QueST shows a large gap on LIBERO (88.6% vs. ~54%), with the paper noting ACT is highly sensitive to chunk-size hyperparameters and data-hungry. Against PRISE [74], QueST's advantage is most pronounced in few-shot transfer, where PRISE's BPE-based temporal abstraction struggles with novel action sub-sequences. Against ResNet-T [38], QueST's improved implementation still trails QueST by several points on LIBERO-90, confirming that the learned skill-space provides genuine cross-task knowledge sharing beyond what a direct observation-to-action mapping can achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

QueST extends the two-stage discrete latent skill learning paradigm established by VQ-BeT [35] and PRISE [74], but departs from both in critical architectural ways. Where VQ-BeT compresses an entire action sequence into a single latent vector via an MLP, QueST uses causal strided convolutions and masked self-attention to produce a sequence of n skill tokens, giving the latent space C^n effective capacity versus C. The decoder borrows ACT's [73] cross-attention structure to attend to all skill tokens while preserving causality, and the quantization layer uses FSQ [45] instead of VQ for better codebook utilization. The key conceptual departure is the deliberate imposition of causal inductive bias throughout the encoder-decoder, validated by ablations showing that removing causality from either component degrades both multitask and few-shot performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides evidence that the structure of the latent skill space matters as much as its existence: a flexible, causally-biased sequence of discrete tokens enables stronger cross-task transfer than a single compressed vector, particularly in data-scarce few-shot settings where QueST achieves 14% absolute improvement over the next best baseline. The finding that even a frozen decoder (no finetuning) outperforms all baselines suggests the learned skill vocabulary is genuinely task-agnostic. However, the held-out tasks remain structurally similar to the pretraining distribution, and the architecture only encodes causality as an inductive bias—geometric invariance and dynamic consistency remain unexplored. Scaling stage-1 to larger datasets like Open X-Embodiment with expanded codebooks is a natural next step to test whether these abstractions generalize to truly novel task families.
