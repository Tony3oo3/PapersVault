## Problem

DNA sequences are functionally heterogeneous, composed of multiple connected regions (promoters, exons, introns) where each region follows a distinct statistical distribution. Autoregressive models capture segment-level distributions accurately but fail to model global sequence properties, while diffusion models capture global structure but produce base-pair-level errors. No single generative model handles both aspects well, and naively combining them via MCMC-based compositional sampling is prohibitively slow.

## Input / Output

The method takes two pretrained, frozen models — an autoregressive model (e.g., HyenaDNA/RegLM) and a diffusion model (e.g., DiscDiff, DFM) — as fixed components, plus an optional condition such as a transcription profile or species label. It outputs synthetic DNA sequences of length 256 or 2048 bp. The procedure is entirely post-training: it requires at most L forward passes through the AR model and one through the DM, with a single tunable threshold T_absorb (default 0.85) controlling how aggressively the AR model overrides DM predictions at each position.

## Method

The Absorb & Escape (A&E) framework samples from the composed distribution p_AR ∘ p_DM by initializing with a DM-generated sequence and iteratively refining segments: the Absorb step uses the AR model to resample a segment conditioned on its left context, and the Escape step commits the refined segment back into the sequence, inspired by Gibbs sampling. The practical Fast A&E variant scans the sequence left-to-right, triggering AR refinement whenever the DM's predicted token probability falls below T_absorb, then continues AR generation token-by-token until the AR probability drops below the DM's, thereby correcting only the regions where the DM is unreliable. This avoids the slow MCMC sampling traditionally required for compositional generation, achieving O(T_DM + T_AR) runtime with at most one forward pass per model. A convergence proof (Proposition 1) establishes that A&E converges to the target composed distribution under assumptions of proper model training, segment homogeneity, and random segment selection. Across 15 species, Fast A&E outperforms both single AR and DM baselines on motif distribution fidelity (TATA-box, GC-box, Initiator, CCAAT-box), BLASTN-based diversity, and Enformer-predicted transcription profiles after genome integration.

## Results

On TP-conditioned promoter design, Fast A&E (Language Model + DFM distilled) achieves the lowest MSE of 0.0262, outperforming DFM (0.0269) and DFM distilled (0.0278). On genome integration with Enformer-predicted transcription profiles, A&E achieves SSE of 17.21 (TP53), 0.28 (EGFR), and 1.65 (AKT1), substantially lower than Hyena (36.25, 0.89, 2.88) and DiscDiff (124.03, 2.17, 25.50). Across 15 species and four motif types (TATA-box, GC-box, Initiator, CCAAT-box), A&E achieves the lowest motif distribution MSE and highest Pearson correlation with natural DNA, consistently outperforming both Hyena and DiscDiff.

## Baselines & Comparisons

A&E consistently outperforms both the AR baseline (HyenaDNA [24]) and the DM baseline (DiscDiff) on all three evaluation axes: motif distribution fidelity, sequence diversity (BLASTN), and functional gene expression (Enformer SSE). On TP-conditioned promoter design, A&E (Language Model + DFM distilled) achieves MSE 0.0262, beating standalone DFM [32] (0.0269), DFM distilled (0.0278), DDSM [4] (0.0334), and the Language Model [20] (0.0333). In the genome integration test, A&E reduces SSE by ~53% over Hyena on TP53 (17.21 vs 36.25) and by ~86% over DiscDiff (17.21 vs 124.03), indicating the compositional approach captures functional promoter properties far better than either model alone. Among diffusion baselines, the authors' DiscDiff substantially outperforms DDSM [4] (S-FID 57.4 vs 504.0 on 256 bp EPD) and D3PM [3] (S-FID 57.4 vs 97.4), motivating its use as the DM component in A&E. The toy experiment (Table 1) further confirms the complementary error profiles: HyenaDNA makes 3,586 incorrect-transition tokens but only 812 illegal-start tokens, while DiscDiff makes 0 illegal-start tokens but 110,192 incorrect-transition tokens, validating the theoretical motivation for combining the two.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two dominant families for discrete sequence generation—autoregressive models (HyenaDNA, RegLM) and diffusion models (DDSM, D3PM, DFM, DNADiffusion)—and identifies a complementary weakness in each when applied to heterogeneous genomic sequences. Conceptually, the Absorb & Escape framework draws on Gibbs sampling [12] for iterative segment-wise refinement, but departs sharply from prior compositional generation work (Du et al. [9, 10]) that requires converting models into energy-based models and running MCMC; instead, A&E achieves composition through at most one forward pass per pretrained model. The paper also introduces DiscDiff, a latent diffusion baseline for discrete DNA data inspired by StableDiffusion's VAE+UNet architecture, which serves both as a competitive baseline and the DM component in the A&E pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that most published diffusion models fail to capture fundamental TATA-box motif distributions—performing worse than a simple VAE+UNet baseline—highlights a gap in the field's evaluation practices and motivates the need for local-accuracy metrics beyond global FID-style scores. The multi-species (15 species) evaluation with genome integration via Enformer provides a more comprehensive functional benchmark than prior single-species studies, though the authors rightly note that wet-lab validation remains essential before clinical application. Open questions include whether the heuristic segmentation in Fast A&E generalizes to longer or more complexly structured sequences, whether T_absorb can be learned rather than tuned, and whether the A&E composition strategy extends to other heterogeneous discrete modalities (e.g., protein sequences with structured domains).
