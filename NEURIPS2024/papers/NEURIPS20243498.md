## Problem

When high-quality data is expensive or impractical to collect, practitioners often supplement it with cheaper 'surrogate' data from different sources, but naively mixing real and surrogate data in standard ERM can actually degrade test error as more surrogate data is added. There is no principled framework for determining how to weight the two sources or how much surrogate data to acquire. This paper asks whether surrogate data—potentially from a completely unrelated distribution—can improve test error on the original distribution, and whether a predictive scaling law can guide the weighting and data budget.

## Input / Output

The method takes n i.i.d. samples from a target distribution D (real data), m i.i.d. samples from a potentially very different distribution D^s (surrogate data), and a scalar weight α ∈ [0,1]; the two distributions need only share the same domain with no closeness assumption. The output is a trained parameter vector θ minimizing the weighted empirical risk, and the scaling law (Eq. 4) provides a closed-form prediction of test error as a function of n, m, and α. In practice, the scaling law requires only the individual scaling curves from real-only and surrogate-only training (obtainable from small sample sizes) to predict the optimal α and the number of surrogate samples needed for a target error level.

## Method

The paper proposes a weighted ERM objective where the real-data loss is weighted by (1−α), the surrogate-data loss by α, and a regularizer (e.g., ridge) is added. The key theoretical insight is that the surrogate data acts as a non-trivial regularizer: by Stein's paradox, shrinking the estimator toward the surrogate-data optimum always reduces test error when α is chosen optimally, even if the surrogate distribution is arbitrarily far from the target. The authors derive a scaling law (Eq. 4) that approximates test error as α²R_su^ex(∞) plus a β-powered combination of the individual excess risks, with β determined by the smoothness of the underlying model class. This law is validated theoretically across four settings (Gaussian sequence model, Sobolev non-parametric regression, low-dimensional asymptotics, and high-dimensional ridge regression) and empirically on sentiment analysis (IMDB/Rotten Tomatoes/Goodreads), image classification (CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100), and survival analysis (TCGA PanCancer). The scaling law enables a practical workflow: fit individual scaling curves on real-only and surrogate-only data, then use the formula to select optimal α and determine how much surrogate data to acquire before training.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is the scaling law (Eq. 4), which predicts test error as a function of the surrogate weight α, original sample size n, and surrogate sample size m. Across all six experimental settings (Gaussian mixture classification/regression, IMDB sentiment analysis, CIFAR-10/100 image classification, TCGA PanCancer survival analysis, and high-dimensional ridge regression), the scaling law curves closely track the empirically measured test error for every (n, m, α) combination tested. The scaling exponent β ranges from 0.27 (IMDB logistic regression) to 1.57 (ridge regression with fixed λ), and the surrogate excess risk at infinite samples R_su^ex(∞) ranges from 0.010 (Gaussian mixture NN) to 2.0 (ridge regression with orthogonal θ* and θ*_s). In the Gaussian mean estimation toy model, the optimal weighted ERM achieves error strictly below 1/n (original-data-only) for any m > 0, confirming the Stein's paradox mechanism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares weighted ERM (its proposed method) against three baselines: (1) training only on original data (α→0), (2) training only on surrogate data (α→1), and (3) naive unweighted ERM that pools both datasets equally. Weighted ERM with optimally tuned α systematically outperforms original-data-only training across all six experimental domains, even when the surrogate distribution is far from the original (e.g., CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100 with no label overlap, or orthogonal θ* and θ*_s in ridge regression). The unweighted ERM baseline can actually degrade in performance as more surrogate data is added (Figure 2), whereas weighted ERM monotonically improves with m. For high-dimensional ridge regression, the paper also compares the simple scaling law (Eq. 4) against the exact high-dimensional asymptotic prediction from Theorem 3; the Theorem 3 curves (blue lines in Figure 5) match empirical data more precisely, while the scaling law provides a good but less accurate approximation, with the discrepancy growing at moderate n/d and m/d ratios. The scaling law's prediction of the optimal α is validated by cross-validation on a held-out validation split, confirming that the predicted α* is near-optimal in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the large body of work on generating and using synthetic/surrogate data for training in CV and NLP, which has focused primarily on *how* to generate useful synthetic data rather than *how to optimally mix* it with real data; (2) the scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., Hoffmann et al., etc.), which has guided LLM development but has not addressed heterogeneous data mixtures; and (3) domain adaptation and transfer learning, where prior bounds (Ben-David et al.) and recent scaling analyses (Hashimoto 2021, Ye et al. 2024) consider only vanilla ERM. The paper departs from all three by introducing a weighted ERM framework with a principled scaling law that captures the dependence on the mixing weight α, and by connecting the benefit of surrogate data to the classical Stein's paradox from mathematical statistics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The scaling law (Eq. 4) provides a practical, low-cost tool for practitioners: by fitting two simple curves (original-only and surrogate-only), one can predict the optimal weight α and the amount of surrogate data needed to reach a target error, without expensive grid searches. The demonstration that even completely unrelated surrogate data (orthogonal parameters, disjoint label sets) reduces test error—via a regularization mechanism analogous to Stein's paradox—challenges the common intuition that distribution shift is purely harmful. Key limitations include the assumption that the Bayes error is achievable with infinite original data (model capacity effects are not captured), the assumption of a shared scaling exponent β for both data sources, and the fact that the scaling law is an empirical approximation whose accuracy degrades in high-dimensional regimes (as shown by the ridge regression comparison with Theorem 3). Open questions include extending the framework to multiple surrogate sources, handling cases where β_or and β_su differ substantially, and incorporating model-size effects.
