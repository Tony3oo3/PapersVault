## Problem

Deep learning surrogate models for PDE simulation require large, diverse datasets to reach high accuracy, yet existing benchmarks either cover simple physics at low resolution or are confined to a single domain. This gap hinders holistic evaluation of new architectures and the emerging trend toward multi-physics foundation models. The Well addresses this by providing 15 TB of high-quality simulations across 16 physically diverse scenarios, curated with domain experts to be challenging yet tractable for modern ML.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241429/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each dataset provides temporally coarsened snapshots on uniform grids (resolutions ranging from 128×128 to 256×256 in 2D and up to 256³ in 3D) stored as fp32 numpy arrays in self-documenting HDF5 files with shape (n_traj, n_steps, coord1, coord2, [coord3]). The autoregressive prediction task consumes a 4-step history of physical field snapshots and produces the next time-step snapshot, with 80/10/10 train/validation/test splits applied along initial conditions. Fields can be scalar, vector, or tensor-valued, and the number of time steps per trajectory varies from 50 to over 1000 depending on the dataset.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the dataset collection and a unified PyTorch benchmarking library rather than a novel architecture. Baseline models—FNO, TFNO, U-net, and ConvNeXt-based U-net (CNextU-net)—are trained via supervised learning on the forward problem (predicting the next snapshot from a 4-step history) with a 12-hour single-GPU (NVIDIA H100) compute budget. Evaluation uses Variance Scaled RMSE (VRMSE) averaged over all physical fields, with additional per-field and frequency-binned metrics for granular analysis. A key empirical finding is a clear split: 9 of 17 problems favor spatial-domain U-net architectures while 8 favor spectral FNO-type models, and autoregressive rollouts degrade substantially even over short horizons, indicating that one-model-fits-all approaches remain difficult.

## Results

On the one-step prediction task (VRMSE, lower is better), CNextU-net achieves the best score on 8 of 17 datasets, with standout results on acoustic_scattering (0.0153), active_matter (0.1034), and helmholtz_staircase where FNO excels (0.00046). A clear architectural split emerges: 9/17 datasets favor U-net-type spatial models while 8 favor spectral (FNO/TFNO) models, with no single class dominating. Several datasets prove extremely challenging, with all four models exceeding VRMSE >10 on rayleigh_taylor_instability and rayleigh_benard, indicating that even predicting the mean field is outperformed. In multi-step rollouts (Table 3), performance degrades substantially across the board, with most models exceeding VRMSE >10 by steps 13–30, underscoring the difficulty of stable autoregressive prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four off-the-shelf architectures (FNO, TFNO, U-net, CNextU-net) under a 12-hour single-GPU budget, deliberately avoiding peak-performance tuning. CNextU-net (ConvNeXt-based U-net) wins the most individual datasets (8/17), excelling on spatially complex problems like acoustic_scattering (0.0153 vs. 0.0351 for U-net) and active_matter (0.1034 vs. 0.2489). FNO and TFNO (spectral methods) dominate on problems with smooth, globally coherent dynamics: FNO achieves a near-perfect 0.00046 on helmholtz_staircase and the best 0.0269 on convective_envelope_rsg, while TFNO leads on planetswe (0.0853) and rayleigh_benard (0.6566). The original U-net is competitive but rarely the best, winning only supernova_explosion_64 (0.3063). Critically, no single architecture class dominates: the 9-vs-8 split between spatial and spectral winners suggests that a one-model-fits-all approach is unlikely to succeed across the full diversity of The Well, motivating future work on adaptive or hybrid architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

The Well positions itself against two existing families of PDE simulation benchmarks: (1) diverse but relatively simple collections like PDEBench and Pinnacle, which cover many equation types at low complexity, and (2) complex but narrow datasets like Airfrans, LagrangeBench, and EAGLE, which target a single physical domain at high fidelity. It also responds to the emerging multi-physics foundation model literature (e.g., McCabe et al. 2023, Rahman et al. 2024, Herde et al. 2024), which requires training and evaluation across heterogeneous physical systems. The Well's contribution is not a new method but a curated, unified 15TB collection that simultaneously provides the diversity, complexity, and volume that neither prior family achieves alone, with a shared HDF5 schema and PyTorch interface for drop-in benchmarking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The baseline results reveal that no single architecture family dominates across the 17 experiments: spatial (U-Net/ConvNeXt) and spectral (FNO/TFNO) models each win roughly half, with no clear physical explanation for the split, suggesting that one-model-fits-all surrogate approaches face a fundamental challenge. Long-term autoregressive stability remains a severe open problem, with many datasets producing VRMSE > 10 within 30 steps even for the best model. The dataset's limitations (uniform grids, moderate resolutions, some 2D-only coverage) mean it will need to be supplemented as architectures improve, but as a 15TB, 16-domain benchmark with a unified interface, it is likely to become a standard reference for evaluating next-generation physics surrogates and multi-physics foundation models.
