## Problem

Existing LLM agent benchmarks fail to simultaneously satisfy task diversity, multi-round interaction, partially-observable environments, and fine-grained progress tracking, and they predominantly report only final success rates that obscure meaningful differences between models—especially when most open-weight models achieve near-zero success. This makes it difficult to diagnose specific agent weaknesses or measure incremental improvements in agent capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242364/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a textual task goal, a sequence of textual observations from the environment (including state descriptions, error messages, and valid action lists), and its own interaction history (memory of past actions and observations) within a sliding-window context. The evaluation produces, per task and per model: a binary success rate (task completed within T steps), a continuous progress rate r_t ∈ [0,1] tracked at every interaction step, grounding accuracy (fraction of valid actions), six sub-skill scores (memory, planning, world modeling, self-reflection, grounding, spatial navigation), and a hard/easy performance split based on subgoal count. The progress rate is the key differentiator—it distinguishes models that achieve similar success rates by capturing partial task completion.

## Method

AGENTBOARD unifies 9 tasks across 4 categories (embodied, game, web, tool) into a single POMDP-based interaction framework where a reflex agent uses act-only prompting with a sliding-window context strategy to handle long interactions. The core metric is a fine-grained progress rate defined in two variants: a continuous matching score r_t^match that compares current state to goal state (used for PDDL, WebShop, WebArena), and a discrete subgoal-based score r_t^subgoal that tracks the fraction of manually annotated subgoals achieved (used for AlfWorld, ScienceWorld, BabyAI, Jericho, Tool-Query, Tool-Operation). Subgoals are human-annotated and verified through three rounds of inspection, with a user study confirming Pearson correlation >0.95 between automatic progress rates and human assessments. The accompanying open-source toolkit provides a WandB-based visualization panel for interactive analysis of progress trajectories, grounding accuracy, sub-skill breakdowns, long-range interaction curves, and individual trajectory inspection.

## Results

GPT-4 achieves the highest average progress rate of 70.0% and success rate of 47.9% across all 9 tasks, substantially outperforming the next-best proprietary model Claude2 (48.9%/26.2%) and the strongest open-weight model Llama3-70b (41.9%/20.2%). The progress rate metric proves more discriminative than success rate: for example, Llama2-13b and Mistral-7b have near-identical success rates (2.1% vs. 3.9%) but differ by 5.7 points in progress rate (18.9% vs. 24.6%), revealing meaningful capability gaps invisible in binary success. Among open-weight models, code-trained LLMs (CodeLlama-34b at 30.0%, Lemur-70b at 30.8%) consistently outperform same-size general LLMs (Llama2-70b at 23.8%), and agent-specific training (AgentLM-70b) improves progress rate by 9.5% over its Llama2-70b base.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against prior agent benchmarks (AgentBench, GAIA, MINT, API-Bank, ToolEval, LLM-Eval), AGENTBOARD is the only framework satisfying all five design criteria simultaneously: task diversity, multi-round interaction, partially-observable environments, fine-grained progress metrics, and analytical evaluation (Table 1). Among the 19 evaluated LLM agents, GPT-4 leads every task category with a 70.0% average progress rate, a 21.1-point margin over the next-best proprietary model Claude2 (48.9%) and a 28.1-point margin over the top open-weight model Llama3-70b (41.9%). Proprietary models consistently outperform open-weight ones: the top-4 proprietary models (GPT-4, Claude2, Gemini1.5-Flash, Claude3-Haiku) all exceed 43% progress rate, while no open-weight model surpasses 42%. Within open-weight models, code-trained LLMs (CodeLlama-34b at 30.0%, Lemur-70b at 30.8%) outperform the larger Llama2-70b (23.8%) by 6–7 points in progress rate, and agent-specific instruction tuning (AgentLM-70b) adds 9.5 points over its Llama2-70b base. The progress rate metric reveals distinctions invisible in success rate: Llama2-13b (18.9%) and Mistral-7b (24.6%) have nearly identical success rates (2.1% vs. 3.9%) yet differ substantially in intermediate progress, and CodeLlama-34b's higher progress rate (30.0%) versus lower success rate (7.6%) compared to CodeLlama-13b (25.8%/10.0%) exposes a grounding-specific failure mode.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Act prompting strategy from ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) and the sliding-window context management from LangChain to construct a unified reflex agent framework, but its core contribution is a new benchmark (AGENTBOARD) that unifies nine diverse environments under a single POMDP formulation. It departs from prior agent benchmarks (AgentBench, GAIA, MINT, API-Bank, ToolEval) by being the first to simultaneously satisfy all five design principles: task diversity, multi-round interaction, partially-observable environments, fine-grained progress metrics, and analytical evaluation. The key methodological departure is the introduction of a subgoal-based progress rate metric and an open-source WandB-powered analytical toolkit, neither of which exists in prior agent evaluation frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The progress rate metric addresses a critical practical gap: when most open-weight models achieve near-zero success rates, the standard metric cannot distinguish between models that make meaningful partial progress and those that do not, obscuring incremental improvements that guide model development. The analytical toolkit (grounding accuracy, sub-skill scores, long-range interaction curves, hard/easy breakdown) provides actionable diagnostics for model developers rather than a single opaque score. Key limitations include reliance on human-annotated subgoals (not scalable as task complexity grows) and evaluation restricted to simulated environments, leaving real-world agent benchmarking as an open challenge. The finding that code training data improves general agentic planning (not just tool-calling) suggests a broader implication for pretraining data curation.
