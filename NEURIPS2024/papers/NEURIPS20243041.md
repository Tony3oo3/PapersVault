## Problem

Existing decentralized optimization algorithms for strongly convex, smooth objectives over mesh networks require agents to know global parameters—such as the Lipschitz constant of the aggregate gradient and the spectral gap of the graph—for stepsize selection, information that is typically unavailable in practice. No deterministic, parameter-free decentralized algorithm was known to achieve linear convergence in this setting, leaving a gap between the rich theory of adaptive centralized methods and the rigid hyperparameter requirements of mesh-network optimization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243041/cropped_page9_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each agent i holds only its own L-smooth, μ-strongly convex loss f_i: R^n → R and communicates exclusively with immediate neighbors over a static, connected, undirected graph; no agent has access to global quantities like L, μ, or the eigenvalues of the gossip matrix. The algorithm outputs a consensus minimizer x* (stacked as X* = 1_m(x*)^T) and, at each iteration, an adaptive stepsize α^k determined by a local backtracking line-search followed by a min-consensus across the network. A variant (Algorithm 3) replaces the global min-consensus with a local one over immediate neighbors, trading monotonicity of the error for reduced communication overhead.

## Method

The key insight is a saddle-point reformulation (P′) that uses a non-identity matrix K ≠ I_m (unlike EXTRA or NIDS, which set K = I_m), combined with a Forward-Backward Splitting step under a carefully chosen variable metric C = diag(α^{-1}K, αK^{-1}(c^{-1}I − L²)). This specific metric structure makes the descent direction −D^{k+1/2} independent of the stepsize α, which is what enables a valid backtracking line-search: each agent locally finds the largest α_i^k satisfying a Wolfe-type inequality on its own f_i, and a min-consensus (global or local) yields a common α^k. The gossip matrix is W = (1−c)I + cW̃ with c ∈ (0, 1/2), and the resulting primal-dual updates (Algorithm 1) require only one communication round per iteration for vector variables plus a negligible scalar min-consensus. The convergence analysis identifies two regimes: in well-connected networks the rate separates into min(centralized gradient rate, consensus rate), while in poorly connected networks the rate degrades to O(κ/(1−λ₂(W̃))), with the condition number κ evaluated locally on the convex hull of the trajectory rather than globally.

## Results

The paper evaluates on a synthetic ridge regression problem (A_i ∈ R^{20×300}, σ=0.1, m=20 agents) across three graph topologies: a line graph, a sparse Erdős-Rényi graph (p=0.1), and a well-connected Erdős-Rényi graph (p=0.5). The proposed Algorithm 1 (global min-consensus) and Algorithm 3 (local min-consensus) consistently outperform both EXTRA and NIDS in convergence speed, with the performance gap widening as the condition number of the local losses increases. Figure 2 shows that the number of iterations to reach ‖X^N − X*‖ ≤ 10^{-5} grows more slowly for the proposed methods than for EXTRA or NIDS as the condition number increases, particularly on poorly connected networks. No specific numerical values are tabulated; results are presented as convergence curves and iteration-count plots.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two non-adaptive decentralized baselines: EXTRA [42] and NIDS [26], both of which require manual grid-search tuning of the stepsize to achieve their best practical performance. The proposed Algorithm 1 (global min-consensus) and Algorithm 3 (local min-consensus) outperform both baselines across all three graph topologies (line graph, sparse ER p=0.1, well-connected ER p=0.5), with the advantage becoming more pronounced as the condition number of the agents' losses increases. Notably, the proposed methods achieve this without any knowledge of the Lipschitz constant, strong convexity parameter, or network spectral gap, whereas EXTRA and NIDS require these for their stepsize selection. Algorithm 3, which replaces the global min-consensus with a local (neighborhood) min-consensus to avoid requiring LoRa-style flooding, shows nonmonotonic error dynamics as predicted by Theorem 5, but its practical convergence speed remains largely comparable to Algorithm 1 and still superior to both EXTRA and NIDS. The theoretical analysis further shows that in well-connected networks the convergence rate separates into optimization and consensus components (matching the best non-adaptive methods), while in poorly connected networks the rate degrades to O(κ/(1−λ₂(W)))—the same form as gradient-tracking methods like DGing and SONATA—but with a local (trajectory-restricted) condition number rather than the global one.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Forward-Backward Splitting (FBS) framework from monotone operator theory [4] and the primal-dual reformulations underlying EXTRA [42] and NIDS [26], but departs critically by choosing K≠I_m in the saddle-point reformulation, which the authors show is essential to obtain a stepsize-independent descent direction amenable to decentralized backtracking. It also contrasts with prior attempts at decentralized adaptivity via Barzilai-Borwein stepsizes [12,13,14], which the paper identifies as lacking convergence guarantees without unknown uniform stepsize bounds, and with stochastic adaptive methods [32,7,21] that rely on bounded-gradient assumptions or diminishing stepsizes incompatible with deterministic linear convergence. The variable metric C in (4) is the key architectural departure: it simultaneously satisfies the averaged-operator convergence condition (c5) and the stepsize-independence requirements (c6)-(c7) that make a local line-search well-defined and network-agnostic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first deterministic, parameter-free decentralized algorithm with provable linear convergence for strongly convex smooth objectives over mesh networks, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify as open. The separation property in well-connected networks (rate governed by the slower of optimization or consensus) and the use of local condition numbers on the iterates' convex hull offer more favorable theoretical guarantees than existing nonadaptive methods. However, the experimental validation is limited to synthetic ridge regression on 20 agents with three graph topologies, leaving open questions about scalability to larger networks, nonconvex or composite objectives, and time-varying topologies. The local min-consensus variant (Algorithm 3) is practically important for networks without broadcast capability, though its nonmonotonic error dynamics may complicate termination criteria in practice.
