## Problem

Publicly available fairness-aware tabular datasets are scarce, small, or far from real-world scenarios, and in online advertising the protected attributes (e.g., gender) are typically unavailable due to privacy and legal constraints. This paper addresses both gaps by releasing a large-scale, privacy-compliant job-advertising dataset with a gender proxy and by identifying how selection bias at the campaign level distorts fairness evaluations and utility metrics in the advertising pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240334/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 59 anonymized tabular features per impression (20 categorical user/product features, 39 numerical user features, display rank, and a binary gender proxy derived from product-interaction patterns) and outputs a click probability. Evaluation produces demographic parity on senior job ads as the fairness metric and two utility metrics: a standard click-rank utility U computed on randomized displays, and a selection-bias-corrected product-rank utility Û that reweights each prediction by the ratio of population-level to campaign-level protected-attribute frequencies. The positive class (click) is extremely rare at 0.7%, making the task a strongly imbalanced binary classification.

## Method

The paper's core technical contributions are (1) a selection-bias-corrected utility metric Û that explicitly accounts for the fact that the recommendation engine predicts P(Y=1|A=a, C=c) rather than P(Y=1|A=a), by weighting each impression with P(A=a_X)/P(A=a_X|C=c); and (2) an experimental framework comparing three training regimes—unfair (protected attribute as feature), unaware (fairness-through-unawareness), and fair (unaware plus a Bechavod-Ligett fairness penalty λ·Penalty(Ŷ,Y,A) added to the log-loss). Baselines use XGBoost (with target-encoded categoricals and scale_pos_weight for imbalance) and PyTorch logistic regression (with learned categorical embeddings concatenated with numerical features, WeightedRandomSampler for oversampling). The fair regime sweeps λ from 0.1 to 5.0 to trace the fairness-utility trade-off, and a variant applies the penalty to only a subset of the training data to reduce the accuracy cost. All experiments are repeated 10 times with different seeds and hyperparameters tuned via Optuna Bayesian optimization.

## Results

On the FairJob dataset (1,072,226 rows, 0.7% positive click rate), XGBoost in the unaware regime achieves AUC 0.758 and NLLH 0.0549, while the unfair variant (including the protected attribute) reaches AUC 0.762 and NLLH 0.0574. The demographic parity (DP) gap between the two is small (0.00278 vs. 0.00323), but the fair utility metric U-tilde favors the unaware model (0.01276 vs. 0.01236), whereas the raw click-rank utility U slightly favors the unfair model (0.01037 vs. 0.01017). The Dummy classifier is perfectly fair (DP = 0) but has no predictive power (AUC = 0.500).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three baselines: a Dummy classifier (unaware), XGBoost in the unaware regime (fairness through unawareness), and XGBoost in the unfair regime (protected attribute included as a feature). The unfair XGBoost achieves a marginally higher AUC (0.762 vs. 0.758) and raw utility U (0.01037 vs. 0.01017), but at the cost of slightly worse demographic parity (DP 0.00323 vs. 0.00278) and notably worse fair utility U-tilde (0.01236 vs. 0.01276), confirming that the selection bias correction in U-tilde penalizes the unfair model. The Dummy classifier, while perfectly fair (DP = 0), is useless for prediction (AUC = 0.500, NLLH = 0.692). The authors further show via logistic regression with a fairness penalty (Figure 6) that at fairness_multiplier = 0.5–1.0, DP improves over the unaware baseline while U-tilde slightly exceeds the unfair model, suggesting a mild fairness-utility trade-off rather than a severe one.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the advertising fairness literature (Lambrecht & Tucker 2019, Speicher et al. 2018, Ali et al. 2019) that identified discriminatory patterns in ad delivery but relied on user-side observations without access to algorithmic features; (2) the fairness-aware tabular learning literature that has been hampered by a scarcity of large, realistic, privacy-compliant datasets (Le Quy et al. 2022, Hort et al. 2023); and (3) the in-processing fairness penalty approach of Bechavod and Ligett (2017), which the paper adopts as its primary bias-mitigation technique. The key departure from prior advertising fairness work is the provision of internal algorithmic features (user context, product attributes, display position) alongside a gender proxy, enabling reproducible study of bias at the model level rather than only at the user-experience level. The paper also extends the standard click-prediction utility metric by incorporating a selection-bias correction term (Eq. 4) that accounts for the non-uniform distribution of protected attributes within a campaign versus the broader population.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a concrete and acknowledged gap: prior to this work, no public, large-scale, privacy-compliant fairness dataset existed for the advertising domain, forcing researchers to rely on small or synthetic benchmarks or non-reproducible private-data studies. The gender proxy, while explicitly acknowledged as imperfect and not a true demographic label, provides a practical path for bias evaluation under realistic privacy constraints, and the selection-bias-corrected utility metric offers a principled way to evaluate recommendation systems when campaign-level data is biased. Open questions remain: how closely the proxy tracks actual gender, whether the fairness-utility trade-offs observed with logistic regression and XGBoost generalize to deep tabular models, and how to validate fairness improvements when ground-truth demographics are unavailable in production. The strong class imbalance (0.7% click rate) and the 1M+ row scale also make this a useful stress test for robust and fair learning methods.
