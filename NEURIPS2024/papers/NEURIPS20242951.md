## Problem

Existing video LLMs produce verbose, superficial captions that describe object appearances rather than narrative context, and they cannot incorporate prior scene information (character names, plot developments) into their descriptions. This is a critical gap for automated audio descriptions, where a listener needs to follow a movie's story through character-anchored, action-focused narration rather than a stream of generic visual observations.

## Input / Output

The model consumes up to 32 video frames per scene (sampled uniformly), which are encoded by a frozen CLIP ViT-H/14 into fixed-dimensional embeddings, along with text tokens from up to 5 preceding scene annotations as narrative context. Optionally, entity/character names can be injected into the prompt. The output is a single concise natural-language caption (audio description) that references named characters and plot-relevant actions, rather than a list of visual objects.

## Method

CALVIN uses a frozen CLIP ViT-H/14 encoder feeding into a Q-Former (4 layers, 32 learned query embeddings initialized from BERT-base, with position embeddings for temporal dynamics) and a linear projection into a LLaMA-2 7B LLM. Training is two-stage: Stage 1 aligns only the Q-Former and linear layer on ~100M image-caption pairs with both CLIP and LLM frozen; Stage 2 fine-tunes the Q-Former and LLM (via LoRA rank 32 on attention QKVO matrices) on a mixed dataset of CC-3M, LLaVA 1.5 instruction data, WebVid-3M video captions, and the MAD audio-description dataset, all reformatted into a unified <image/video><context><question><caption> instruction template. The key design choice is integrating MAD directly into Stage 2 rather than as a separate fine-tuning stage, and mixing image VQA data with video captioning to transfer visual understanding. At test time, the model supports entity prompting (injecting character names) and few-shot fine-tuning on 20–50 scenes from a new movie using chain-of-thought reformatted captions with regularization data to prevent overfitting.

## Results

On the MAD-named-eval split, CALVIN with 3-scene context achieves BertScore 39.08, CIDEr 25.47, ROUGE-L 16.30, and SPICE 7.33, outperforming the previous SOTA (AutoAD-I) by approximately 64% on BertScore and 16% on CIDEr. Without any context, CALVIN still achieves BertScore 27.25 and CIDEr 14.74, surpassing all no-context baselines by a wide margin. On the TV-Captioning (TVC) dataset in zero-shot evaluation, CALVIN achieves CIDEr 38.9, roughly 3× better than the next-best model (VideoLLaVA at 12.4), demonstrating strong generalization to unseen movie/TV content.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On MAD-named-eval, CALVIN (3S context) outperforms AutoAD-I (the prior SOTA) on all four metrics: BertScore 39.08 vs. 23.8, CIDEr 25.47 vs. 21.9, ROUGE-L 16.30 vs. 13.9, and SPICE 7.33 vs. 4.8. It also surpasses AutoAD-II on CIDEr (25.47 vs. 19.5) and ROUGE-L (16.30 vs. 13.4). Against the no-context baselines ClipCap and CapDec, CALVIN without context already achieves roughly 2× higher BertScore and CIDEr. On the TV-Captioning zero-shot benchmark, CALVIN dominates all three off-the-shelf video LLMs: it beats VideoLLaVA (the strongest baseline) by 3.72 on BertScore and 26.5 on CIDEr, and outperforms MovieChat and VideoLlama by even larger margins on CIDEr (38.9 vs. 8.43 and 3.34 respectively). The CIDEr gap on TVC is particularly striking, suggesting CALVIN's instruction-tuned captions align much more closely with the reference captions' n-gram distributions than generic video LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

CALVIN builds directly on the BLIP-2 Q-Former architecture for projecting frozen CLIP ViT-H/14 embeddings into an LLM's latent space, and adopts LLaVA 1.5's two-stage training recipe (first align the projection module on image-caption data, then jointly fine-tune projection and LLM with instruction-formatted data). It departs from the AutoAD I/II line of work, which used CLIP features with smaller language models, by scaling to a full 7B Llama-2 backbone and integrating the MAD audio-description dataset into the main instruction-tuning stage rather than treating it as a separate fine-tuning step. Relative to general-purpose video LLMs (VideoLlama, MovieChat, VideoLLaVA), CALVIN's key distinction is the explicit use of multi-scene text context as input to the LLM, positioning it as a specialized contextual captioner rather than a generic video understanding model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work advances automated audio description for visually impaired audiences by showing that a full video-LLM with scene-level context can produce crisp, narrative captions that name characters and track plot, substantially outperforming prior SOTA on MAD-eval (~68% BertScore gain) and generalizing zero-shot to TV-Captioning. The practical test-time adaptation strategies—entity prompting and 20-scene few-shot fine-tuning in under 6 minutes—offer a low-cost path to deploying the model on new movies without retraining. Key limitations include reliance on the relatively small MAD dataset, a 32-frame cap per scene, no audio processing, and the absence of raw video in MAD (only CLS embeddings), which constrains evaluation. Open questions include whether the approach scales to longer-form video, how to incorporate audio/dialogue, and whether the image-VQA-data-helps-video finding generalizes beyond this architecture.
