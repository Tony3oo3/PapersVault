## Problem

Existing uncertainty quantification methods for graphical model inference face a fundamental trade-off: sampling-based approaches (e.g., Monte Carlo) are unbiased but prohibitively slow on large graphs with no convergence guarantee, while fast closed-form methods (e.g., NETCONF, SocNL) underestimate posterior uncertainty by assuming every neighbor necessarily reduces a node's uncertainty. Additionally, no prior work establishes a theoretical link between the computed uncertainty and the expected model prediction error, leaving decision-makers without a principled interpretation of UQ results.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph with per-edge compatibility matrices (which may differ across edges, unlike prior methods requiring a uniform matrix) and prior uncertainty vectors expressed as interval widths across k classes for each node. It produces posterior uncertainty vectors, also as interval widths per class per node, where wider intervals indicate greater uncertainty. The interval-width representation is non-probabilistic and avoids the Dirichlet-distribution assumptions of competing methods, enabling additive rather than multiplicative aggregation of neighbor uncertainty.

## Method

LinUProp linearizes Belief Propagation via Centered BP and applies interval arithmetic to derive upper and lower bounds on messages and beliefs, then computes the interval width (uncertainty) by subtracting bounds. The key insight is that this linearization restricts operations to interval addition, sidestepping the multiplicative interval rules that cause underestimation in prior methods. The resulting stable-state equation (Theorem 3.1) is a linear system vec(B) = vec(E) + T·vec(B) where T encodes graph dependencies through absolute-valued centralized compatibility matrices, yielding a closed-form solution and a guaranteed-convergent iterative update when the spectral radius of T is below 1. A bias-variance decomposition of the linearized posterior shows that LinUProp's interval width is a generalized variance component of the expected prediction error, and a Neumann-series expansion provides per-node interpretability of uncertainty contributions.

## Results

In uncertainty-guided active learning with a fixed labeling budget of 20b, LinUProp-based strategies (BB, LC+BB) achieve the highest mean test accuracy in 28 of 32 experimental configurations across four datasets, two inference methods (BP and NETCONF), and four labeling accuracies (70–100%). The most dramatic gains appear with NETCONF inference: on PolBlogs at 100% labeling accuracy, LC+BB reaches 88.24% test accuracy versus 55.00% for NETCONF's native Certainty Score (CS/LC+CS), a 33-point gap. On PubMed under the same setting, BB/LC+BB achieve 67.82% versus 44.45% for CS/LC+CS. LinUProp also demonstrates fast convergence (within 10 iterations on all datasets) and linear scalability in the number of edges, with a PCC of 0.9084 against Monte Carlo ground-truth uncertainty on a 4×4 grid.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is NETCONF (Eswaran et al., SDM 2017), whose native uncertainty quantification uses a Dirichlet-multinomial model where the Certainty Score (CS) is the sum of posterior Dirichlet parameters. NETCONF's core assumption—that every neighbor necessarily reduces a node's uncertainty—leads to systematic underestimation, particularly for high-degree nodes surrounded by noisy or uninformative neighbors. In active learning, this causes CS/LC+CS to preferentially label low-degree nodes, leaving high-degree nodes perpetually uncertain and stalling accuracy gains. LinUProp (BB/LC+BB) consistently outperforms CS/LC+CS by 7–33 percentage points across all four datasets under NETCONF inference, with the largest gaps on PolBlogs (33 pts) and PubMed (23 pts) where the degree distribution is most skewed. Under BP inference (Table 2), LinUProp also outperforms Random, LC, and Entropy baselines in most configurations, though the margin is smaller since BP does not have a native UQ method to directly compare against. The paper also notes that LinUProp's runtime is comparable to NETCONF (e.g., 0.0084s vs 0.0126s on PolBlogs), so the accuracy gains come without a computational penalty.

## Relation to Prior Work

LinUProp builds on two foundational ideas: the linearized (Centered) Belief Propagation of Gatterbauer et al. [11], which provides the linear message-passing structure, and interval arithmetic [6], which supplies the non-probabilistic uncertainty representation. It departs from the Dirichlet-based UQ methods (NETCONF [7], SocNL [35]) that model uncertainty multiplicatively and assume every neighbor necessarily reduces a node's uncertainty, leading to systematic underestimation. Unlike prior bound-propagation work [20, 22] that quantifies error bounds on marginal probabilities, LinUProp quantifies posterior uncertainty as interval widths and, for the first time, connects the computed uncertainty to the expected prediction error via a bias-variance decomposition, identifying it as a generalized variance component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LinUProp offers a practical, linearly scalable UQ method for graphical models with guaranteed convergence and a clear theoretical interpretation, filling a gap between slow MC sampling and underestimating Dirichlet-based approaches. The bias-variance connection gives decision-makers a principled understanding of what the quantified uncertainty represents, which prior UQ methods for graphical models lacked. Key limitations include the convergence requirement (spectral radius < 1), which can fail on graphs with strong global homophily/heterophily, and the restriction to MRFs with BP-style inference. Open questions include whether the interpretability of uncertainty contributions translates to better human-in-the-loop decisions and whether the approach extends to other graphical model families or learned potentials.
