## Problem

When ML models are periodically retrained using model-annotated samples (a common practice to avoid expensive human labeling), the long-term dynamics remain uncharacterized in the presence of strategic human agents who adapt their features to game the classifier. Prior strategic classification work assumed one-time deployment with true labels available, while prior retraining-with-model-annotations work ignored strategic feedback. This paper asks: how do acceptance rates, true qualification rates, classifier bias, and inter-group fairness evolve over many retraining rounds when agents strategically respond to each successive model?

## Input / Output

At each round t, the decision-maker accumulates a growing training set S_t = S_{t-1} ∪ S_{m,t-1} ∪ S_{o,t-1}, where S_{m,t-1} contains N post-best-response features labeled by the previous model f_{t-1} and S_{o,t-1} contains K new human-annotated samples drawn from a fixed prior distribution (with possible systematic bias). The practical constraint N ≫ K (e.g., N=2000, K=100) reflects the high cost of human annotation. The output is a sequence of linear classifiers f_t(x) = 1(h_t(x) ≥ θ) making binary accept/reject decisions, along with tracked quantities: acceptance rate a_t, true qualification rate q_t, and classifier bias Δ_t = |a_t − q_t|, each computed in expectation over the random training set.

## Method

The paper formalizes a sequential game where agents best-respond to f_{t-1} via x_t = argmax_z {f_{t-1}(z) − c(x,z)} with quadratic cost, then the decision-maker retrains f_t via ERM on the accumulated dataset. The core theoretical contribution characterizes the long-run dynamics: acceptance rate a_t increases monotonically (Thm 3.3) and converges to 1 when K/N is small (Prop 3.4); qualification rate q_t decreases under convexity conditions on the feature CDF and labeling function (Thm 3.5); and classifier bias Δ_t evolves depending on the sign of the decision-maker's systematic bias μ(D^o, P) (Thm 3.6). To stabilize the dynamics, the paper proposes a refined retraining process that replaces hard model labels f_{t-1}(x) with probabilistic labels drawn from the scoring function h_{t-1}(x), which provably keeps the learned conditional distribution D_{Y|X}^t equal to the human-annotation distribution D_{Y|X}^o (Prop D.2). For fairness, the paper shows that enforcing demographic parity at every round can lock in disadvantage for group j (Thm 4.2), and instead proposes disparate retraining strategies across groups combined with early stopping to minimize unfairness mid-process (Thm 4.1).

## Results

The paper's main contributions are theoretical: Theorem 3.3 proves the acceptance rate a_t increases monotonically under retraining, Proposition 3.4 shows a_t → 1 when the ratio of human-annotated to model-annotated samples K/N is below a threshold λ, and Theorem 3.5 shows the qualification rate q_t decreases monotonically under convexity conditions on the feature and labeling distributions. Experimentally, with r = K/N = 0.05 (N=2000 model-annotated, K=100 human-annotated samples) over T=15 rounds, a_t rises from ~0.5 toward 1 while q_t declines from its initial value q_0=0.5 on Gaussian and German Credit data, and classifier bias Δ_t diverges accordingly. The refined retraining process (using a probabilistic sampler h_{t-1}(x) instead of the hard classifier f_{t-1}) stabilizes a_t and q_t, keeping Δ_t bounded by the systematic bias μ(D^o, P).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it contrasts internal design choices. (1) Original retraining (model-annotated samples via hard classifier f_{t-1}) vs. refined retraining (probabilistic sampler h_{t-1}(x)): the original process drives a_t to 1 and amplifies Δ_t, while the refined process stabilizes both a_t and q_t, with Δ_t bounded by the systematic bias (Prop. D.2, Fig. 18). (2) Strategic vs. non-strategic agents: removing strategic behavior (Fig. 21) yields much more stable dynamics, confirming that agent best responses are the primary driver of the adverse trends. (3) Different r = K/N ratios (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3): smaller r (more model-annotated data) accelerates the divergence of a_t from q_t, consistent with Prop. 3.4. (4) Fairness intervention (enforcing demographic parity at each round) vs. no intervention: Thm. 4.2 shows that without intervention the disadvantaged group can flip to advantaged, while with intervention (under sufficiently low noise σ_t) the disadvantaged group remains disadvantaged indefinitely, suggesting fairness constraints may not benefit the disadvantaged group in the long run. (5) Disparate retraining strategies (refined for group i, original for group j) produce a U-shaped unfairness curve with a minimum around t=5, enabling an early-stopping mechanism to achieve near-perfect demographic parity (Fig. 4, Fig. 19).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends strategic classification from one-shot deployment to a sequential retraining framework where the model is periodically updated using both human-annotated and model-annotated samples. It builds on the data feedback loop literature (Taori & Hashimoto, ICML 2023) on bias amplification during retraining, but is the first to incorporate strategic agent best responses into that retraining dynamic. For the fairness analysis, it adopts the noisy best response model from Jagadeesan et al. (ICML 2021) and the linear classifier framework from Levanon & Rosenfeld (ICML 2022), while departing from prior fairness-in-strategic-classification works (Zhang et al. 2022, Liu et al. 2020) that never considered iterative retraining with model-annotated data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental tension: model-annotated retraining under strategic feedback causes acceptance rates to monotonically increase while true qualification rates may decrease, and—counterintuitively—enforcing demographic parity at every retraining round may lock in disadvantage for the affected group rather than remedying it. The proposed refined retraining process (probabilistic sampler) and early-stopping mechanism offer practical mitigations, but the theoretical results rely on realizability (Assumption 3.1), linear classifiers, and specific distributional convexity conditions that may not hold in high-stakes deployments. Open questions include whether these dynamics persist under non-linear models, how to address the systematic bias that the refined process preserves, and how to operationalize the early-stopping criterion in production systems where the 'optimal' stopping point is unknown a priori.
