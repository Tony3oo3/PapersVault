## Problem

Traditional policy learning requires training or fine-tuning a network to map states to actions, but this paper asks whether one can directly generate optimal policy network parameters from a single (even suboptimal) behavioral demonstration, without any downstream gradient-based adaptation. The gap is the absence of a mechanism to learn the underlying parameter-space distribution that links agent behaviors to deployable policies, enabling one-shot policy synthesis across tasks and embodiments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240609/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes short agent trajectories (state-action sequences of ~60 steps) from partially-trained RL replay buffers as prompts, which are encoded into 256-dimensional behavior embeddings via contrastive learning. It outputs full policy network parameters (e.g., 22,664 for MetaWorld, 50,956 for quadrupedal locomotion) as deployable control policies requiring zero fine-tuning. Notably, input trajectories can be highly suboptimal (sampled from the first 0.5M timesteps of SAC training) and even contain added Gaussian noise, yet the generator still produces high-performing policies.

## Method

The architecture has three components trained sequentially: (1) a per-layer autoencoder with noise augmentation that compresses flattened policy parameters into fixed-dimension latent representations (e.g., [2, 1024]) and reconstructs them; (2) a contrastive embedding module that learns mutual information between the first n state-action pairs and post-success states, producing 128-dim h and v vectors concatenated as 256-dim conditions; (3) a conditional latent diffusion model with a 1D convolutional UNet backbone that denoises random noise into latent parameter representations conditioned on the behavior embedding, using DDIM sampling with 10 inference steps. The key insight is operating in a shared latent parameter space rather than the full parameter space, enabling cross-task and cross-embodiment generalization: behaviors from different tasks share underlying skills that manifest as correlated parameter patterns, so the diffusion model can recombine these patterns for unseen tasks without any gradient updates at test time.

## Results

On MetaWorld seen tasks, Make-An-Agent achieves Top-5 success rates of 0.82–1.0 across 8 tasks (e.g., 1.0 on window open, door open, plate slide, handle press; 0.82 on dial turn) with 100 test trajectories, and maintains qualified rates (100% success) of 23–50%. On 8 unseen tasks, Top-5 success rates range from 0.45 (handle press side) to 1.0 (drawer close, faucet open, door lock), with qualified rates of 4–55%. On Robosuite, the method transfers policies trained on Panda to Sawyer with strong performance. Under 0.1 standard deviation Gaussian noise on input trajectories, Make-An-Agent retains high success rates while all baselines degrade significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Make-An-Agent outperforms all four baselines (Multi-task BC, Multi-task RL/CARE, Meta-RL with hypernetworks, and Meta-IL with Decision Transformer) on both seen and unseen MetaWorld tasks, with the gap widening on unseen tasks where baselines struggle to adapt from single-environment trajectories. On seen tasks, the advantage is attributed to the diversity of generated parameters enabling better adaptation to environmental randomness, whereas baselines adapted from singular trajectories show limited generalization. On unseen tasks, Make-An-Agent requires no fine-tuning and directly uses test trajectories as generation conditions, while all baselines require gradient-based adaptation and still underperform. Under noisy trajectory inputs (σ=0.1 Gaussian noise), Make-An-Agent remains resilient because its contrastive behavior embeddings capture key dynamics without directly learning state-action mappings, while all four baselines suffer significant performance degradation. The cross-embodiment transfer on Robosuite (Panda→Sawyer) further demonstrates an advantage over baselines that are typically tied to a single robot morphology.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the latent diffusion paradigm (LDM) from image generation to the space of neural network policy parameters, using an autoencoder to compress policy weights into a compact latent representation where a 1D convolutional U-Net performs conditional denoising. It departs from hypernetwork-based meta-learning (Beck et al., 2023; Ha et al., 2016), which treats generated weights as an initialization requiring downstream gradient-based adaptation, by instead using contrastive behavior embeddings as direct prompts that yield deployable policies without any fine-tuning. The contrastive behavior embedding draws on contrastive predictive coding (van den Oord et al., 2018) but repurposes it to capture mutual information between early trajectory segments and post-success states rather than predicting future observations. Unlike prior diffusion-for-parameters work (G.pt, p-diff) that conditions on learning metrics or generates only normalization layers, this approach conditions on agent behavior trajectories and generates full policy networks across varying architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a new paradigm where suboptimal, noisy trajectories from partially-trained agents can directly prompt the generation of policies that outperform their source, eliminating the need for downstream adaptation or reward signals. The demonstrated cross-embodiment transfer (Panda-trained policies deployed on Sawyer) and real-world quadruped locomotion deployment suggest practical relevance for reducing the data and compute burden of robot policy learning. Key limitations include reliance on a parameter autoencoder that constrains the diversity of generable policies, testing only on small MLP policies (22K–51K parameters), and the requirement of tens of thousands of pre-collected policy checkpoints per domain. Open questions include whether this scales to larger architectures (CNNs, transformers), whether the behavior embedding can be learned without success signals in fully reward-free settings, and how the method behaves when the training and test task distributions are truly disjoint rather than sharing underlying skills.
