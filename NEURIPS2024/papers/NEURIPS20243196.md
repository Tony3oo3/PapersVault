## Problem

Personalized federated learning aims to give each client a model tailored to their local data while benefiting from collaboration, but prior work largely assumes all clients follow the protocol honestly. When adversarial (Byzantine) clients are present, they inject corrupted gradients that induce an optimization error growing with data heterogeneity, potentially making collaboration counterproductive. This paper asks: under what conditions does full collaboration perform strictly worse than fine-tuned personalization or even purely local learning, and how should the collaboration level be scaled according to heterogeneity and the adversarial fraction?

## Input / Output

Each correct client i holds m local data points sampled i.i.d. from its own distribution D_i and receives gradient vectors from all other clients at each iteration, some of which are arbitrarily corrupted by up to f < n/2 Byzantine adversaries. The method produces a personalized parameter vector θ_i per correct client, obtained by running a robust interpolated gradient descent where the collaboration parameter λ ∈ [0,1] interpolates between the local empirical loss and the (robustly estimated) average loss over correct clients. The output can be a class label (binary or multi-class) or a mean estimate, depending on the task, and the key tunable is λ, which the theory prescribes as a function of heterogeneity, adversarial fraction, and hypothesis class complexity.

## Method

The paper analyzes an interpolated personalized objective L_i^λ(θ) = (1−λ)L_i(θ) + λL_C(θ) solved via a personalized variant of robust distributed gradient descent (Algorithm 1), where each client broadcasts its parameters, collects gradients from all peers, robustly aggregates them using an (f,κ)-robust rule (NNM pre-aggregation followed by trimmed mean), and updates with a projected step. The core theoretical contribution is a combined bound (Theorem 1) decomposing the local risk gap into an optimization error term O(λ²κG²/μ²) driven by adversarial corruption, a domain-adaptation discrepancy term 2λΦ(D_i, D_C) capturing heterogeneity, and a generalization term O(√(Pdim(H)/m)). Minimizing this bound yields a closed-form approximation λ* ≈ Π_[0,1]((√(Pdim(H)/m) − Φ(D_i, D_C)) / ((f/n)G²)), showing that the optimal collaboration level is multiplicatively suppressed by the adversarial fraction and gradient dissimilarity, and vanishes entirely when heterogeneity exceeds the local sample complexity. Empirically, the authors validate these predictions on mean estimation with synthetic Gaussians and on binary/multi-class classification (Phishing, MNIST with a CNN), confirming that fine-tuning λ to an intermediate value substantially outperforms both full robust FL and local learning in moderate-adversary, moderate-heterogeneity regimes.

## Results

In the mean estimation warm-up (n=600, f=100, m=20, σ=15), the theoretical optimal collaboration parameter λ* closely tracks the empirical minimizer across all tested heterogeneity levels (σ_h ∈ {0,1,2,3,4}), Byzantine fractions (f ∈ {0,50,100,150}), and task complexities (σ ∈ {1,7,13,19,25,31}). In the binary classification experiments (n=20, Phishing and MNIST), full collaboration (λ=1) becomes suboptimal when the adversarial fraction exceeds 6 clients, heterogeneity is high (small Dirichlet α), or the local dataset is sufficiently large (m≥48). Most strikingly, when f=9 out of n=20 clients are adversarial, robust federated learning completely fails, achieving local test accuracy below 50%, while fine-tuned personalization (λ∈(0,1)) substantially outperforms both full collaboration and local learning. In the mean estimation setting with f=150, choosing the right collaboration level reduces the estimation error by 50% compared to the purely local estimator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it compares three collaboration regimes: local learning (λ=0), full robust federated learning (λ=1), and fine-tuned personalization (λ∈(0,1)). The key finding is that full collaboration (λ=1) is strictly worse than fine-tuned personalization in most realistic heterogeneous settings with adversarial clients, and can even be worse than local learning when the adversarial fraction is critical (f=9 out of n=20). On the Phishing dataset with logistic regression (n=20, α=3), fine-tuned personalization achieves the best local test accuracy for moderate adversarial fractions (f=3, f=6) and large local sample sizes (m=48, m=128). On MNIST with a CNN (n=20), the same pattern holds: intermediate λ values dominate both extremes. The theoretical bound (Theorem 1) predicts that λ*→0 when the distributional discrepancy Φ(D_i, D_C) exceeds √(Pdim(H)/m), confirming that local learning is optimal under high heterogeneity, and that λ* is further scaled down by the multiplicative factor (f/n)·G² capturing the adversarial impact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: Byzantine-robust federated learning (building on the NNM pre-aggregation and trimmed-mean defenses of Allouah et al., 2023, and the tight error bounds of Karimireddy et al., 2022) and personalized FL via loss interpolation (Mansour et al., 2020). Its key conceptual departure is importing the domain-adaptation threshold result of Ben-David et al. (2010) into the Byzantine setting, showing that the collaboration benefit is not merely reduced by heterogeneity but multiplicatively rescaled by the adversarial fraction and gradient dissimilarity. Prior personalized-FL-under-Byzantine work (Li et al., 2021; Kundu et al., 2022; Mishchenko et al., 2023) either restricts to linear models, assumes a common minimizer across clients, or omits the generalization analysis that this paper treats as central to the trade-off.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that full collaboration can be strictly worse than local learning when the adversarial fraction is non-negligible and data is heterogeneous—challenges the default assumption in robust FL that more collaboration is always better. The closed-form approximation for λ* gives practitioners a concrete recipe for tuning collaboration, though it relies on strong convexity, bounded loss, and a specific interpolated personalization scheme that may not transfer directly to regularization-based or clustering-based personalization. Open questions include whether the insights extend to communication-efficient algorithms (Algorithm 1 is explicitly not), to non-convex deep-learning objectives beyond the binary-classification analysis, and to other personalization frameworks such as layer specialization or meta-learning.
