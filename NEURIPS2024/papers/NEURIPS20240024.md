## Problem

Baseline sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained with an L1 sparsity penalty suffer from shrinkage: the penalty simultaneously penalizes feature detection and magnitude estimation, causing systematic underestimation of feature activations and degrading reconstruction fidelity. The L1 term conflates two distinct roles of the encoder—deciding which features are active and estimating how strongly they fire—so the sparsity constraint introduces unwanted bias into magnitude estimation with no compensatory sparsity benefit.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240024/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes activation vectors x ∈ R^n collected from a single site (MLP output, attention output pre-W_O, or residual stream) and layer of a trained language model, and produces a sparse linear reconstruction x̂ = x₀ + Σ f_i(x) d_i where M ≫ n unit-norm dictionary directions d_i are learned and the sparse coefficients f_i(x) ≥ 0 are computed by a single forward pass of the encoder. Training is fully unsupervised on hundreds of millions to billions of activations, with decoder column norms constrained to one to prevent trivial loss minimization via weight scaling.

## Method

The Gated SAE replaces the baseline single-layer ReLU encoder with a gated ReLU encoder that separates feature detection from magnitude estimation: a binary gate (Heaviside step on W_gate(x−b_dec)+b_gate) determines which features are active, while a ReLU path (W_mag(x−b_dec)+b_mag) estimates their magnitudes, and the two are multiplied elementwise before decoding. The L1 sparsity penalty is applied only to the ReLU of the gate's pre-activations, leaving the magnitude path free from shrinkage bias, while an auxiliary reconstruction loss (using a frozen copy of the decoder) ensures the gate learns to detect genuinely useful features. Weight tying parameterizes W_mag as a per-feature rescaling of W_gate (via exp(r_mag)), adding only 2M parameters over a baseline SAE and making the gated encoder equivalent to a single linear layer with a parameterized JumpReLU activation. This architecture, combined with the modified three-term loss (reconstruction + gate sparsity + auxiliary gate reconstruction), yields a Pareto improvement over baseline SAEs: at equal training compute, Gated SAEs achieve comparable reconstruction fidelity with roughly half the L0, resolve shrinkage (γ≈1), and produce comparably interpretable features in a double-blind human study.

## Results

Gated SAEs achieve a Pareto improvement over baseline SAEs across all tested sites (MLP outputs, attention outputs, residual streams) in GELU-1L, Pythia-2.8B, and Gemma-7B: they recover more cross-entropy loss at any fixed L0 sparsity level. Concretely, Gated SAEs require roughly half the L0 (number of firing features) to match baseline SAE reconstruction fidelity — e.g., on Gemma-7B residual stream layer 6, a Gated SAE achieves 99.35% CE recovered at L0=14.3 versus 97.9% at L0=25.4 for the best baseline. Gated SAEs also resolve shrinkage, with relative reconstruction bias γ≈1.0 (e.g., 0.996–1.006) compared to γ=0.72–0.93 for baseline SAEs. In a double-blind interpretability study (342 features across both models), Gated SAE features are comparably interpretable to baseline features (mean score difference 0.13, 95% CI [0, 0.26]).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard (baseline) SAE from Bricken et al. [8], trained with the same L1-regularized reconstruction loss and unit-norm decoder constraint. Gated SAEs win on every tested site and model: they achieve strictly higher loss recovered at every L0 value on the Pareto frontier (Figs. 1, 3, 8, 9), meaning no baseline SAE configuration matches or exceeds a Gated SAE at the same sparsity. The advantage is most pronounced at moderate-to-high sparsity (lower L0), where Gated SAEs recover 1–5% more CE loss than the best baseline at the same L0, and where baseline shrinkage (γ as low as 0.72 for attention SAEs) is most severe. On interpretability, the two methods tie: the paired double-blind study finds a small positive mean difference of 0.13 in favor of Gated SAEs (95% CI [0, 0.26]), but a one-sided Wilcoxon-Pratt test does not reject the null of equal interpretability (p=0.06). An ablation (Fig. 5b) further shows that simply fixing shrinkage on a frozen baseline SAE ('baseline + rescale & shift') recovers only a small fraction of the Gated SAE's performance gap, indicating the gated architecture also learns better encoder/decoder directions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper modifies the baseline sparse autoencoder (SAE) architecture of Bricken et al. [8] by replacing the single ReLU encoder with a gated ReLU encoder, drawing on the Gated Linear Unit pattern from Shazeer [43] and Dauphin et al. [12]. The key conceptual departure is separating the encoder's two implicit roles—detecting which features are active versus estimating their magnitudes—and applying the L1 sparsity penalty only to the detection pathway, thereby limiting the scope of shrinkage bias. This contrasts with prior shrinkage fixes (e.g., Wright and Sharkey's [51] post-hoc fine-tuning) by resolving the issue during training, and with parallel work by Taggart [45] who found similar gains via JumpReLU but without the auxiliary reconstruction loss or the explicit detection/magnitude decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Gated SAEs are a drop-in Pareto improvement over baseline SAEs—requiring roughly half the L0 for equivalent reconstruction fidelity across multiple models (GELU-1L, Pythia-2.8B, Gemma-7B) and sites—making them likely to become the default SAE architecture for mechanistic interpretability work. The ablation showing that addressing shrinkage alone is insufficient (the gated architecture also learns better encoder/decoder directions) suggests the architectural separation has benefits beyond bias correction. Open questions include whether results generalize beyond the Pythia and Gemma families, whether human interpretability of features correlates with downstream task usefulness (circuit analysis, steering), and whether the performance gap could be partially closed by cheaper inference-time thresholding of baseline SAEs.
