## Problem

Existing VideoQA benchmarks rely on short videos (typically under a minute), cover only a handful of basic capabilities (concept existence, object relations, activity recognition), and use GPT-3.5 for scoring free-form answers, which the authors show is inaccurate and poorly aligned with human preferences. These limitations create a mismatch between evaluation and real-world video understanding, where content spans multiple minutes and dozens of shots, and where temporal reasoning is essential.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242826/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds an LVLM a sequence of uniformly sampled frames (1 to 1 fps, typically 8–64 frames) from a 30-second to 6-minute multi-shot YouTube clip, paired with a free-form question; optionally, auto-generated YouTube subtitles are appended as supplementary text context. The model produces a free-form text answer, which GPT-4 scores on a 0–3 semantic-similarity scale against a human-written ground-truth answer. Final results are reported as per-capability means across 26 leaf abilities organized in a 3-level taxonomy (Perception/Reasoning → 9 L-2 categories → 26 L-3 capabilities), enabling fine-grained radar-chart comparisons.

## Method

MMBench-Video is constructed from 609 YouTube videos spanning 16 categories, with 1,998 human-annotated QA pairs designed to be temporally indispensable. A key quality-control step uses GPT-4v to sample a single random frame four times per question; any QA whose average single-frame score reaches 2.5 or above is discarded as temporally dispensable (246 questions removed). For evaluation, the authors replace the GPT-3.5 judge with GPT-4 using a carefully crafted 3-grade prompt that scores semantic similarity while ignoring grammatical differences, and validate this against human ratings on a 100-question subset, showing GPT-4 has lower mean absolute error and greater inter-version stability than GPT-3.5. The benchmark is then used to evaluate 20+ open-source Video-LLMs, open-source image LVLMs, and proprietary models (GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude-3v, GPT-4v) under varying frame counts, revealing that all Video-LLMs score near 1/3 while proprietary models reach 1.9–2.2, and that shot count (not duration) is the dominant factor degrading model performance.

## Results

On MMBench-Video (3-grade scale, 0–3), GPT-4o with 1fps sampling achieves the highest overall mean score of 2.15, followed by GPT-4o-[16f] at 1.86 and Gemini-Pro-v1.5-[1fps] at 1.94. The best open-source image LVLM, VILA1.5-40B-[14f], scores 1.61, while the best open-source Video-LLM, LLaVA-NeXT-Video-[32f], reaches only 1.14. All open-source Video-LLMs cluster near a score of 1.0, indicating nascent video understanding capability. GPT-4o-[1fps] outperforms the best open-source Video-LLM by 63% and the best open-source image LVLM by 16%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o-[1fps] (2.15) dominates all baselines, outperforming GPT-4v-[16f] (1.68) by 28% and Gemini-Pro-v1.5-[1fps] (1.94) by 11%. Among open-source models, VILA1.5-40B-[14f] (1.61) leads image LVLMs, surpassing InternVL2-26B-[16f] (1.41) and Idefics2-8B-[8f] (1.10), while the best Video-LLM LLaVA-NeXT-Video-[32f] (1.14) trails VILA1.5-40B by 29%. All open-source Video-LLMs (Video-ChatGPT 0.93, Video-LLaVA 1.05, VideoChat2 0.99, LLaMA-VID 1.08, PLLaVA 1.03, ShareGPT4Video 1.05) cluster tightly around 1.0, with gaps of less than 0.2 between them, indicating a shared ceiling in video understanding. On image VQA benchmarks (MMBench, MMStar), Video-LLMs such as PLLaVA-7B (52.79/34.73) and Video-LLaVA (52.32/31.33) perform on par with or below the rudimentary LLaVA-v1.5-7B (61.38/33.07), and far below InternVL-Chat-v1.5 (79.95/57.07), confirming that Video-LLMs lack even basic spatial understanding. Claude-3v-Opus-[4f] (1.19) underperforms all other proprietary models, struggling with multi-frame inputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MMBench capability taxonomy (3-level hierarchy) to the video domain, inheriting six L-2 perception/reasoning capabilities and adding three video-specific ones (Hallucination, Commonsense Reasoning, Temporal Reasoning). It departs from the Video-ChatGPT evaluation paradigm, which relied on GPT-3.5 for scoring free-form video QA responses, by replacing it with a GPT-4-based 3-grade judge shown to align more closely with human preferences. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against the short-video, few-capability VideoQA benchmarks (MSVD-QA, MSRVTT-QA, TGIF-QA, ActivityNet-QA) by introducing long-form multi-shot YouTube videos, temporally-indispensable questions, and a 26-leaf capability taxonomy, explicitly targeting the gap between benchmark design and real-world video consumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a striking and well-evidenced gap: all open-source Video-LLMs cluster near 1/3 while even a single-frame Idefics2-8B outperforms them on temporal reasoning, and proprietary models (GPT-4o at 2.15/3) lead by a wide margin. This challenges the assumption that video-specific architectures inherently confer temporal understanding advantages over general-purpose image LVLMs. Open questions include whether the 6-minute duration cap and 609-video scale adequately represent real-world video understanding, whether GPT-4-based judging introduces systematic biases at scale, and whether the temporal-indispensability filtering (removing 246 questions) has inadvertently narrowed the capability space. The benchmark's integration into VLMEvalKit positions it as a likely standard evaluation tool for the field.
