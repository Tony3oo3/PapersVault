## Problem

Existing tracking benchmarks are severely limited in object category diversity (GOT-10k: 563, LaSOT: 70, TrackingNet: 27) and video scale, making it impossible to train trackers that generalize across the vast diversity of real-world objects. Vision-language tracking datasets compound this problem by providing only 1.4K–2K linguistic sentences, far too few for robust language-guided tracking. VastTrack addresses both gaps by introducing a benchmark spanning 2,115 categories, 50,610 videos, and over 50K language descriptions.

## Input / Output

Each video sequence is captured at 6 fps with an average length of 83 frames (4.2M frames total across 50,610 clips), annotated with per-frame axis-aligned bounding boxes plus absence labels for out-of-view or fully occluded frames. A natural-language sentence describing the target's color, behavior, surroundings, and interactions accompanies each video to support vision-language tracking. The evaluation adopts a hybrid protocol in which the 3,500-video test set contains 561 classes overlapping with the 47,110-video training set and 141 completely unseen classes, mimicking real-world tracking of both familiar and novel objects.

## Method

VastTrack is built by selecting 2,115 object categories from ImageNet, V3Det, WordNet, and Wikipedia, organized into 30 coarse classes in a hierarchical tree with each fine category verified by a domain expert. Videos are sourced from YouTube under Creative Commons licensing (66K+ candidates inspected, 50,610 retained), yielding a long-tail distribution across classes that mirrors real-world frequency. Annotation follows a multi-stage pipeline: experts label the initial frame, a dedicated annotator labels all subsequent frames, and a validation team of 2–3 experts performs multi-round verification and refinement to ensure precision. The paper evaluates 25 representative trackers spanning CNN-based, CNN-Transformer, and pure-Transformer architectures under one-pass evaluation using PRE, NPRE, and SUC metrics. Results reveal substantial performance degradation (e.g., SeqTrack SUC drops from 0.725 on LaSOT to 0.396 on VastTrack), and retraining experiments on SiamRPN++ and OSTrack confirm the dataset's utility for improving generalization.

## Results

On VastTrack (3,500 test videos, 702 classes), SeqTrack achieves the best overall performance with 0.402 PRE, 0.429 NPRE, and 0.396 SUC, followed by MixFormer (0.398 PRE, 0.424 NPRE, 0.395 SUC) and DropMAE (0.365 PRE, 0.397 NPRE, 0.375 SUC). All three top trackers are Vision Transformer-based. Critically, every evaluated tracker suffers a severe performance drop on VastTrack compared to existing benchmarks: SeqTrack's SUC falls from 0.855 (TrackingNet) and 0.725 (LaSOT) to 0.396 (VastTrack), a drop of 0.459 and 0.329 respectively, indicating that current trackers lack the generalization needed for universal tracking across 2,115 object categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, VastTrack evaluates 25 existing trackers rather than proposing a new method. The headline finding is that all trackers degrade substantially on VastTrack: the best tracker (SeqTrack) drops from 0.725 SUC on LaSOT to 0.396 on VastTrack (−0.329), and OSTrack drops from 0.711 to 0.336 (−0.375). Among the 25 trackers, Vision Transformer-based methods (SeqTrack, MixFormer, DropMAE) dominate the top three, while the non-Transformer RTS (tracking-by-segmentation) surprisingly outperforms several Transformer trackers like OSTrack (0.336) and SwinTrack (0.330). An interesting ranking shift is observed: trackers that perform well on LaSOT (e.g., OSTrack, SimTrack) rank relatively lower on VastTrack compared to GRM, RTS, and ToMP, suggesting that LaSOT's 70-class evaluation does not capture the category-diversity challenges of VastTrack's 702 test classes. Retraining experiments confirm VastTrack's utility: fine-tuning SiamRPN++ and OSTrack on VastTrack's training set yields 1.7–3.3% SUC gains across all four benchmarks, validating the dataset's value for improving generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

VastTrack extends the lineage of large-scale tracking benchmarks (TrackingNet, LaSOT, GOT-10k, TNL2K) by pushing both category diversity (2,115 vs. 563 in GOT-10k) and video count (50,610 vs. ~10K in GOT-10k) to a new scale, drawing conceptual inspiration from vast-vocabulary detection benchmarks like V3Det (13,204 classes) and LVIS (1,203 classes). Unlike prior tracking datasets that use balanced per-class video counts (LaSOT) or semi-automated annotations (TrackingNet), VastTrack adopts a long-tail category distribution and multi-round manual annotation with linguistic descriptions. The evaluation protocol introduces a hybrid split (561 overlap + 141 unseen classes) that departs from both full-overlap (LaSOT, TrackingNet) and one-shot (GOT-10k) paradigms, better reflecting real-world tracking where some categories are familiar and others are not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The evaluation of 25 trackers reveals that even the best methods (SeqTrack, MixFormer) suffer SUC drops of 0.33–0.46 relative to existing benchmarks, confirming that current trackers are far from achieving the 'universal tracking' goal and that category diversity in training data is a critical bottleneck. The retraining experiments (1.7–3.3% SUC gains) validate VastTrack's utility for improving generalization, but the paper acknowledges it does not propose a new baseline tracker that closes the gap. Open questions include whether the 6 fps frame rate and short average sequence length (83 frames) limit applicability to long-term tracking, and whether the hybrid evaluation protocol's 7% test ratio is sufficient for reliable generalization assessment across 702 test classes.
