## Problem

The pretraining data composition of modern language models—specifically the proportions of different languages, code, and data sources—remains a closely guarded trade secret, and existing membership inference attacks only test whether individual instances were included. This paper introduces data mixture inference, the task of recovering the full distributional make-up of training data, and identifies BPE tokenizer merge lists as a previously overlooked but highly informative signal for this task.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the ordered merge list of a BPE tokenizer (a sequence of token-pair merges m^(1),…,m^(M)) and sample corpora (typically 1 GB each) from n disjoint data categories such as 112 natural languages, 37 programming languages, or 5 English domains. It produces a probability vector α̂ over the n categories estimating each one's byte-level proportion in the tokenizer's training data. The LP is truncated to the top T merges (default T=30,000) to keep the problem tractable, and the method operates on bytes for standard BPE tokenizers or characters for sentencepiece-based tokenizers.

## Method

The core insight is that BPE's iterative training process makes the merge list order a direct fingerprint of token-pair frequencies: at step t, the merge m^(t) must have been the most frequent pair after applying all preceding merges, so the weighted sum of per-category pair counts for m^(t) must dominate that of every other pair. The authors encode this as a linear program (LP1) with variables α (category proportions) and slack variables v for constraint violations, minimizing total violation to handle sampling noise. Because the full LP has O(V³) constraints, they solve a truncated version LP1_T using simultaneous delayed row and column generation: starting from a small constraint subset, they iteratively detect violated constraints via a priority-queue-based checker that exploits the fact that only O(V) pair counts change between consecutive merge steps, adding new constraints until the solution is feasible for the full truncated program.

## Results

In controlled experiments, the LP-based attack achieves log₁₀(MSE) of −7.30 to −7.73 on language mixtures (n=5 to 112), −5.98 to −6.46 on code mixtures, and −3.74 on English domain mixtures (n=5). This represents 2–6 orders of magnitude improvement over random guessing and 2–5 orders of magnitude over both baselines (TEE and TC). The most challenging setting is mixed English domains (−3.74), where categories share vocabulary but differ in style, while mixed natural languages are easiest due to distinct vocabularies. On commercial tokenizers, the attack recovers specific proportions (e.g., GPT-3.5: 62.6% code; GPT-4O: 39% non-English; CLAUDE: 57.5% code) that corroborate or extend publicly disclosed information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: Random guessing, TEE (Tokenizer Encoding Efficiency, which uses byte-to-token ratio normalized by a single-category reference tokenizer), and TC (Token Classification, which assigns each vocabulary token to the category where it appears most frequently). The LP-based method dominates all baselines by 2–5 orders of magnitude in log₁₀(MSE) across every setting. TEE performs best on code mixtures (−2.54 at n=5) while TC performs best on language mixtures (−2.12 at n=5), but neither exceeds one order of magnitude improvement over random. The gap widens with more categories: at n=112 languages, the method achieves −7.69 versus −4.46 for TC and −4.15 for TEE. The authors note that TEE's correlation between training proportion and encoding efficiency is imprecise (true proportion varies up to an order of magnitude for a given efficiency value), and TC's hard assignment of tokens to categories loses information when tokens are shared across categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the well-studied membership inference literature (which tests whether specific instances or authors contributed to training data) by formulating a new task—data mixture inference—that recovers global distributional proportions across categories. While distribution inference attacks have been studied for classifiers, CNNs, and GANs using meta-classifier approaches, this is the first to target LM pretraining data, and it exploits a previously overlooked information source: the ordered BPE merge list. The method builds directly on the BPE training algorithm (Sennrich et al., 2016), whose iterative most-frequent-pair selection encodes frequency information into the merge ordering, and formulates the recovery problem as a linear program solved via delayed column and row generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The attack reveals previously unknown training data compositions for major commercial LLMs (e.g., GPT-4O's 39% non-English data, CLAUDE's ~58% code), enabling external auditing for representational biases, copyright concerns, and design choices that were previously opaque trade secrets. A key open question is whether model producers can defend against this attack (e.g., by reordering merges or abandoning BPE), and whether similar information leaks from other tokenizer families or from the LM parameters themselves. The finding that all studied tokenizers include 7–26% book data—despite books not being a primary stated source—suggests tokenizer training distributions may systematically diverge from LM pretraining distributions, with downstream consequences for encoding efficiency and 'glitch token' vulnerabilities.
