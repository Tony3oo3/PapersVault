## Problem

Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) for continuous state and action spaces lacks algorithms with formal performance guarantees, despite being essential for safety-critical applications like autonomous driving and robotics. Existing IRL methods either operate in finite tabular settings, rely on policy-matching heuristics without solving the IRL problem directly, or require repeated oracle calls to an RL solver. This paper fills the gap by providing the first LP-based IRL framework for continuous MDPs with explicit PAC-style sample complexity bounds.

## Input / Output

The method consumes truncated i.i.d. expert trajectories (horizon H), a generative-model oracle that returns next states given (x,a) pairs, and user-chosen linearly independent basis functions for both cost and value functions. It outputs a cost function as a sparse linear combination of the cost basis functions that is ε-inverse feasible with probability at least 1−δ, along with explicit bounds on the number of expert samples m, generative-model calls k per constraint, and sampled constraints N needed. A key structural detail is the linear normalization constraint ∫(c−T*γu)dxda=1, which eliminates trivial constant-cost solutions and makes the problem well-posed without requiring identifiability.

## Method

The paper characterizes the inverse feasibility set C(π_E) via occupation measures, primal-dual LP duality, and complementary slackness conditions, yielding an infinite-dimensional linear feasibility problem. To make it tractable, it restricts decision variables to finite-dimensional subspaces spanned by basis functions (tightening) and replaces the uncountably many pointwise constraints with N randomly sampled ones via the scenario approach (relaxation), producing a finite-dimensional convex LP solvable in polynomial time. The regularization parameter θ on the ℓ₁-norm of basis coefficients bounds the dual optimizer and yields an explicit approximation error ε_approx that depends on the projection residuals of the true cost and value functions onto the chosen subspaces. For the realistic offline setting, empirical estimates of the occupation measure and transition operator are plugged in, and uniform concentration bounds from statistical learning theory give the final sample complexity: m = O(n_c log(n_c/δ)/((1−γ)²ε²)) expert samples and N = O(exp(dim(X×A))) sampled constraints, with probability 1−δ the recovered cost is (ε + ε_approx)-inverse feasible.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contributions are theoretical sample complexity and approximation error bounds rather than empirical benchmark numbers. Theorem 4.1 establishes that sampling N ≥ N(n_c + n_u + 1, g(ε/L_Λ), δ) constraints (where N scales as ~{1/ε, log(1/δ), n} and g(r) ~ r^{dim(X×A)}) suffices to guarantee, with probability ≥ 1−δ, that the recovered cost c̃_N is (ε_approx + ε)-inverse feasible. Theorem 4.2 extends this to the sample-based setting, requiring m = O(n_c log(n_c/δ) / ((1−γ)²ε²)) expert trajectories and k = O(n_u log(n_u N/δ) / ε²) generative model calls per constraint. The approximation error ε_approx (Proposition 4.2) is governed by the projection residuals of the true cost and value function onto the chosen finite-dimensional basis, vanishing as n_c, n_u, θ → ∞ for linearly dense bases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without a standard empirical comparison table; comparisons are made at the level of problem formulation and guarantee scope. The framework strictly generalizes the tabular MDP inverse feasibility sets of Metelli et al. [52, 53, 54] (recovered exactly in Appendix A.2 when X and A are finite and π_E is stationary Markov) and the deterministic-expert LP formulations of Ng & Russell [12] and Komanduru & Honorio [49, 50]. Compared to Dexter et al. [51], which handles continuous states but finite actions, this paper extends to continuous action spaces as well, at the cost of an exponential-in-dimension sample complexity (N ~ exp(dim(X×A))) that [51] also exhibits. Relative to Pauwels et al. [48] (deterministic continuous-time inverse optimal control via sum-of-squares), the present work handles stochastic discrete-time dynamics without requiring polynomial dynamics or semi-algebraic constraints, and adds probabilistic (PAC-style) performance guarantees. The paper does not report head-to-head numerical benchmarks against any prior method; the sole numerical illustration (Appendix C, a truncated LQR) is intended to validate the sample complexity scaling rather than to compare against competing algorithms.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the finite-dimensional inverse LP framework of Ahuja & Orlin and Iyengar & Kang to infinite-dimensional continuous MDPs by leveraging occupation measures, linear duality, and complementary slackness. It generalizes the tabular IRL formulations of Metelli et al. and the continuous-state-with-finite-action setting of Dexter et al. to the full continuous state-and-action space, while also extending Pauwels et al.'s deterministic continuous-time inverse optimal control to the stochastic discrete-time case. The computational tractability is achieved through the scenario approach from randomized convex optimization (Campi, Garatti, Mohajerin Esfahani), combined with linear function approximation and l1-regularization, which is a novel application of these tools to the IRL problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical treatment of IRL in continuous state and action spaces with formal PAC-style guarantees, filling a significant gap between empirical IRL methods (which lack guarantees) and prior theoretical work (restricted to tabular or finite-action settings). The explicit sample complexity bounds and the normalization constraint addressing the reward ambiguity problem provide a foundation for safety-critical applications. However, the exponential dependence on the dimension of the state-action space (curse of dimensionality) limits practical applicability to low-dimensional problems, and the paper acknowledges that better sampling distributions and exploitation of problem structure are open questions. The identifiability problem remains unresolved, and the empirical validation is limited to a simple LQR example.
