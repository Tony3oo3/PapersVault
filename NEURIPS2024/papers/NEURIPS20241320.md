## Problem

When a policy maker makes decisions affecting groups of individuals, their underlying social welfare function is typically implicit and unobserved. This paper asks whether one can learn such a function from past decisions, formalizing the problem over the weighted power mean family (which encompasses utilitarian, egalitarian, and Nash welfare) and proving that it is PAC-learnable with polynomial sample complexity — a result that was previously unestablished for this function class.

## Input / Output

The method operates on d-dimensional utility vectors in [u_min, u_max]^d. In the cardinal setting, each utility vector is paired with a scalar social welfare value (regression); in the ordinal setting, pairs of utility vectors are accompanied by a binary preference label, optionally corrupted by i.i.d. or logistic (BTL) noise. The output is the power mean exponent p, the weight vector w ∈ Δ_{d-1} (when unknown), and the temperature parameter τ (in the logistic noise case). A key structural constraint is that the function class is non-convex in p but quasilinear in w for fixed p, which the algorithm exploits.

## Method

The core contribution is deriving tight statistical complexity measures for the weighted power mean function class: pseudo-dimension (1 for known weights, O(d log d) for unknown) in the cardinal case, and VC dimension (O(log d) and O(d log d) respectively) in the ordinal case. The ordinal bound relies on the observation that the difference of log power means between two utility vectors has at most 2d−1 roots as a function of p, limiting sign changes and hence shattering capacity. For noisy settings, the paper adapts Natarajan et al.'s unbiased estimator framework for i.i.d. label noise and derives Rademacher-based bounds for logistic noise where τ is jointly estimated. The practical algorithm performs a grid search over p with gradient descent over w at each grid point, leveraging the quasiconvexity of both ℓ₂ and logistic losses in w for fixed p to ensure convergence to a near-optimal solution despite the overall non-convexity.

## Results

The paper establishes polynomial sample complexity bounds for learning weighted power mean social welfare functions. For cardinal values under ℓ₂ loss, the sample complexity is O(1) with known weights and O(d log d) with unknown weights. For pairwise comparisons under 0-1 loss, the bounds are O(log d) (known weights) and O(d log d) (unknown weights), with matching lower bounds shown for the known-weights case. Under i.i.d. label noise at rate ρ, complexity scales as O(log d/(1−2ρ)²) and O(d log d/(1−2ρ)²); under logistic noise with temperature τ_max, it scales as O(τ_max²κ²) and O(τ_max²κ²d log d). Empirically, on semi-synthetic WeBuildAI data, the learned parameters' test loss closely matches the true parameters' loss, and KL divergence between true and learned weights decreases uniformly with more samples and less noise.

**Table 1: Sample complexity of learning weighted power mean functions.**

| Social Welfare Information | Loss | Known Weights | Unknown Weights |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cardinal values | ℓ₂ | O(ξ²) | O(ξ²d log d) |
| Pairwise comparisons | 0-1 | O(log d) | O(d log d) |
| Pairwise comparison with i.i.d. noise | 0-1 | O(log d / (1−2ρ)²) | O(d log d / (1−2ρ)²) |
| Pairwise comparisons with logistic noise | Logistic | O(τ_max²κ²) | O(τ_max²κ²d log d) |

where ξ = u_max(u_max − u_min), κ = log(u_max/u_min), ρ ∈ [0, 1/2) is the i.i.d. mislabeling probability, and τ_max is the maximum logistic noise temperature.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing learning methods or baselines; it is primarily a theoretical contribution establishing PAC learnability and sample complexity bounds for the weighted power mean function class. The empirical evaluation in Section 6 validates the theoretical predictions by comparing learned parameters (p, w, τ) against the ground-truth generating parameters on semi-synthetic WeBuildAI data, rather than benchmarking against alternative algorithms. The key empirical finding is that the proposed grid-search-on-p plus gradient-descent-on-w algorithm recovers parameters whose test loss matches that of the true parameters, with KL(w*‖ŵ) and |p̂ − p*| both decreasing as sample size increases and noise decreases. The paper also verifies the predicted O(d log d) scaling of error with d in fully synthetic experiments (Appendix E, Figure 8). The closest related work (Procaccia et al. [18] on voting rules, Basu and Echenique [1] on decision rules under uncertainty) addresses different function classes and input representations, so no direct empirical comparison is made.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learnability-of-aggregation-functions line of work (Procaccia et al. on voting rules with rankings, Pellegrini et al. on empirical Lp-mean aggregation, Melnikov and Hüllermeier on OWA) by providing the first PAC learnability guarantees for the weighted power mean family under cardinal utilities and both cardinal and ordinal observations. It builds directly on Natarajan et al.'s noisy-label framework for the i.i.d. noise case and on the BTL/Luce model for the logistic noise case. The axiomatic characterization of power means as the unique family satisfying monotonicity, symmetry, and scale invariance (Cousins; Roberts) motivates the function class choice, while the statistical complexity tools (pseudo-dimension, VC dimension, Rademacher complexity) are applied in a novel way to exploit monotonicity in p and the restricted number of sign changes in the comparison function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical justification that a policy maker's implicit social welfare function can be reliably inferred from past decisions, with explicit sample complexity guarantees that scale polynomially in the number of individuals. The restriction to power means is both a strength (unique under natural axioms) and a limitation: extending to non-parametric or broader parametric families remains open. Practical challenges persist—utility estimation is nontrivial, the optimization landscape is non-convex, and scaling to very large d (e.g., national populations) is computationally prohibitive. The food-resource-allocation application demonstrates feasibility but relies on externally learned utility functions, leaving the end-to-end problem of learning from raw decision data unresolved.
