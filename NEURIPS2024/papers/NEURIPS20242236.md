## Problem

Depth refinement methods that merge numerous image patches to recover high-resolution details suffer from heavy computational cost, inconsistent depth structures, and blurred edges due to noisy annotations or pseudo-labels. Prior approaches trained on synthetic data lack generalizability to real-world scenes, while natural-scene annotations are too sparse, inaccurate, or edge-blurred to supervise fine-grained refinement. The paper identifies the root cause as two distinct degradation modes—local inconsistency and edge deformation—and models refinement as a noisy Poisson fusion problem to address both simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image resized to two resolutions (low L and high H), passes both through a frozen depth predictor (e.g., MiDaS, LeReS, ZoeDepth) to obtain two depth maps, and feeds these into the refinement network. The primary output is a high-resolution refined depth map at the original image resolution; a secondary output is a depth edge representation G_S (gradient magnitude map) generated through coarse-to-fine self-distillation, which serves as pseudo-labels for training. During two-stage inference, the image is partitioned into (s+1)² overlapping windows across S=3 iterations, with adaptive resolution adjustment per window based on local edge density.

## Method

SDDR models depth refinement as noisy Poisson fusion, decomposing prediction errors into local inconsistency noise (regional affine perturbation) and edge deformation noise (Gaussian blur on edges). A coarse-to-fine self-distillation loop generates low-noise depth edge representations as pseudo-labels: the image is progressively subdivided into more windows across S iterations, with scale-and-shift alignment between consecutive iterations to maintain consistency. Training is guided by two losses: an edge-guided gradient loss that supervises gradient alignment only within k-means-clustered high-frequency regions (with per-region affine alignment), and an edge-based fusion loss that enforces distributional consistency between a learnable soft mask Ω and the pseudo-label G_S via quantile sampling, controlling where the network fuses low- vs. high-resolution features. The refinement network is a lightweight U-Net with a shared MiT-b0 encoder and four self-attention layers for cross-resolution feature interaction, reducing parameters by 12.5× over GBDF while achieving superior accuracy and edge quality across five benchmarks.

## Results

On Middlebury2021 with MiDaS as the depth predictor, SDDR achieves δ₁=0.879, REL=0.112, and ORD=0.299, outperforming the best prior one-stage method (GBDF: δ₁=0.871, REL=0.115, ORD=0.305). In the two-stage setting with ZoeDepth on Hypersim, SDDR reaches δ₁=0.933, REL=0.083, and ORD=0.189, surpassing PatchFusion (δ₁=0.881, ORD=0.258) by 5.2% and 26.7% respectively. On the in-the-wild DIODE benchmark, SDDR improves REL by 5.7% and ORD by 9.0% over the best baseline (GBDF). In terms of efficiency, SDDR requires only 16.733G FLOPs (one-stage) versus 286.13G×63 patches for Boost and 810.813G×177 patches for PatchFusion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SDDR consistently outperforms all five baselines across every dataset and predictor combination. Against the one-stage methods, SDDR improves over GBDF [3] by 0.8% in δ₁ and 2.6% in ORD with MiDaS on Middlebury2021, and over Kim et al. [14] by 6.6% in REL and 20.7% in ORD under the same setting. Graph-GDSR [4] performs comparably to the raw depth predictor on most metrics, while SDDR delivers clear gains in both accuracy and edge quality. In the two-stage comparison, SDDR surpasses PatchFusion [21] by 5.2% in δ₁ and 26.7% in ORD with ZoeDepth on Hypersim, and outperforms Boost [25] on all three benchmarks. On the generalization benchmarks (DIML and DIODE), SDDR achieves the best D³R (0.220 vs. 0.267 for GBDF on DIML) and ORD (0.293 vs. 0.322 for GBDF on DIODE), confirming that the self-distillation paradigm transfers well to unseen in-the-wild scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant tile-based two-stage refinement paradigm (Boost, PatchFusion) that merges numerous patches for local details, and from one-stage global refinement methods (GBDF, Kim et al.) that produce blurred edges. It reframes depth refinement as a noisy Poisson fusion problem, decomposing prediction errors into local inconsistency noise and edge deformation noise, then addresses both through a self-distillation loop that generates low-noise depth edge pseudo-labels via coarse-to-fine window refinement. The edge-based guidance (edge-guided gradient loss with k-means clustering for high-frequency regions, and edge-based fusion loss with quantile sampling for adaptive feature blending) is a novel optimization objective that replaces the ad-hoc pseudo-label strategies (guided filtering of depth predictions, adversarial training) used by prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The self-distillation paradigm offers a principled path to training depth refinement models on real-world data without requiring perfectly annotated edges, addressing a core bottleneck that has limited generalizability of prior methods. The transferability result—where SDDR-generated edge labels directly improve another model (GBDF)—suggests the framework could serve as a label generator for broader refinement research. Limitations remain: performance is still bounded by the underlying depth predictor's quality on specular, low-light, or weak-texture inputs, and the coarse-to-fine refinement, while more efficient than tile-based methods, still requires multiple forward passes (S=3 iterations, 30 windows) for the two-stage variant.
