## Problem

First-order optimization algorithms (SGD, Adam, etc.) require many sequential iterations to converge, and while parallel computing has been widely used to reduce per-iteration evaluation cost (data/model/pipeline parallelism), no general framework existed to reduce the number of sequential iterations themselves. The inherent iterative dependency—where each iteration's output feeds the next—makes concurrent execution of iterations nearly impossible in standard FOO, leaving a significant gap in optimization efficiency for expensive tasks like deep RL and neural network training.

## Input / Output

The method takes an objective function F(θ) with stochastic gradient access ∇f(θ), initial parameters θ₀, a scalar kernel function k(·,·) (e.g., Matérn or RBF), and a parallelism level N. It produces optimized parameters θ_T after T sequential iterations, where each sequential iteration internally executes N parallel FOO sub-steps. A key design choice is that only a local gradient history of size T₀ (not the full history) is retained for kernelized estimation, keeping space complexity at O(T₀d) and computational cost at O(T₀³ + T₀d) per iteration, which is critical for high-dimensional problems (d up to ~2.4M in their experiments).

## Method

OptEx breaks the iterative dependency in FOO through a three-stage pipeline: (1) a kernelized gradient estimation that models ∇F as a Gaussian process with separable kernel K(·,·)=k(·,·)I, using the local history of evaluated gradients to predict ∇F(θ) at any input via the GP posterior mean μ_t(θ); (2) multi-step proxy updates that sequentially apply the FOO algorithm using these estimated gradients to approximate the inputs {θ_{t,s}} for the next N iterations; (3) approximately parallelized iterations where N standard FOO steps are executed concurrently on the proxy inputs using ground-truth gradients ∇f(θ_{t,s-1}), with the final output θ_t = θ_t^(N) feeding the next sequential iteration. The separable kernel decouples the d-dimensional GP into d independent scalar GPs, and the local history keeps computation tractable. Theoretically, the estimation error diminishes as O(√(γ_{T₀}/T₀)) with accumulated gradients, yielding a Θ(√N) acceleration rate over standard SGD in sequential iteration complexity.

## Results

On synthetic functions (Ackley, Sphere, Rosenbrock) with parallelism N=5, OptEx achieves at least a 2× reduction in sequential iterations compared to the Vanilla baseline to reach the same optimality gap, consistent with the theoretical Θ(√N) acceleration rate (√5 ≈ 2.24). On CIFAR-10 (d=2,412,298) and Shakespeare Corpus (d=1,626,496) neural network training with N=4, OptEx consistently outperforms Vanilla by a large margin in both training/test error and wallclock time, approaching the performance of the ideal Target baseline. In reinforcement learning (DQN on OpenAI Gym tasks, N=4), OptEx delivers more stable and superior cumulative reward improvements over Vanilla, and in some cases even exceeds the Target baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares OptEx against two baselines: Vanilla (standard FOO with N=1, using SGD for neural network training and Adam for synthetic/RL tasks) and Target (an ideal but impractical parallelization that uses ground-truth gradients for proxy updates). OptEx decisively outperforms Vanilla across all experimental settings: on synthetic functions it requires at least 2× fewer sequential iterations (N=5); on CIFAR-10 and Shakespeare it achieves substantially lower train/test error at equal iteration counts or wallclock time (N=4); and on RL tasks it yields higher cumulative reward per episode. Against the Target baseline, OptEx slightly underperforms on synthetic functions (as expected, since Target uses true gradients while OptEx relies on kernelized estimation), but approaches Target closely on neural network training. Notably, in reinforcement learning OptEx can even surpass Target, attributed to the kernelized gradient estimation's variance asymptotically approaching zero with accumulated gradient history, whereas Target retains fixed gradient variance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Gaussian Process posterior inference (from Bayesian optimization and kernelized bandits) to predict future gradients in first-order optimization, enabling 'proxy updates' that break the sequential dependency inherent in FOO. It departs from the dominant parallel-computing literature in optimization (data parallelism, model parallelism, pipeline parallelism), which reduces per-iteration evaluation cost, by instead targeting the number of sequential iterations required for convergence. The iteration complexity analysis builds on standard SGD convergence theory (Liu et al. 2023, Drori & Shamir 2020) while incorporating the estimation error from the kernelized gradient prediction, yielding a Θ(√N) acceleration bound that is tight (matching upper and lower bounds).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OptEx offers a general, domain-agnostic mechanism to reduce sequential iterations in any FOO algorithm, complementing rather than competing with existing parallelism techniques. However, the practical speedup is modest (~2× with N=5 in synthetic experiments), and the GP assumption on the gradient landscape is strong and may not hold for all deep learning objectives. The acknowledged overhead of kernelized gradient estimation (storage and O(T₀³) computation per iteration) limits immediate applicability to very large models, and the paper does not demonstrate scaling beyond ~2.4M parameters. Open questions include whether the GP assumption can be relaxed, whether the method composes cleanly with data parallelism, and whether the speedup persists at the scale of modern LLM training.
