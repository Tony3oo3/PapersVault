## Problem

LLMs exhibit a large gap between pass@1 and pass@k accuracy in autoformalization: correct formalizations frequently appear among multiple generations but are difficult to identify. Standard self-consistency techniques from math reasoning (comparing final answers) and code generation (comparing execution traces) do not transfer to autoformalization because of variable-naming inconsistencies across candidates and the absence of executable test cases for mathematical statements.

## Input / Output

Given a natural language mathematical statement, the framework uses few-shot prompting to generate k=10 Isabelle/HOL formalization candidates from an LLM (temperature 0.7). Each candidate is scored on two axes—symbolic equivalence (its proportion within the derived equivalence class) and semantic consistency (cosine similarity between BERT embeddings of the LLM-re-informalized candidate and the original text)—and the combined score produces a ranked ordering from which the top candidate is returned as the final formalization.

## Method

The core contribution is a dual self-consistency scoring framework that requires no training. Symbolic equivalence decomposes each formal statement into premises and conclusion, standardizes variable names (introducing an auxiliary variable α for numerical-relational goals, or performing bipartite matching weighted by string edit distance for non-numerical goals), and then uses ATPs (Sledgehammer, Z3, CVC5) to verify logical equivalence between all candidate pairs; the score is the size of each candidate's equivalence class. Semantic consistency back-translates each candidate to natural language via a second LLM call and measures BERT embedding cosine similarity to the original statement, catching trivial or semantically distorted formalizations (e.g., reducing a problem to '4=4') that symbolic equivalence alone would accept. The two softmax-normalized scores are combined via a log-weighted formula with a tunable trade-off parameter α (set to 0.5), and the highest-scoring candidate is selected as the final autoformalization.

## Results

On the MATH dataset, SymEq (symbolic equivalence) achieves the best 1@k accuracy across all five LLMs, reaching 46.1% with Codex and 42.0% with GPT-4, compared to 43.8% and 37.5% for the log-probability baseline respectively. On miniF2F, SymEq with GPT-4 reaches 41.1% 1@k versus 32.9% for the baseline. The log-combination strategy further boosts accuracy by up to 7.8% (MATH) and 10.7% (miniF2F) over SymEq alone with GPT-4, and achieves relative labeling efficiency gains of 8.4%–21.9% across all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: (1) a log-probability baseline that scores candidates by LLM confidence (or LLM self-ranking for Codex/GPT-4), (2) a Naive method that filters candidates by whether an ATP can prove the formalization, and (3) a Cluster method applying adaptive k-means on BERT embeddings of formal statements. SymEq consistently outperforms all three baselines at 1@k across every LLM–dataset combination, with the largest gains on weaker models (e.g., +8.6% over baseline for Mistral-7B on MATH) and the smallest on stronger ones (e.g., +2.3% for Codex on MATH). SemCo is more competitive but generally trails SymEq, winning only in a few cases (e.g., 3@k for Mistral-7B and GPT-4 on MATH). The Naive method is the weakest, often performing below the log-probability baseline (negative relative efficiency), while Cluster is intermediate. The log-combination of SymEq and SemCo further improves over SymEq alone by 2.3%–22.6% depending on model and dataset, confirming the two signals are complementary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the self-consistency paradigm (Wang et al., ICLR 2022) to the autoformalization setting, where neither answer-matching (as in math reasoning) nor execution-based comparison (as in code generation) is applicable. It introduces two complementary consistency signals—symbolic equivalence verified via automated theorem provers (Sledgehammer, Z3, CVC5) and semantic consistency measured by BERT embedding similarity of back-translated text—that go beyond prior autoformalization work (Wu et al. 2022, ProofGPT, MMA) which focused on generation rather than candidate selection. The variable-matching step (numerical standardization or bipartite matching via edit distance) is a practical contribution that addresses a known obstacle in ATP-based equivalence checking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, training-free method to close the 19.5–26.5% gap between pass@1 and pass@10 in LLM autoformalization, reducing human verification effort by up to ~22%. The category-specific complementarity (geometry favors semantic consistency, number theory favors symbolic equivalence) suggests that no single consistency signal suffices, motivating hybrid scoring in future autoformalization pipelines. Key open questions remain: whether the approach transfers to Lean 4 or Coq, whether specialized models (ProofGPT, MMA) further narrow the gap, and whether current embeddings can capture the fine-grained notational distinctions that matter in formal mathematics.
