## Problem

Existing interpretable methods for medical image classification suffer from three critical gaps: (1) concept-based models rely on expensive expert annotation and treat concept features as the sole basis for prediction, degrading accuracy; (2) neural-symbolic concept models focus on supervised concept settings and produce concept importance scores that are misaligned with saliency maps, reducing clinician trust; (3) single-dimensional explanations (e.g., only saliency maps or only concept scores) provide insufficient decision information for physicians. The paper addresses the absence of a unified framework that simultaneously aligns concept scores, reasoning rules, and saliency maps while maintaining competitive classification accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes medical images (CT, chest X-ray, or dermatology) resized to 256×256 as primary input, supplemented by text concept descriptions processed through a vision-language model (BioViL) for automatic pseudo-labeling. Beyond the standard disease class label, the model produces three additional interpretability outputs: per-concept prediction scores (continuous values in [0,1]), fuzzy-logic reasoning rules expressing concept polarity and relevance for each class, and pixel-level saliency maps. The concept set itself is auto-generated by GPT-4V from sampled images, requiring only 20% of the dataset to be annotated for fine-tuning rather than full expert labeling.

## Method

Med-MICN is an end-to-end framework with four modules: a backbone CNN extracts pixel-level features; a concept encoder simultaneously produces concept embeddings and complementary feature embeddings; a VLM-based alignment module generates concept heatmaps via cosine similarity between text and visual embeddings, then applies average pooling and thresholding to derive binary concept labels; and a neural-symbolic layer with two per-class networks (concept polarity Φ and concept relevance Ψ) outputs fuzzy-logic reasoning rules via the formula ŷⱼ = minᵢ{max(1−I_{o,i,j}, I_{r,i,j})}. The key architectural distinction from prior CBMs is that concept embeddings are concatenated with the original image features through a fusion module before the classification head, so the model leverages both intrinsic spatial information and concept semantics rather than relying solely on the concept bottleneck. Training optimizes a composite loss combining task cross-entropy, concept binary cross-entropy (L_c), and neural-symbolic prediction loss (L_neural) with equal weighting (λ₁=λ₂=0.1), enabling multi-dimensional alignment between image features, concept semantics, and saliency maps without post-hoc explanation.

## Results

Med-MICN achieves the best accuracy across all four medical datasets while providing multi-dimensional interpretability. On COVID-CT, it reaches 84.75% accuracy (ResNet50) and 86.44% (DenseNet169), surpassing the SSSD-COVID black-box baseline (81.76%) by 3.0–4.7 points. On DDI, Chest X-Ray, and Fitzpatrick17k, it improves over its own backbone by 3.5–6.8% in accuracy (e.g., 82.58% vs 76.52% with VGG19 on DDI; 88.30% vs 81.41% with VGG19 on Chest X-Ray). Ablation studies confirm that both the concept loss L_c and the neural-symbolic loss L_neural are indispensable, with their combination yielding up to 8.71% AUC improvement over the backbone alone on DDI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Med-MICN decisively outperforms both interpretable baselines across all four datasets. Against Label Free CBM [41], it gains 12–17 percentage points in accuracy (e.g., 84.75% vs 69.49% on COVID-CT; 82.58% vs 70.34% on DDI) while offering richer multi-dimensional explanations (concept rules + saliency maps vs. concept scores alone). Against DCR [3], the advantage is even more pronounced: Med-MICN achieves 84.75% vs 55.93% accuracy on COVID-CT and 88.30% vs 62.02% on Chest X-Ray, indicating that DCR's neural-symbolic approach degrades severely on complex medical images. Compared to the black-box SSSD-COVID [51] (evaluated only on COVID-CT), Med-MICN with ResNet50 improves accuracy by 3.0 points (84.75% vs 81.76%) and F1 by 4.75 points (84.75% vs 80.00%), while additionally providing interpretability. Notably, Med-MICN also surpasses its own backbone baselines (e.g., +3.39% Acc over ResNet50 on COVID-CT, +6.01% over VGG19 on DDI), demonstrating that the concept-embedding fusion module enhances rather than degrades classification performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

Med-MICN unifies three previously separate lines of interpretable deep learning: Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., 2020), neural-symbolic concept reasoning (Barbiero et al., 2023), and concept embedding models (Espinosa Zarlenga et al., 2022). Its key departure is treating these not as competing alternatives but as complementary dimensions that must be aligned simultaneously—concept scores, logical rules, and saliency maps—rather than relying on any single explanation channel. The use of GPT-4V for concept generation and BioViL for pseudo-labeling extends the Label-free CBM paradigm (Oikarinen et al., 2023) into the medical domain, where expert annotation cost is prohibitive, and the fusion of concept embeddings with original image features (rather than replacing them) directly addresses the well-known accuracy degradation of pure CBMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multi-dimensional alignment strategy offers a practical safeguard for clinical deployment: if one explanation channel is misleading, physicians can cross-check against the others, reducing the risk of over-reliance on a single potentially erroneous signal. The automated concept labeling pipeline lowers the barrier to applying concept-based interpretability to new medical datasets without extensive expert annotation. However, the evaluation is limited to four relatively small datasets (746–5,933 images) and binary classification tasks, and the neural-symbolic layer's scalability to larger concept sets or multi-class settings remains an open question. Whether the alignment benefits persist on larger, more heterogeneous medical corpora or in 3D volumetric imaging is untested.
