## Problem

Existing vision-and-language benchmarks overwhelmingly rely on single visual inputs, making them unable to assess a model's ability to compare two images and reason about their relativity. Despite being fundamental to everyday decision-making (e.g., judging which apple is fresher or which sofa fits better), comparative reasoning across multiple visual inputs remains largely unexplored in the AGI literature.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240906/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark sample consists of a horizontally concatenated pair of visually or semantically relevant RGB images plus a text question about their relativity. The expected output is a comparative answer: binary left/right for most tasks, multiple-choice (with a 'None' option) for Existence and Quality, or three-way left/right/same for Quantity. Evaluation uses exact-match accuracy, and the benchmark is split 80:20 into a 31.8K test set and a 7.9K held-out set.

## Method

MLLM-COMPBENCH is constructed by mining image pairs from 14 public vision datasets (spanning 14 domains) using their metadata (attributes, temporal tags, depth maps, object counts) and CLIP visual similarity to ensure pairs are sufficiently similar. Questions are generated via rule-based templates, GPT-4V in-context prompting (for Existence), or GPT-4 text transformation (for Quantity), then verified and answered by five human annotators with cross-verification requiring unanimous agreement. For evaluation, two images are concatenated side-by-side and fed to MLLMs (GPT-4V, Gemini1.0-Pro, LLaVA-1.6, VILA-1.5) with task-specific prompts; the paper also investigates a two-stage reasoning strategy (analyze each image independently, then compare via a pure language question) and LoRA fine-tuning of LLaVA-1.6 on temporality and quantity subsets, revealing that decomposed absolute reasoning often underperforms direct relative comparison.

## Results

On the MLLM-COMPBENCH test split (31.8K samples), GPT-4V achieves the highest average accuracy at 74.7%, followed by LLaVA-1.6 (66.0%), Gemini1.0-Pro (63.0%), and VILA-1.5 (58.0%), all well below the human upper bound of 86.5% (Table 4). The weakest areas across all models are Existence (GPT-4V: 50.1% avg on MB/SD), Spatiality (GPT-4V: 56.1%), and Quantity (GPT-4V: 63.8%), while State and Emotion relativities are comparatively strong (GPT-4V: 90.0% and 87.6% respectively). Post-deadline evaluations show GPT-4o reaching 83.1% and Gemini1.5-Pro reaching 74.2%, representing substantial gains over their predecessors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V [1] is the strongest baseline at 74.7% average accuracy, outperforming LLaVA-1.6 [33] (66.0%), Gemini1.0-Pro [58] (63.0%), and VILA-1.5 [32] (58.0%) by 8.7, 11.7, and 16.7 points respectively. However, GPT-4V still trails human annotators by ~18 percentage points (74.7% vs. 86.5%), indicating substantial room for improvement. LLaVA-1.6 [33] is notably weak on Existence (23.6% avg on MB/SD, near chance) but surprisingly strong on Emotion (89.9% avg), while Gemini1.0-Pro [58] and VILA-1.5 [32] remain close to chance on Spatiality (56.6% and 51.8%). Post-deadline, GPT-4o [42] improves over GPT-4V by 8.4 points on average (83.1% vs. 74.7%), with the largest gains in Existence (+22.9 on MB, +25.3 on SD) and Spatiality (+12.1), while Gemini1.5-Pro [52] gains 11.2 points over Gemini1.0-Pro (74.2% vs. 63.0%), with a notable 32.4-point jump on CelebA emotion but a 4.8-point drop on SoccerNet temporality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing fourteen existing vision datasets (MIT-States, Fashionpedia, SoccerNet, VQAv2, etc.) into a unified comparative reasoning benchmark, rather than building on a single prior method. It departs from prior multi-image datasets (NLVR2, Birds-to-Words, Spot-the-diff, Q-bench2, Relative Attributes) that each target only one narrow comparison type, by systematically covering eight distinct relativity dimensions across fourteen visual domains. The paper also positions itself against the broader MLLM evaluation landscape (VQA, captioning, chart understanding), arguing that the near-exclusive focus on single-image inputs has left comparative reasoning unassessed. A concurrent work (REMI, Kazemi et al.) addresses multi-image reasoning but focuses on different domains (math, physics, code, tables).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest MLLMs (GPT-4V at 74.7%, GPT-4o at 83.1%) remain substantially below human performance (86.5%), with particularly stark weaknesses in spatial reasoning (near-chance for proprietary models), existence detection, and quantity comparison. The finding that a two-stage 'analyze-then-compare' strategy actually degrades GPT-4V's performance challenges the intuitive decomposition approach and suggests that holistic visual comparison is harder to decompose than expected. The fine-tuning results (dramatic temporality gains but minimal quantity gains) point to vision-encoder-level bottlenecks (CLIP's weak counting) that language-side fine-tuning cannot fix, motivating new architectural approaches. Open questions include whether the benchmark's 80:20 split and binary/multiple-choice format adequately capture the full difficulty of comparative reasoning, and whether the 140-sample human evaluation is sufficient to establish a reliable upper bound.
