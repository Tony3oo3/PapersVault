## Problem

Even state-of-the-art LLMs like GPT-4 achieve only ~28% accuracy on college-level scientific problems, revealing deficiencies in symbolic equation derivation, rigorous numerical computation, and multi-concept scientific reasoning. The core bottleneck is data scarcity: most legally accessible scientific QA pairs lack the step-by-step chain-of-thought reasoning traces needed for effective instruction tuning, and merely training on bare QA pairs degrades general language capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240046/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset takes 257,143 raw scientific questions (physics, chemistry, college-level math, and Lean formal theorems) as input and produces 254,051 verified instruction instances as output. Each instance follows a structured template: an 'Analysis' paragraph identifying relevant knowledge points, numbered 'Step 1…Step n' reasoning lines with explicit equations and calculations, and a final summary sentence stating the answer. For Lean formal proofs, the format is a theorem statement followed by its proof script. Questions originally in Chinese are translated to English to leverage higher-quality English pre-training corpora.

## Method

The key contribution is a three-stage self-reflective annotation pipeline that uses GPT-4 to autonomously generate, critique, and revise reasoning traces with minimal human intervention. Stage 1 applies a standard CoT prompt; Stage 2 feeds incorrect outputs back to GPT-4 with a reflective prompt asking it to identify its error type and produce a corrected solution; Stage 3 incorporates the ground-truth answer as a direct hint for remaining failures. An outcome-reward-model-based GPT-4 labeling step filters correct vs. incorrect solutions at each stage. A separate instruction-quality classifier (trained on ChatGLM3-6B-Base features with 41,709 labeled examples) then removes low-quality traces where the final answer is correct but intermediate reasoning is flawed. The resulting SciInstruct mixture (48.76% physics/chemistry, 35.40% math, 15.84% Lean proofs) is used to fine-tune ChatGLM3-6B/32B-Base, Llama3-8B-Instruct, and Mistral-7B:MetaMATH via supervised instruction tuning with DeepSpeed at learning rate 3e-6 for two epochs.

## Results

Fine-tuning ChatGLM3-6B-Base on SciInstruct yields SciGLM-6B with an average scientific benchmark score of 41.40 (vs. 38.12 for the base model, a +4.87% gain) and an average math score of 49.24 (vs. 42.56, a +6.68% gain). At 32B scale, SciGLM achieves 45.48 Avg. Sci and 57.46 Avg. Math, outperforming its base by +2.67% and +3.97% respectively. On general language tasks (MMLU, CEval), SciGLM maintains or slightly improves performance (e.g., MMLU 70.08 vs. 69.05 at 32B), confirming that scientific instruction tuning does not sacrifice general capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SciGLM-6B substantially outperforms Galactica-6.7B on CEval-Sci (60.00 vs. 11.44) and SciEval (62.09 vs. 50.87), and beats Llama3-8B-Instruct few-shot on Avg. Sci (41.40 vs. 34.66) and Mistral-7B:MetaMATH few-shot (41.40 vs. 28.76). At 32B scale, SciGLM surpasses Galactica-30B on SciEval (69.84 vs. 54.96) and CEval-Sci (66.19 vs. not reported). On math benchmarks, SciGLM-6B achieves 49.24 Avg. Math, exceeding MAmmoTH & MetaMath-7B (individual scores: GSM8K 66.30, MATH 24.10, SAT-Math 41.40) and MAmmoTH & MetaMath-13B (GSM8K 71.04, MATH 26.18, SAT-Math 48.18). However, SciGLM-32B still trails GPT-4 on SciEval (69.84 vs. 73.93), SciBench (5.15 vs. 28.52), and GPQA (25.76 vs. 39.70), indicating that the gap to frontier closed-source models remains significant on the hardest graduate-level questions. Notably, fine-tuning Llama3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B:MetaMATH on SciInstruct yields smaller gains (e.g., Llama3 drops from 34.66 to 34.54 Avg. Sci), suggesting the dataset is most effective when aligned with the ChatGLM3 architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-instruct and instruction-tuning lineage (Self-Instruct, Platypus, FLAN) to the scientific domain, where high-quality chain-of-thought reasoning traces are scarce. Methodologically, it builds on the self-correction frameworks of Reflexion and Reflection-tuning, adapting them into a three-stage critic-and-revise annotation pipeline that uses GPT-4 as both generator and outcome-reward-model judge. For formal proof data, it repurposes LeanDojo's theorem-proof corpus, and it positions itself as a more efficient alternative to continued pre-training approaches like Galactica and MINERVA, which require substantially more compute to inject scientific knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SciInstruct addresses a genuine bottleneck: the lack of large-scale, step-by-step annotated scientific reasoning data that is legally accessible and covers college-level physics, chemistry, math, and formal proofs. The self-reflective annotation framework is a reusable methodology that could be applied to other expert domains with similar data scarcity. However, the paper is limited by its reliance on GPT-4 for annotation (introducing potential bias), modest model scales (6–32B), and a dataset of ~254k samples; the authors themselves note that larger datasets and models are needed. Open questions include whether the instruction-quality classifier can be repurposed as a process reward model for RL-based training, and whether the cross-domain transfer effects (e.g., physics data improving math scores) persist at larger scales.
