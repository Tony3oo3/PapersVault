## Problem

Existing citation attribution benchmarks are automatically curated and dominated by ambiguous, unattributable, or trivial excerpts (only ~21% are 'reasonable'), making them poor proxies for real-world research assistance. Furthermore, no prior benchmark provides full paper text or an open-ended search space, and it remains unclear whether LMs can reliably link scientific claims to their sources. This paper asks: can an LM act as a research assistant to correctly identify a referenced paper from a text excerpt?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240252/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a short text excerpt (typically 1–3 sentences) from a published ML paper containing a single anonymized citation marker [CITATION], where the excerpt is guaranteed to be attributable, unambiguous, and non-trivial (no author names or title acronyms). The output is the exact title of the cited paper, selected from an open-ended corpus of ~218 million papers on Semantic Scholar. The agent is limited to 15 actions per instance and can only access paper metadata (title, abstract, citation count) via search and full text via the read command, which retrieves the open-access PDF.

## Method

The paper introduces two contributions: (1) CiteME, a 130-sample benchmark of human-curated, unambiguous citation excerpts from recent ML papers, filtered to exclude any instance that GPT-4o could answer without external tools; and (2) CiteAgent, a ReAct-style autonomous agent that wraps a frozen LM (GPT-4o, Claude 3 Opus, or LLaMA-3-70B) with three custom commands—search(query, sort), read(ID), and select(ID)—backed by the Semantic Scholar API. The agent follows a think-act loop: it reasons about the excerpt, issues a search or read command, observes the result, and iterates up to 15 steps before being forced to select a paper. The prompt includes a demonstration trajectory modeled on SWE-Agent, and the agent can sort results by relevance or citation count, read full paper text, and refine queries across multiple rounds. No additional training or fine-tuning is required; the system relies entirely on the LM's in-context reasoning and tool-use capabilities.

## Results

On CiteME, CiteAgent with GPT-4o (search and read commands, with demonstration) achieves 35.3% accuracy, substantially outperforming the retrieval baselines SPECTER2 and SPECTER, which both score 0%. Among LLM backbones, Claude 3 Opus reaches 27.7% and LLaMA-3-70B reaches 21.0%. Human evaluators achieve 69.7% accuracy in an average of 38.2 seconds per instance, leaving a 34.4-percentage-point gap between the best LM system and human performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Both retrieval baselines—SPECTER2 (Singh et al., EMNLP 2022) and SPECTER (Cohan et al., 2020)—achieve 0% accuracy on CiteME, indicating that embedding-based retrieval over the full Semantic Scholar corpus is entirely unable to resolve unambiguous citation references from short text excerpts. CiteAgent with GPT-4o dramatically outperforms both baselines, reaching 35.3% accuracy by iteratively searching and reading full papers via Semantic Scholar. However, even the best LM-based system (35.3%) falls well short of human performance (69.7%), a gap of 34.4 percentage points. The paper further shows that without any external tools (no commands, no demonstration), GPT-4o scores 0% on CiteME by design, since all instances were filtered to exclude papers it could answer from memorization alone, confirming that the gains come entirely from the agentic search-and-read loop rather than parametric knowledge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the ReAct think-act loop architecture and the SWE-Agent prompt design (with demonstration trajectories) to construct CiteAgent, an LM-based system for open-ended citation attribution. It departs from prior citation recommendation benchmarks (FullTextPeerRead, ACL-200, RefSeer, arXiv) by replacing automatically curated, often ambiguous excerpts with manually curated ones that unambiguously reference a single paper, and by replacing fixed-corpus retrieval with open-ended Semantic Scholar search. The retrieval baselines (SPECTER, SPECTER2) represent the prior state of the art in scientific document embedding, but the paper reframes the task from in-corpus kNN retrieval to an agentic search-and-read workflow more closely mirroring how researchers actually find cited papers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a substantial and previously unquantified gap: frontier LMs achieve only 4.2–18.5% accuracy (35.3% with the CiteAgent tool loop) versus 69.7% for humans, while the purpose-built SPECTER2 retrieval model scores 0%. This provides a concrete, challenging testbed for tracking progress on LM-based claim verification, a capability the authors argue is prerequisite for reducing hallucination in LM-generated scientific text. Open questions include whether the 34.4% remaining gap to humans can be closed with better reasoning or longer search horizons, whether the benchmark generalizes beyond machine learning, and whether the small size (130 samples) limits statistical power for fine-grained model comparisons.
