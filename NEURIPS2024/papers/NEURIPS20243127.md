## Problem

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) frequently fail to produce correct PDE solutions because the gradients of the PDE residual loss and boundary loss can be conflicting (negative inner product) and severely imbalanced in magnitude, causing one loss to increase while the other decreases. While prior work identified gradient imbalance heuristically, no principled geometric criterion existed to characterize exactly when adverse training occurs, nor a unified optimization framework that guarantees both losses decrease simultaneously at every step.

## Input / Output

The method takes a nonlinear PDE (differential operator N and boundary operator B), a domain Ω with boundary ∂Ω, randomly sampled collocation points in the interior, and boundary sample points as input. It produces a trained neural network u(x;θ) approximating the PDE solution, evaluated by relative L²-error against an analytic or finite-element reference solution. Unusually, the optimizer operates on the two component gradients ∇L_r and ∇L_b separately at each iteration rather than only their sum, requiring per-step computation of their angle and magnitude ratio to determine whether the total gradient lies inside the dual cone.

## Method

The paper defines a dual cone region K*_t at each iteration as the set of vectors having non-negative inner product with both ∇L_r and ∇L_b, and proves (Theorem 4.2) that the standard total gradient ∇L = ∇L_r + ∇L_b falls outside this region precisely when the gradients are conflicting and one dominates the other by a factor exceeding a threshold determined by the angle between them. To stay within the dual cone, DCGD restricts the update direction to a computable subspace G_t formed by conic combinations of the projections of ∇L onto the orthogonal complements of each individual gradient. Three concrete variants are proposed: DCGD (Projection) projects ∇L onto G_t when it is outside K*_t; DCGD (Average) takes the midpoint of the two projections; and DCGD (Center) projects ∇L onto the angle bisector of the two unit-normalized gradients, which empirically yields the most balanced descent. A convergence theorem guarantees that DCGD reaches a Pareto-stationary point (where no direction decreases both losses) or converges at O(1/√T) in the non-convex setting, and the framework unifies PCGrad, MGDA, and Nash-MTL as special cases.

## Results

DCGD (Center) achieves the lowest relative L² error across all five benchmark PDEs in Table 1. On the 2D Helmholtz equation, DCGD reaches 0.0029 (±0.0005), outperforming the next-best method LRA (0.0066) by more than 2×. On the challenging 3D Helmholtz equation, DCGD achieves 0.0774 versus 0.0831 for LRA and 0.6109 for ADAM. On the 5D Heat equation, DCGD attains 0.0008, the best result among all tested optimizers. For complex PDEs where vanilla PINNs fail (Table 2), DCGD reduces the convection equation error from 0.4880 to 0.0246 and the Volterra IDE error from 0.0068 to 0.0011.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCGD (Center) consistently outperforms all 8 competing optimizers on the five benchmark PDEs. Against ADAM, DCGD achieves roughly 21× lower error on Helmholtz (0.0029 vs 0.0609) and 8× lower on 3D Helmholtz (0.0774 vs 0.6109). Against the strongest baseline LRA, DCGD wins on Helmholtz (0.0029 vs 0.0066), Burgers' (0.0124 vs 0.0180), 5D Heat (0.0008 vs 0.0052), and 3D Helmholtz (0.0774 vs 0.0831), while tying on Klein-Gordon (both 0.0069). MTL-based methods (PCGrad, MGDA, CAGrad, Aligned-MTL) are substantially worse: MGDA and Aligned-MTL fail to converge on most equations (errors >0.5), while PCGrad and CAGrad trail DCGD by 2–5× on the 2D benchmarks. On complex PDEs where vanilla PINNs fail, DCGD reduces the convection equation error by 20× (0.0246 vs 0.4880 for PINNsFormer) and the Volterra IDE error by 6× (0.0011 vs 0.0068 for A-PINN). Combining DCGD with LRA or NTK further improves results on most equations (e.g., DCGD+LRA reaches 0.0023 on Helmholtz and 0.0050 on Klein-Gordon), confirming compatibility with existing loss-balancing strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the PINN framework of Raissi et al. [1] and reframes its training as a multi-objective optimization problem, connecting it to the broader multi-task learning (MTL) literature (PCGrad, MGDA, CAGrad, Nash-MTL, Aligned-MTL). Prior PINN-specific optimization work—learning rate annealing [30], NTK-based balancing [31], MultiAdam [34], and DPM [35]—addressed gradient imbalance heuristically, while MTL algorithms handled gradient conflict through ad-hoc projections or bargaining solutions. DCGD departs from both lines by providing a unified geometric characterization (the dual cone) that subsumes several MTL algorithms as special cases and yields a principled convergence guarantee to Pareto-stationary points in the non-convex setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clear geometric criterion (Theorem 4.2) for when PINNs are adversely trained—combining angle and magnitude ratio of the two loss gradients—gives practitioners a diagnostic tool and a principled fix that is architecture- and PDE-agnostic. The convergence guarantee to Pareto-stationary points (rather than merely stationary points) is a meaningful theoretical advance for multi-objective non-convex optimization. Open questions include designing more powerful DCGD specifications beyond projection/average/center, extending the framework to more than two loss terms, and understanding whether the dual cone geometry generalizes to the more complex multi-task settings beyond the two-loss PINN structure.
