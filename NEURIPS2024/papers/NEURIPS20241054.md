## Problem

Despite LLMs being deployed in medical settings, there is no formal definition of medical safety for LLMs, no benchmark to measure it, and no demonstrated method to improve it. Existing safety evaluation benchmarks target general harm (illegal activity, violence, fraud) and do not capture domain-specific concerns such as patient confidentiality, concealment of medical errors, or violations of physician-patient ethical obligations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241054/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For evaluation, the benchmark feeds harmful medical requests (text prompts spanning 9 AMA ethical principles, with or without medical jargon) to an LLM and scores the response's harmfulness on a 1–5 scale using GPT-3.5 as a judge, where 1 indicates full refusal and 5 indicates full compliance. For improvement, the benchmark provides 900 safety demonstrations—each pairing a harmful request with a GPT-4-generated refusal and brief ethical justification—which serve as instruction-tuning data. The 1,800 total demonstrations are split evenly into MedSafety-Eval (450 GPT-4-generated + 450 Llama-2-generated requests) and MedSafety-Improve (same composition), ensuring the evaluation set is held out from fine-tuning.

## Method

The paper defines medical safety as alignment with the AMA's nine Principles of Medical Ethics and operationalizes this by generating 1,800 harmful medical requests: 900 via direct GPT-4 prompting (rich in medical jargon) and 900 via Greedy Coordinate Gradient adversarial jailbreaking of Llama-2-7b-chat (less jargon-heavy), then pairing each with a GPT-4-generated safe refusal. Safety is measured using a GPT-3.5-judged harmfulness score (1–5) that rates the extent of compliance with a harmful request against a usage policy derived from the AMA principles. To improve safety, the authors fine-tune medical LLMs (Medalpaca-7b/13b, Meditron-7b) using LoRA (r=4, alpha=16, 3 epochs, learning rate 1e-4) on MedSafety-Improve, GenSafety-Improve, or both, showing that instruction-tuning on safety demonstrations significantly reduces harmfulness scores across all evaluation sets while preserving medical performance on MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, and MMLU-Medical. Dataset validity is confirmed through a user study with 25 practicing physicians who verified that the harmful requests violate the intended ethical principles.

## Results

Medical LLMs (Medalpaca, Meditron, ClinicalCamel, Med42) achieve average harmfulness scores of 1.78–3.78 on GenSafety-Eval, 1.90–2.98 on MedSafety-Eval-GPT4, and 1.36–2.11 on MedSafety-Eval-Llama2 (lower is safer; 1 = full refusal, 5 = full compliance). All medical LLMs are significantly less safe than their safety-aligned general-knowledge counterparts (Llama-2-chat) on all three evaluation datasets, with effect sizes ranging from −0.35 to −2.72 (Wilcoxon signed-rank, all p < 10⁻¹⁴ after Bonferroni correction). Fine-tuning on MedSafety-Improve (n=900) or BothSafety-Improve (n=1,800) reduces harmfulness scores by 0.55–2.55 points across models and datasets, while preserving medical performance on MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, and MMLU-Medical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 14 LLMs across three safety benchmarks. All six medical LLMs (Medalpaca-7b/13b, Meditron-7b/70b, ClinicalCamel-70b, Med42-70b) are significantly less safe than their safety-aligned general-knowledge counterparts (Llama-2-chat at matching parameter sizes), with the largest gaps seen for Medalpaca-7b (effect size −1.66 on MedSafety-Eval-GPT4) and Meditron-7b (−1.97). Among general-knowledge models, safety-aligned variants (Llama-2-chat, Llama-3.1-instruct, Mistral-instruct, Mixtral-instruct, GPT-3.5/4/4o) consistently outperform their non-aligned counterparts (Llama, Llama-2 base, Vicuna, Pythia, Mistral base, Mixtral base). After fine-tuning on MedSafety-Improve or BothSafety-Improve, Medalpaca-7b, Medalpaca-13b, and Meditron-7b all show statistically significant safety improvements (effect sizes −0.55 to −2.55) on all three evaluation datasets, with BothSafety-Improve (n=1,800) yielding the largest gains. Fine-tuning on medical safety alone also improves general safety and vice versa, indicating cross-domain transfer. Medical performance (MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, MMLU-Medical) is preserved after safety fine-tuning, confirming no safety–utility trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM safety evaluation paradigm (established by Qi et al. for general safety via harmfulness scoring and GenSafety-Eval, and by Bianchi et al. for safety-tuned Llamas) to the medical domain, where no prior formal definition or benchmark existed. It repurposes the Greedy Coordinate Gradient jailbreaking technique from Zou et al. not as an attack on deployed models but as a data-generation tool to elicit harmful medical prompts from Llama-2-7b-chat. The safety-alignment component follows the instruction-tuning/LoRA recipe from Qi et al. and Bianchi et al., but applies it to medical LLMs (Medalpaca, Meditron) using a newly curated medical safety demonstration set grounded in the AMA's nine Principles of Medical Ethics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs are increasingly deployed in clinical and administrative medical settings, the absence of a domain-specific safety benchmark left a critical gap in evaluating whether these systems can be trusted. The finding that purpose-built medical LLMs are significantly less safe than their general-knowledge safety-aligned counterparts underscores that medical-domain fine-tuning does not inherit safety guardrails. Open questions include whether safety definitions should be nuanced across subspecialties and patient contexts, whether RLHF with domain-expert feedback can capture more granular safety standards, and whether the GPT-3.5-as-judge evaluation methodology generalizes reliably. The benchmark's modest size (1,800 examples) and reliance on a single LLM judge are limitations that future work should address.
