## Problem

Deep neural networks trained with cross-entropy loss suffer from overconfidence, producing high-confidence incorrect predictions that undermine failure detection. While LogitNorm addresses this by fixing logit magnitude, the paper identifies a remaining gap: atypical samples whose labels poorly describe the image content still force the model to overfit logit directions, producing near-1.0 softmax scores on ambiguous inputs. No prior method differentiates the optimization of typical versus atypical samples during training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR, 224×224 for ImageNet) through any backbone (ResNet, WRNet, DenseNet, or DeiT-Small). During training, it additionally computes per-channel feature mean and variance for each sample and compares them against a FIFO Historical Feature Queue of 20,000 entries. At inference, the confidence score is the cosine similarity between the predicted logit direction and the one-hot label direction (not the standard maximum softmax probability), which is then thresholded to produce an accept/reject decision.

## Method

TAL introduces a typicalness score τ ∈ [0,1] computed by taking the minimum L2 distance between a sample's feature statistics (mean μ, variance σ²) and those stored in a Historical Feature Queue (HFQ) initialized from correctly predicted samples after 5% of training epochs, then min-max normalizing within the batch. This τ drives a dynamic logit magnitude T(τ) = T_min + (1−τ)(T_max − T_min) (with T_min=10, T_max=100), so typical samples receive a small T that strongly optimizes direction alignment, while atypical samples receive a large T that relaxes the directional pull. The overall training loss is τ·L_TAL + (1−τ)·L_CE, where L_TAL is a LogitNorm-style loss with the dynamic magnitude and L_CE is standard cross-entropy, ensuring atypical samples still receive some gradient signal on both magnitude and direction but with weaker directional force. At inference, no typicalness computation is needed; the model simply outputs cosine-similarity-based confidence, making the approach model-agnostic and drop-in compatible with existing architectures.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 (New FD setting, ResNet-110), TAL achieves an AURC of 347.72 and FPR95 of 54.39, outperforming all individual baselines. When combined with FMFP, TAL w/ FMFP reduces AURC to 320.73, a >7% improvement over FMFP alone (345.37), confirming the paper's headline claim of >5% AURC improvement over the prior SOTA. On ImageNet (New FD, ResNet-50), TAL reduces AURC by 3.7 points vs. MSP (338.45 vs. 351.26) on Textures OOD and by 11.6 points on WILDS OOD (288.67 vs. 326.85), while maintaining comparable accuracy (76.43% vs. 76.13%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAL outperforms all standard confidence-based baselines (MSP, Cosine, Energy, MaxLogit, Entropy, Mahalanobis, SIRC) on the New FD AURC metric across all three CNN architectures, with the largest margin over OpenMix (347.72 vs. 406.80 AURC on ResNet-110) and Gradnorm (347.72 vs. 679.48). Against LogitNorm, TAL improves AURC from 356.88 to 347.72 while also achieving higher accuracy (72.45% vs. 70.34%), confirming that dynamic magnitude assignment is superior to a fixed constant. Compared to the prior SOTA FMFP, TAL alone is marginally worse on AURC (347.72 vs. 345.37) but substantially better on FPR95 (54.39 vs. 62.99) and AUROC (87.89 vs. 84.86); the combination TAL w/ FMFP dominates on all three metrics (AURC 320.73, FPR95 55.22, AUROC 88.48), demonstrating that the two methods are complementary. On ImageNet, TAL consistently improves over MSP by 3.7–11.6 AURC points depending on the OOD test set, while SIRC provides only marginal additional gains when stacked on top of TAL (338.45 → 336.56 AURC). The paper notes that OOD-detection methods (Energy, LogitNorm) perform poorly in the FD task because they disrupt the confidence hierarchy within in-distribution samples, a finding supported by the consistently higher AURC values of these methods in the Old FD setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends LogitNorm [40], which identified that cross-entropy loss drives logit magnitude growth and proposed a fixed-magnitude decoupling to mitigate overconfidence. TAL departs from LogitNorm's constant magnitude by introducing a dynamic magnitude T(τ) that depends on a per-sample 'typicalness' score computed via mean/variance statistics of feature channels compared against a Historical Feature Queue (HFQ). The typicalness concept draws on prior observations about atypical samples [44, 51] and the CORES [31] insight that in-distribution samples exhibit larger feature magnitudes and variances. The method is positioned as complementary to existing failure detection approaches like FMFP [49] and SIRC [41], rather than a replacement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The insight that overconfidence partly stems from forcing models to fit atypical samples whose labels poorly describe image content provides a concrete, actionable diagnostic for a long-standing reliability problem in safety-critical applications. The method's simplicity (a FIFO queue of feature statistics plus a linear magnitude schedule) makes it easy to adopt across architectures, and its demonstrated complementarity with FMFP suggests it can be layered onto existing pipelines. However, the typicalness measure is limited to per-channel mean/variance, the HFQ initialization requires a pre-trained model, and the dynamic magnitude bounds (T_min=10, T_max=100) are set empirically without theoretical justification. Open questions include whether richer typicalness estimators (e.g., density-based or learned) would further improve performance, and whether the approach transfers to non-visual modalities or open-vocabulary settings.
