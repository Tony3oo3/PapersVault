## Problem

Frozen LLMs serve as the backbone of Large Multimodal Models, yet the mechanism by which they generalize to non-textual inputs (image, video, audio) without any multimodal finetuning remains poorly understood. It is unclear whether perceptual tokens are simply translated into textual equivalents or whether the LLM architecture itself facilitates cross-modal processing. This paper investigates the internal representations of frozen LLMs when exposed to multimodal inputs to explain this generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244158/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes multimodal inputs (images, video clips, audio clips) encoded by frozen modality-specific encoders (ViT, TimeSformer, AST) and projected into LLM input space via a lightweight trainable mapping module, concatenated with textual token embeddings. The frozen LLM (e.g., Vicuna-v1.5-7B) processes the combined sequence, and the paper extracts internal token representations at every LLM block for analysis. Beyond standard text generation outputs (captions, VQA answers), the method produces IMA alignment scores (cosine similarity between perceptual and textual token distributions at each layer) and a modality-agnostic pruned subnetwork (α-SubNet) obtained by intersecting Wanda-derived pruning masks across modalities.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic empirical analysis of token representations inside frozen LLMs across two setups: single-task (ST) training on individual image/video/audio datasets and multitask (MT) training on LLaVA-1.5 variants. Three key findings emerge: (1) perceptual and textual tokens occupy distinct narrow cones with different norms, change rates, and vocabulary distributions; (2) they activate highly overlapping LLM weight subnetworks (IoU ~0.65–0.69); and (3) an Implicit Multimodal Alignment (IMA) effect pulls perceptual and textual tokens closer during both mapping-module training and inference, with the strongest alignment occurring after self-attention layers inside each block. The authors attribute this to the residual-stream architecture, where refinement blocks act as steering blocks that gradually align the narrow cones. Practical implications include using IMA score as a proxy metric for task performance and hallucination, skipping FFN computations for visual tokens (which change minimally across layers), and compressing the LLM to a single α-SubNet by intersecting modality-specific Wanda pruning masks, achieving comparable performance to task-specific pruning at ~50% sparsity.

## Results

The paper is primarily an empirical analysis of frozen LLMs exposed to multimodal inputs. Key quantitative findings include: (1) high overlap between modality-activated subnetworks, with IoU scores of 0.69 (GQA vs VQAv2), 0.67 (GQA vs Audiocaps), and 0.65 (COCO-Text) at 30% sparsity; (2) cross-modal token similarity inside LLM blocks reaches 0.45 (Vicuna-v1.5) and 0.58 (LLaVA-1.5-4), versus only 0.1 and 0.15 in the residual stream, confirming the Implicit Multimodal Alignment (IMA) effect; (3) the α-SubNet (intersection of task-specific Wanda masks at 30% sparsity, yielding ~50% global sparsity) achieves performance comparable to task-specific Wanda pruning, especially on VQAv2; (4) skipping visual token computations in FFN layers from a given start layer causes only slight performance degradation while significantly reducing FLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary method comparison is the α-SubNet compression approach against task-specific Wanda pruning [39], magnitude pruning, and random masking. The α-SubNet, obtained by intersecting Wanda-derived pruning masks across multiple modalities (at 30% per-task sparsity, yielding ~50% global sparsity), is significantly better than magnitude pruning and random masking baselines. It achieves performance comparable to task-specific Wanda pruning, with the closest match on VQAv2 and slight degradation on COCO captioning. Additionally, the paper demonstrates that individual task-specific Wanda masks transfer well across tasks within the same modality (e.g., ~1 point CIDEr degradation when using an OKVQA mask for COCO captioning) and across modalities (image→video→audio), with somewhat larger degradation for OPT and Llama 2 compared to Vicuna-v1.5. This validates the core finding that different modalities activate highly overlapping LLM weights, making a single modality-agnostic subnetwork viable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the efficient multimodal model paradigm (freezing LLMs and training only a lightweight connector, as in LLaVA-1.5 and EP-ALM) by asking *why* this works rather than proposing yet another connector. It draws on the anisotropy/narrow-cone literature for LLM embeddings and the Wanda pruning framework for subnetwork extraction, but repurposes both as diagnostic tools to characterize how perceptual and textual tokens coexist inside a frozen transformer. The key conceptual departure is from the 'foreign language' hypothesis (image tokens are simply translated to text tokens) and from the 'modality-specific subnetwork' hypothesis, showing instead that the residual-stream architecture itself acts as a set of steering blocks that implicitly align disparate token distributions without any explicit alignment objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The IMA score as a proxy for both task performance and hallucination rate offers practitioners a cheap diagnostic that could guide model selection and safety evaluation without running full benchmarks. The finding that hallucinations stem from insufficient internal alignment reframes a widely-studied problem from a data/training issue to a representational-geometry issue, potentially opening new mitigation strategies. However, the analysis is confined to ≤7B frozen LLMs with concatenated-token architectures; whether the IMA effect persists (or changes character) in larger, end-to-end-trained, or interleaved multimodal models like Gemini or GPT-4V remains an open question that limits the generality of the conclusions.
