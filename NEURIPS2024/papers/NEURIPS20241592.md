## Problem

There is no standardized benchmark for evaluating out-of-distribution generalization and adaptation of EMG gesture classifiers, making it difficult to compare results across papers or assess real-world readiness. Prior work relies on randomized train-test splits or k-fold cross-validation that mix data from the same subject, inflating accuracy and failing to capture the distribution shifts (inter-subject anatomy, electrode placement, session-to-session drift) that arise in actual deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241592/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Raw multi-channel EMG time-series signals (4–256 electrodes, 200–4000 Hz sampling) are windowed into 250 ms segments and converted into 224×224 2D activity maps via one of four preprocessing methods: raw heatmap, RMS-window heatmap, short-time Fourier transform spectrogram, or continuous wavelet transform. The model outputs a discrete gesture class label (6–10 classes depending on dataset). A key constraint is that the 224×224 resolution forces downsampling for high-density arrays (128 or 256 electrodes), which the authors identify as a limitation for preserving spatial detail.

## Method

The benchmark standardizes a two-stage training pipeline: (1) pretraining an ImageNet-initialized image classifier (ResNet18, EfficientNet, ViT, or EfficientViT) on all subjects except the held-out test subject, and (2) optional few-shot fine-tuning on the first X% (5–80%) of the test subject's data to measure adaptation. Generalization is assessed via leave-one-subject-out cross-validation (LOSO-CV), while adaptation is assessed via train-test splits for time series (TSTS) and intersession fine-tuning. The framework spans nine EMG datasets with varying electrode counts, sampling rates, and subject populations, and additionally tests domain-generalization training objectives (IRM, CORAL) against standard cross-entropy loss. A new 13-subject, 64-electrode FlexWear-HD dataset collected with a reusable FPCB-based wearable is introduced to provide an easy-to-don high-density option.

## Results

Across 9 EMG datasets, the best LOSO-CV (leave-one-subject-out) accuracy before fine-tuning ranges from 11.1% (Ninapro DB3) to 83.9% (FlexWear-HD, EfficientNet). After fine-tuning with just 20% of a new subject's data, accuracy improves dramatically: FlexWear-HD reaches 96.9% (EfficientNet), Myo Dataset 94.9%, and UCI EMG 91.7%. With only 5% of a subject's data (~8 s for Myo, ~30 s for FlexWear-HD), the benchmark achieves 93.2% and 94.6% accuracy respectively, representing an average 44% absolute improvement over the pretrained model. The best-performing architecture varies by dataset: EfficientNet leads on 4 of 9 datasets, ResNet18 on 3, and EfficientViT on 2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four ImageNet-pretrained architectures (ResNet18, EfficientNet, ViT/TinyViT, EfficientViT) across all nine EMG datasets. EfficientNet [57] achieves the best fine-tuned accuracy on 4 of 9 datasets (CapgMyo 89.7%, UCI 91.7%, Ninapro DB3 45.1%, FlexWear-HD 96.9%) and the highest pretraining accuracy on FlexWear-HD (83.9%). ResNet18 [55] leads on Ninapro DB5 (68.5% fine-tuned), Ninapro DB2 (52.4%), and Hyser (87.4%), while EfficientViT [58] is best on Myo Dataset (94.9%) and Ninapro DB5 fine-tuning (69.2%). ViT/TinyViT [56] is consistently competitive but never the top performer, and shows the largest drop on Hyser after fine-tuning (70.6% vs. 87.4% for ResNet18). Domain generalization algorithms IRM [62] and CORAL [63] yield performance comparable to standard cross-entropy training, confirming that the distribution shift in EMG is not easily addressed by these methods alone. The key finding is that no single architecture dominates across all datasets, and the choice of preprocessing method (raw heatmap, RMS, STFT, CWT) interacts with architecture to determine best performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground as the first standardized benchmark for out-of-distribution generalization and adaptation in EMG gesture classification, unifying nine previously disparate datasets under a common evaluation protocol (LOSO-CV and train-test splits for time series). It builds on the established practice of converting EMG time-series into 2D representations (heatmaps, spectrograms, CWTs) and classifying them with ImageNet-pretrained CNNs and ViTs, as demonstrated by Ozdemir et al. and Dere and Lee, but departs from prior work by systematically comparing preprocessing methods, architectures, and adaptation strategies across all datasets simultaneously. The paper also extends the domain generalization literature (IRM, CORAL) into the EMG setting, and introduces a new high-density wearable EMG dataset (FlexWear-HD) that enables evaluation of a practical, easy-to-don sensor configuration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark addresses a critical gap in the EMG research community where inconsistent data splits and evaluation protocols made cross-paper comparisons unreliable, potentially inflating reported accuracies. The finding that only ~8–30 seconds of subject-specific data (5% fine-tuning) yields 93–94% accuracy has direct practical implications for reducing calibration time in prosthetic and assistive technology deployment. Key open questions remain: whether higher-resolution activity maps (>224×224) would improve generalization for high-electrode-count datasets, whether a truly universal EMG classifier is achievable, and how performance holds during spontaneous real-world activities rather than cue-based laboratory settings. The limited use of a single seed for most experiments (due to ~10,000 GPU hours of compute) and the absence of transition data in several datasets are notable limitations.
