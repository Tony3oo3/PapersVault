## Problem

Music recommendation systems exhibit severe popularity bias, concentrating 80% of recommendations among just 10% of artists and leaving long-tail songs largely invisible. While adversarial data poisoning and individual strategic manipulation have been studied, the paper addresses the unexplored question of how small collectives of platform users can achieve disproportionate influence on transformer-based recommenders through coordinated, authentic modifications to the playlists they own—without requiring knowledge of model architecture or weights.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243783/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The collective takes as input a target song s*, the subset of playlists they own (a fraction α of training data), and song frequency statistics gathered either internally from their own playlists (InClust) or from public APIs and scraped stream counts (DirLoF). The output is a set of modified playlists where s* is inserted at a strategically chosen position in each playlist, subject to an authenticity constraint limiting the Levenshtein edit distance to at most 1 (a single insertion per playlist). Effectiveness is measured as amplification—the ratio of newly gained test-time recommendations to the fraction of manipulated training playlists—where a value above 1 indicates disproportionate influence relative to training frequency.

## Method

The paper proposes two coordination strategies that exploit statistical properties of transformer-based sequential recommenders without requiring access to model weights. InClust places s* before the most frequent song in the collective's playlists, concentrating effort to push s* into the top-K for contexts near that song's embedding cluster; DirLoF places s* after the least frequent song (identified via external frequency data), exploiting the long-tail distribution so that a single insertion can overpower weak existing signals for low-frequency contexts. Both strategies leverage the discontinuity at the top-K boundary—being ranked K+1 yields zero recommendations—by concentrating probability mass on specific contexts rather than spreading it uniformly. A hybrid strategy interleaves both approaches, and the paper demonstrates that even collectives controlling as few as 60 playlists (0.001% of training data) can achieve 25× amplification, equivalent to 40× the recommendations of a naturally occurring song at the same training frequency, while largely preserving recommendations for other songs and artists.

## Results

The DirLoF strategy achieves an amplification of up to 25× for tiny collectives (α=0.025%, ~60 playlists), meaning the target song receives 25× more test-time recommendations than its training frequency would predict. This corresponds to 40× more recommendations than an average song occurring at the same training frequency, since low-frequency songs are typically ignored by the recommender. For larger collectives (α≈2%), the InClust strategy reaches an amplification of 10, while the Random baseline only achieves ~4×. Fixed-position baselines (Insert@i, AtTheEnd) are essentially ineffective with amplification near 0 across all collective sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two coordinated collective strategies (DirLoF and InClust) against four uncoordinated baselines: Random insertion, fixed-position Insert@i, AtTheEnd, and Random@i-j. DirLoF dominates all baselines by a wide margin, achieving 16.4× amplification at α=0.001 versus only 3.4× for Random and ≈0 for all fixed-position strategies. The key finding is that strategic positioning (choosing *where* to insert the song) matters far more than simply inserting it: Random insertion at any position yields ~3-4× amplification, while DirLoF's targeted low-frequency anchor placement achieves 5× more. InClust becomes competitive only at larger collective sizes (α≥0.1%), where it can target multiple context clusters simultaneously, eventually outperforming DirLoF. The hybrid strategy, which combines both approaches, provides the best overall performance across the full range of collective sizes. All strategies satisfy the authenticity constraint (Levenshtein distance ≤ 1), unlike prior shilling or poisoning attacks that make larger playlist modifications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic collective action framework of Hardt et al. (ICML 2023) from tabular prediction tasks to sequential, transformer-based recommender systems, specifically automatic playlist continuation. It deliberately departs from the shilling attack and data poisoning literature by imposing an authenticity constraint (Levenshtein distance ≤ 1) and requiring no knowledge of model architecture or weights, instead relying only on aggregate song frequency statistics. The strategies (InClust and DirLoF) are derived from a statistical optimality assumption about how transformer attention functions encode sequential patterns, positioning the work at the intersection of collective action theory and the practical mechanics of sequential recommendation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that collectives controlling fewer than 60 playlists can achieve 40× the recommendation frequency of naturally occurring songs at the same training prevalence raises urgent questions about platform governance and the feasibility of user-side countermeasures against popularity bias. The paper's demonstration that this is achievable while largely preserving service quality and user experience complicates the platform's incentive to detect and block such strategies. Open questions include whether the strategies generalize beyond APC to other sequential recommendation tasks, how to design mechanisms that incentivize fair collective action while disincentivizing malicious use by well-resourced actors, and what long-term equilibria emerge when multiple collectives compete.
