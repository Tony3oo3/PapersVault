## Problem

Deep Transformers suffer from oversmoothing: the self-attention mechanism acts as a pure low-pass graph filter, progressively stripping high-frequency information so that token representations converge to indistinguishable values in deeper layers. While prior work identified this issue, no method redesigned the self-attention itself from a graph signal processing perspective with minimal computational overhead, leaving a gap between the theoretical understanding of attention as a graph filter and a practical, general-purpose fix.

## Input / Output

GFSA is a drop-in replacement for the standard self-attention layer, consuming the same token embeddings (n×d matrix) and producing the same-shaped output, so it integrates into any Transformer backbone without architectural changes. The only added parameters are three scalar coefficients (w_0, w_1, w_K) per attention head per layer—tens to hundreds in total—and the only extra computation is one additional matrix multiplication to form A̅². The hyperparameter K (typically 2–10) controls the effective order of the high-order term and is tuned per task.

## Method

The paper reinterprets the standard self-attention matrix A̅ as the simplest possible graph filter (H = A̅) and enriches it to a three-term matrix polynomial H_GFSA = w_0·I + w_1·A̅ + w_K·A̅^K, where the coefficients are learned via gradient descent. To avoid the O(n³ log K) cost of computing A̅^K directly, the authors approximate it with a first-order Taylor expansion at a=1 combined with a forward finite difference, yielding A̅ + (K−1)(A̅² − A̅), which requires only one extra matrix product. Theorem 3.1 proves that positive coefficients with unit sum produce a low-pass filter while alternating-sign coefficients (w_k = (−α)^k) produce a high-pass filter, so the learned coefficients let the network adaptively balance smoothing and high-frequency preservation across layers. The method is validated across six domains—NLU, causal LM, image classification, graph regression, speech recognition, and code classification—consistently improving accuracy with negligible parameter overhead, and a selective even-layer application strategy further cuts runtime cost by ~27%.

## Results

GFSA improves Transformer performance across six domains with only tens to hundreds of additional parameters. On GLUE, GFSA raises BERT's average from 82.51 to 83.58, ALBERT from 84.01 to 85.05, and RoBERTa from 85.87 to 86.79. On ImageNet-1k, DeiT-S (12-layer) top-1 accuracy increases from 79.8% to 81.1% (+1.3%), and Swin-S from 82.9% to 83.0%. On LibriSpeech 100h, Transformer+GFSA reduces test-clean WER from 11.02 to 10.30 (−6.53%), and on PCQM4M, Graphormer+GFSA reduces validation MAE from 0.1286 to 0.1193 (−7.20%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On GLUE, GFSA outperforms ContraNorm [28] on average across all three backbones (BERT: 83.58 vs. 83.48; ALBERT: 85.05 vs. 84.69; RoBERTa: 86.79 vs. 86.61), with the largest single-task gain on RoBERTa+CoLA (+3.77% over base). On ImageNet-1k (12-layer DeiT-S), GFSA (81.1%) surpasses AttnScale [80] (80.7%), FeatScale [80] (80.9%), HAT [4] (80.9%), ContraNorm [28] (80.4%), and LayerScale/LateInsertion [75] (80.5%), though it trails DiversePatch [25] (82.2%) on the 24-layer setting. For graph-level tasks, GFSA consistently improves over Graphormer [94] (e.g., PCQM4M validation MAE 0.1193 vs. 0.1286), GPS [63], and Graph-ViT [30] across Peptide-func, ZINC, and MNIST. On LibriSpeech 100h, Transformer+GFSA achieves 10.30 WER (test-clean), beating the vanilla Transformer (11.02) by 6.53%, while Branchformer+GFSA [59] reaches 2.11/4.94 on 960h test-clean/other, improving over Branchformer alone (2.13/5.00). On code classification (Devign), RoBERTa+GFSA achieves 64.39% accuracy (+2.40% over base), and CodeT5-small+GFSA (63.69%) surpasses CodeT5-base (63.51%) with only ~100 additional parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Shi et al.'s (ICLR 2022) observation that the self-attention matrix is a symmetrically normalized adjacency matrix, and extends it by formally treating self-attention as the simplest graph filter (H = A̅) within the directed-graph signal processing framework of Marques et al. (2020). It departs from prior oversmoothing remedies—Fourier-domain rescaling (Wang et al., 2022), adversarial high-frequency training (Bai et al., 2022), contrastive normalization (Guo et al., 2023), and JKNet-style layer fusion (Shi et al., 2022)—by proposing the minimal next-step filter H = w₀I + w₁A̅ + w_K·A̅^K, where the high-order term is approximated via a first-order Taylor expansion to keep overhead at tens of parameters. The authors explicitly note that several recent GCN filters (ChebNet, GPR-GNN, GREAD) are special cases of their formulation, positioning GFSA as a unifying and generalizable design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GFSA offers a drop-in, parameter-light (144 params for BERT, <72 for DeiT) upgrade that consistently improves performance across six domains—NLU, causal LM, vision, graphs, speech, and code—by injecting high-frequency information that vanilla self-attention discards. The selective even-layer strategy and linear-attention integration (11.8× faster than vanilla GFSA) address the main practical limitation of added runtime. Open questions include whether richer polynomial filters (beyond three terms) yield further gains, how GFSA interacts with very large-scale pre-training (GPT-4, LLaMA-class models), and whether the Taylor approximation's error bound (2√n·K) becomes tight enough to matter at extreme sequence lengths.
