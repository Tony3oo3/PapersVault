## Problem

While several methods exist for editing language models to correct hallucinations (ROME, MEMIT, GRACE, Transformer-Patcher), no theoretical understanding existed for what determines their success or selectivity, and no guarantee could be made that individual edits would not degrade overall model performance. Additionally, the vulnerability of redistributed foundation models to hyper-specific, undetectable weight modifications (stealth attacks) had not been identified or quantified.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained language model (transformer or selective state space architecture), a specific hallucinating prompt, and a desired corrected response, and produces edited model weights by modifying a single row of W₁ and a single column of W₂ in one chosen block, or by inserting a new jet-pack block. No retraining, fine-tuning, or access to training data is required; the edit is computed from a few forward passes and a short gradient descent on the output vector. The edited model produces the corrected response to the target prompt while leaving all other responses unchanged, with the degree of selectivity governed by the intrinsic dimensionality of the model's feature space at the edited layer.

## Method

The paper's central theoretical insight is that a single metric—the separability-based intrinsic dimensionality of the model's latent feature vectors—provably determines both editability (Theorem 2) and vulnerability to stealth attacks (Theorem 3), with false-detection probability decreasing exponentially in this dimension. For in-place edits, a linear separator is encoded into the least-important neuron of W₁ to detect the trigger prompt's feature direction on the unit sphere (exploiting the fact that RMSNorm/LayerNorm project features to a sphere surface), and the corresponding W₂ column is replaced with a vector found via gradient descent that produces the corrected output. The paper introduces a new jet-pack block J(x) = x + W₂σ(W₁ρ(x) + b) with a re-centred normalisation ρ(x) = (x−μ)/‖x−μ‖ that maximises intrinsic dimensionality, achieving hyper-selective multi-edit correction using only standard operations (matrix-vector products, ReLU, RMSNorm). The framework unifies GRACE and Transformer-Patcher as special cases, and reveals that the same mechanism enabling selective editing also enables cheap, undetectable stealth attacks requiring only a few inference runs on a laptop.

## Results

For 10,000 simultaneous jet-pack edits on MCF, the method achieves near-perfect edit success rates (0.9983 for Llama-3-8B, 1.0 for GPT-J-6B and Mamba-1.4B) with perplexity ratios of 1.0000–1.0012, indicating negligible impact on other model behaviour. Detector false positive rates on Wikipedia prompts are 0 across all three models, and on other MCF prompts range from 0.0002 to 0.0008. Cross-domain evaluation on Pile-10k (up to 10,000 prompts) confirms FPR of 0 for Llama and GPT-J, and 1.55×10⁻⁴ for Mamba. Standard benchmarks (TinyBenchmarks, MMLU-Pro) show at most a 0.46 pp drop in tinyARC accuracy, identical to the effect of simply zeroing a single neuron.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ROME, stealth in-place edits achieve lower perplexity ratios across 1,000 MCF edits, indicating less disruption to the model's original behaviour; ROME's linear edit mechanism pollutes the latent representation of every input, whereas stealth edits are gated by a detector neuron that is inactive for non-trigger prompts. Against GRACE, the jet-pack block achieves similar FPR rates and perplexity ratios in practice, but operates in a feature space with higher intrinsic dimension (16.7 vs. 13.9 for GPT-J-6B layer 18), providing stronger theoretical selectivity guarantees. GRACE's test retention rate degrades noticeably at 1,000 edits when prompts are related, while the jet-pack's ReLU-based architecture and optimised normalisation maintain selectivity. The paper also shows that the jet-pack can be viewed as a more optimised variant of GRACE, unifying the two approaches under the intrinsic-dimension framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies several previously disparate model-editing approaches—ROME/MEMIT (memory editing), Transformer-Patcher, Knowledge Neurons, and GRACE—under a single theoretical framework governed by the intrinsic dimensionality of a model's feature vectors, a concept originally introduced for binary classification in [32]. It departs from prior editing work by providing the first provable worst-case guarantees on edit selectivity (Theorems 2 and 3) and by revealing that the same metric that makes a model editable also quantifies its vulnerability to a newly identified class of stealth attacks. The jet-pack block it introduces is architecturally related to GRACE's key-value adaptor but is explicitly optimised via the intrinsic-dimensionality metric, and the paper shows GRACE can be re-expressed as a variant of the jet-pack, bridging the two families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical security implication is significant: any redistributed foundation model (Llama, GPT, Mamba, etc.) can be compromised with a few inference runs on a laptop, without access to training data or backpropagation, and the resulting trigger is extremely difficult to detect through conventional testing. This reframes the widespread sharing of open-weight models as a concrete security risk rather than merely a trust assumption. Open questions include whether training procedures can be modified to reduce intrinsic dimensionality (and thus vulnerability), whether detection methods exist for stealth-attacked models, and whether the approach extends to non-autoregressive or multimodal architectures. The jet-pack block also offers a practical, retraining-free tool for patching specific hallucinations, though its current limitation to single-prompt edits (rather than generalising corrections) constrains its applicability.
