## Problem

The Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) restricts agents to 18 discrete actions, making it incompatible with continuous-control methods like PPO and SAC, which have historically been evaluated on entirely different benchmarks (MuJoCo, DM-Control). This split has limited cross-pollination of ideas between the discrete- and continuous-control RL communities and prevented direct comparison of the two agent families on identical game dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244287/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Agents receive 4 stacked, grey-scaled, downsampled pixel frames as observations and output a 3-dimensional continuous action vector (r, θ, fire) ∈ [0,1]×[-π,π]×[0,1], where (r, θ) are polar coordinates specifying joystick position and the third dimension simulates the fire button. Crucially, these continuous values are mapped back to the same 9 discrete joystick events plus fire that the original ALE uses, via a threshold τ that partitions the unit circle into regions, so the underlying game mechanics and reward structure are identical to the ALE.

## Method

CALE is implemented as a thin wrapper around the existing ALE/Stella emulator that replaces the 18-action discrete space with a 3D continuous space parameterized in polar coordinates, using a tunable threshold τ to demarcate the nine joystick position events (lower τ yields more sensitive control, higher τ can occlude corner events). The paper provides SAC baselines using the Dopamine framework, comparing two convolutional encoder architectures (φ_SAC from Yarats et al. and φ_DQN from Mnih et al.), two exploration strategies (Gaussian sampling from the actor vs. ε-greedy uniform sampling), and multiple τ values. Baselines are evaluated under both the 200M-frame protocol and the Atari 100k sample-efficiency benchmark, with aggregate IQM scores and per-game breakdowns. A key finding is that SAC dramatically underperforms DQN and Data-Efficient Rainbow on the same games, partly due to the absence of minimal action sets in CALE and a rightward bias introduced by the polar-coordinate initialization at (0.5, 0.0, 0.5).

## Results

The SAC baseline on CALE achieves an aggregate IQM of 0.4 (where 1.0 corresponds to human-level performance), indicating a substantial gap to human-level play. In the 200M training regime, SAC dramatically underperforms DQN on the ALE, while in the 100k regime it underperforms Data-Efficient Rainbow (DER). Notably, the convolutional encoder φ_SAC outperforms the DQN encoder φ_DQN in both training regimes, and standard SAC exploration (sampling from the Gaussian actor) outperforms ε-greedy exploration in the 200M setting. A discrete-action variant of SAC (SAC-D) on the original ALE dramatically underperforms the continuous-action SAC on CALE, suggesting the continuous parameterization provides an advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DQN (Mnih et al., 2015) in the 200M regime, SAC on CALE dramatically underperforms in aggregate IQM, though per-game results are mixed: SAC surpasses DQN on Asteroids, Bowling, and Centipede, is comparable on Asterix, Boxing, MsPacman, and Pong, and underperforms on BankHeist, Breakout, and SpaceInvaders. Against Data-Efficient Rainbow (DER; Van Hasselt et al., 2019) in the 100k regime, SAC also dramatically underperforms, likely because DER was specifically tuned for the ALE while SAC was not tuned for CALE. Against discrete-action SAC (SAC-D; Christodoulou, 2019) on the original ALE, the continuous-action SAC on CALE surprisingly outperforms SAC-D, suggesting the continuous parameterization is advantageous in this setting. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) is also evaluated on CALE and shown alongside SAC in Figure 6, but the paper does not provide a detailed per-game breakdown of the PPO comparison. The authors attribute SAC's underperformance to the lack of tuning for CALE and the absence of minimal action sets, which disadvantages continuous agents on games where only a subset of joystick events matter.

## Relation to Prior Work

CALE is a direct extension of the Arcade Learning Environment (Bellemare et al., 2013), reusing the same Stella emulator and game set but replacing the 18 discrete joystick events with a three-dimensional continuous action space parameterized as polar coordinates (r, θ) plus a fire button. Conceptually, it bridges the long-standing divide between discrete-action benchmarks (ALE) and continuous-control benchmarks (MuJoCo, DM-Control), which have historically prevented direct comparison of Q-learning-based and policy-gradient-based agents. The baselines draw on SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) for continuous control and DQN/DER for discrete control, with the Dopamine framework providing the experimental infrastructure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CALE provides the first unified benchmark where discrete- and continuous-action agents can be evaluated on identical game dynamics, enabling direct study of how action-space parameterization affects learning. The preliminary SAC baseline achieves only 0.4 IQM (human-level = 1.0), underscoring that continuous control on pixel-based, non-smooth environments remains substantially unsolved. Key open questions include the role of encoder architecture, exploration strategy design for non-robotic continuous tasks, offline RL with continuous actions, and whether plasticity findings from MuJoCo transfer. A notable limitation is the absence of minimal action sets in CALE, which may disadvantage continuous agents relative to discrete ones on many games.
