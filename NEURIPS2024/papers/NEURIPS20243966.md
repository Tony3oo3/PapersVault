## Problem

Tokenization in large language models introduces performance biases across languages, increased adversarial vulnerability, and worse character-level modeling, yet no byte-level autoregressive architecture had matched the performance of subword-level Transformers under matched compute budgets. Prior byte-level approaches like MegaByte (fixed-size patches) and MambaByte (state-space blocks) still lagged significantly behind tokenized baselines when controlling for both training and inference FLOPs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243966/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw UTF-8 encoded text bytes (256-byte vocabulary) with a fixed context window of T bytes, and produces next-byte logits over all 256 possible byte values. A key structural detail is that the input is dynamically partitioned into variable-size patches at 'spacelike' byte boundaries (bytes that are not letters, digits, or UTF-8 continuation bytes), so the effective sequence length seen by the global model is roughly T/6 to T/8 patches rather than T tokens. During training, if the number of patches exceeds the maximum global context T_global, excess targets are masked; if fewer, activations are zero-padded.

## Method

SpaceByte is a byte-level Transformer decoder that inserts larger-dimension 'global' transformer blocks in the middle of the network, but applies them only after spacelike bytes that are not preceded by another spacelike byte (i.e., at word boundaries and after BOS tokens), rather than at a fixed interval as in MegaByte. The architecture stacks local transformer blocks (with sliding window attention of size D_local) before and after the global blocks (with full global attention over all patches), expanding the activation dimension via zero-padding before the global blocks and truncating after. The core insight is that the first character of a word is the hardest to predict, so concentrating the more expensive global computation at word boundaries is more efficient than spreading it uniformly. This simple dynamic patching rule, combined with the dual local-block structure and longer attention windows, allows SpaceByte to outperform all other byte-level architectures and roughly match SentencePiece-tokenized Transformers across English books, LaTeX papers, and source code.

## Results

On the primary compute-controlled comparison (10^19 FLOPs), SpaceByte achieves 1.009 bits-per-byte on PG-19, 0.748 on arXiv, and 0.500 on Github, outperforming all other byte-level architectures by a wide margin. SpaceByte roughly matches the SentencePiece subword Transformer (0.989, 0.768, 0.508) and outperforms the GPT2-tokenized subword Transformer (1.013, 0.796, 0.554) on arXiv and Github. In the cross-work comparison (Table 2, ~728M FLOPs-per-byte), SpaceByte-793M+184M achieves 0.918/0.833/0.663/0.411 bits-per-byte on PG-19/Stories/arXiv/Github, competitive with a 1B-parameter subword Transformer (0.908/0.809/0.666/0.400) while using only ~977M parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpaceByte significantly outperforms MegaByte [7] on all three datasets (e.g., 1.009 vs. 1.083 on PG-19, 0.748 vs. 0.822 on arXiv), with the gap attributed to dynamic word-aligned patching versus MegaByte's fixed patch size. Against the sliding-window attention Transformer baseline [29], SpaceByte also wins decisively (1.009 vs. 1.089 on PG-19), showing that simply increasing context via window attention is insufficient to close the byte-level gap. Compared to the standard byte-level Transformer [48], SpaceByte improves by 0.129 bits-per-byte on PG-19 (1.009 vs. 1.138). Against the SentencePiece subword Transformer, SpaceByte is within 0.02 bits-per-byte on PG-19 (1.009 vs. 0.989) and actually outperforms it on arXiv (0.748 vs. 0.768) and Github (0.500 vs. 0.508). In the cross-work comparison (Table 2), SpaceByte outperforms MambaByte [6] on PG-19 (0.918 vs. 0.930) and Stories (0.833 vs. 0.908), ties on arXiv (0.663), and is marginally behind on Github (0.411 vs. 0.396), while using a comparable FLOPs-per-byte budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpaceByte extends the multiscale byte-level modeling paradigm introduced by MegaByte (Yu et al., NeurIPS 2023) and the hierarchical Transformer architecture of the Hourglass Transformer (Nawrot et al., ACL 2022), but makes a crucial architectural departure: replacing fixed-size patches with dynamically sized patches aligned to word boundaries via a simple 'spacelike byte' heuristic. It also incorporates sliding window attention (from Longformer/Sparse Transformer/Shortformer) in the local blocks to handle long contexts efficiently, and adds a local model before the global blocks (unlike MegaByte's single post-global local model). The paper positions MambaByte as complementary rather than competing, noting Mamba blocks could substitute for Transformer blocks within the SpaceByte architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that a byte-level model can roughly match subword-level Transformer performance in compute-controlled settings, which is significant because it removes tokenization as a prerequisite for competitive language modeling—eliminating associated biases (language unfairness, adversarial vulnerability) without a performance penalty. However, the simple 'spacelike' patching rule is acknowledged to underperform on languages without spaces (e.g., Chinese), and the variable global-block spacing complicates efficient batched inference. Key open questions include learning optimal patching rules from data, extending multiscale modeling to sentence/paragraph scales, and integrating Mamba blocks for further efficiency gains.
