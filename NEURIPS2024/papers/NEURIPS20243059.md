## Problem

Neuroscience studies of language processing have been constrained by small data regimes, making it difficult to achieve the kind of scale-driven progress that has propelled NLP forward. No prior intracranial dataset combined naturalistic movie stimuli with high temporal resolution recordings, rich linguistic annotations (universal dependency parses, POS tags, speaker identity), and multimodal features at a scale sufficient for post-hoc natural experiments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243059/cropped_page2_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset comprises 43.5 hours of sEEG recordings (1,688 electrodes across 10 subjects, sampled at 2048 Hz) aligned to 21 Hollywood movies, with word onsets manually annotated on spectrograms to millisecond precision, 223,068 words tagged with POS and UD dependency parses, 46,935 scene labels from Places365, 937 unique speaker identities, and 16 per-word features (5 visual via OpenCV, 4 auditory via librosa, 6 language via Stanza/GPT-2). Analyses produce word-onset responsive electrode maps (244 electrodes), per-electrode GLM beta coefficients decomposing neural responses into visual/auditory/language contributions, and time-resolved linear decoding curves (ROC-AUC in 250ms windows shifted by 100ms) for sentence onsets, word onsets, and noun/verb distinctions.

## Method

The dataset construction pipeline aligns intracranial IFP recordings to movie audio via electrical triggers (100ms periodic pulses logged with wall-clock and movie timestamps), then layers manual annotations: spectrogram-based word onset/offset alignment, UD parse correction, speaker identity, and scene labeling. For quantitative analysis, word responsiveness is identified by paired t-tests comparing 100ms pre- and post-onset windows at five offsets (Bonferroni-corrected), and a GLM (pymer4, with movie as random effect for multi-movie subjects) regresses 500ms post-onset mean activity on all 16 normalized features to decompose contributions. A separate linear decoding analysis fits logistic regression per electrode on 250ms neural windows (decimated 10×, z-normalized) to classify sentence onsets vs. silence, word onsets vs. silence, or nouns vs. verbs, reporting ROC-AUC across a [-1s, +1s] time window to reveal the temporal dynamics of language processing in temporal and frontal lobes.

## Results

Across 1,688 electrodes from 10 subjects, 244 electrodes (~16%) show a statistically significant word-onset response after controlling for all audio and visual features. For sentence position, 235 electrodes (~15%) have a significant GLM beta coefficient for index-in-sentence, and 114 electrodes (~7%) show significant sentence-position modulation after controlling for all 16 confound features, predominantly in the transverse temporal cortex and superior temporal sulcus. Linear decoding reveals a mirrored temporal dynamics: sentence-onset information peaks at ~100 ms in the temporal lobe versus ~300 ms in the frontal lobe, while word-onset decoding peaks at ~200 ms (temporal) versus ~300 ms (frontal). The noun-verb distinction is most decodable in the frontal lobe, peaking at t = 400 ms after word onset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a dataset paper, the Brain Treebank does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines. Instead, the analytical contributions serve as internal validation of the dataset's utility. The key 'comparison' is between raw neural responses and responses after controlling for confounds: 244 electrodes retain significant word-onset responses after regressing out all 5 visual and 4 auditory features, confirming that the observed effects are not purely driven by co-occurring sensory inputs. Similarly, 114 electrodes show sentence-position modulation that survives controlling for all 16 features simultaneously, and 69 electrodes show part-of-speech sensitivity in the GLM. The paper also contrasts its scale with the closest prior intracranial dataset (Berezutskaya et al. [42]), which used only a 6.5-minute short film versus the Brain Treebank's average of 4.3 hours per subject, highlighting the order-of-magnitude increase in naturalistic language exposure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the aligned multimodal movie treebank pipeline of Yaari et al. (EMNLP 2022) by coupling it with large-scale intracranial sEEG recordings, creating the first dataset that combines full Universal Dependency annotation, multi-modal feature extraction, and high-resolution neural data from naturalistic movie viewing. It departs from prior intracranial language studies (which used controlled stimuli or short clips, e.g., Berezutskaya et al. 2022 with a 6.5-minute film) by scaling to 43.5 hours of full Hollywood movies per cohort, and from prior naturalistic-stimulus fMRI/EEG work by providing the temporal and spatial resolution of depth electrodes. The analytical framework (GLM with 16 features, linear decoding) follows standard approaches from the computational neuroscience literature, but the multi-layer annotation (UD parses, word onsets, scene labels, speaker identity) is novel in its combination with intracranial data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Brain Treebank provides the first large-scale resource for studying how linguistic structure (syntax, POS, surprisal) maps onto neural activity at single-electrode resolution during naturalistic comprehension, bridging the gap between controlled lab paradigms and ecologically valid language processing. Key open questions remain: the authors note they have barely exploited speaker identity, multimodal integration, or the full dependency parse trees, and more powerful (non-linear) decoders may reveal richer structure. Limitations include single-viewing per movie, heterogeneous movie assignments across subjects, English-only content, and potential sampling bias from the epilepsy patient population. The dataset is positioned as a long-term shared resource to enable the kind of post-hoc natural experiments that have driven progress in NLP.
