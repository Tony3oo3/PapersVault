## Problem

While empirical studies have demonstrated that similarity-based edge reconstruction attacks (SERA) can recover graph topology from GNN node representations, the theoretical conditions under which such attacks succeed or fail remain unexplored. In particular, it is unclear whether feature homophily is necessary for SERA's effectiveness, how graph sparsity governs reconstruction difficulty, and whether privacy-preserving mechanisms like noisy aggregation (NAG) can provably resist these attacks.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes an undirected graph with adjacency matrix A and i.i.d. isotropic Gaussian node features X ∈ R^{n×d}, processed through an L-layer linear GNN encoder with weight matrix W. The adversary receives only the resulting node representations H^{(L)} (black-box access, no raw features or model parameters) and must output a reconstructed adjacency matrix via a thresholded similarity score. The theoretical outputs are non-asymptotic bounds on FPR, FNR, and AUROC as functions of graph size n, sparsity parameter, feature dimension d, encoder depth L, and the condition number κ(W).

## Method

The paper establishes three complementary theoretical results. First, a non-asymptotic analysis (Theorem 4.1) proves that SERA achieves zero false negatives and vanishing false positives on sparse random graphs (average degree O(log n)) with i.i.d. Gaussian features, showing that homophily is not required—sparsity alone suffices for near-perfect reconstruction when d ≫ (log n)^{6L+2}. Second, a lower-bound analysis (Theorem 5.1) shows that on dense stochastic block models with Θ(1) intra-group connection probability, SERA's error rate is bounded below by non-vanishing constants, revealing a fundamental barrier. Third, a privacy guarantee (Theorem 6.1) extends prior NAG analyses to GCN, GAT, SAGE, and GIN by bounding the KL divergence between representations under adjacent graphs, yielding a min-error lower bound that depends on the ratio of weight operator norms to noise scale σ². Empirically, the paper validates all three results on Erdős-Rényi graphs, SBMs, and eight real-world datasets, and evaluates NAG under both unconstrained and spectrally-normalized training schemes.

## Results

On 8 real-world graph datasets, SERA achieves AUROC of 92.5–99.9% on homophilic Planetoid datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed) and 72.6–87.3% on heterophilic datasets (Squirrel, Chameleon, Actor), substantially outperforming the feature-similarity baseline (AFS) which scores only 44.7–87.6%. Theoretically, Theorem 4.1 proves that on sparse Erdős-Rényi graphs with edge probability p = log n / n, SERA achieves FNR = 0 and FPR ≤ (C₂ log n)^(2L)/n, yielding AUROC ≥ 1 − (C₂ log n)^(2L)/n. On dense SBMs with intra-group probability p = Θ(1), Theorem 5.1 establishes non-vanishing error lower bounds, confirming SERA's inherent limitations on dense graphs. Under NAG defense with spectral normalization (constrained scheme), SERA AUROC drops below 0.6 at σ = 1 while model utility remains above 70% accuracy on Cora.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between SERA and the feature-similarity baseline (AFS), which predicts edges solely from raw node feature cosine similarity without any GNN encoding. SERA outperforms AFS by 15–45 AUROC points across all datasets, with the largest gains on heterophilic graphs (e.g., Squirrel: 87.3 vs. 46.2 for GCN L=2 pre-training; Actor: 87.1 vs. 44.7), demonstrating that GNN message-passing amplifies topological signal beyond what features alone provide. Among GNN architectures, GCN consistently outperforms the linear GNN (LIN) by 10–20 AUROC points on heterophilic datasets, though the gap narrows on homophilic Planetoid data where both reach >99%. Increasing depth from L=2 to L=5 does not improve attack efficacy and often degrades it (e.g., GCN on Squirrel drops from 87.3 to 82.1), consistent with the theoretical oversmoothing prediction in Theorem 4.1. Post-training (supervised) generally reduces SERA AUROC by 3–15 points compared to random initialization, suggesting that training dynamics worsen weight conditioning. On dense SBM graphs (K=3, p=0.3, q=0.05), SERA AUROC plateaus well below 1.0 across all configurations, empirically confirming the non-vanishing error lower bound from Theorem 5.1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first non-asymptotic theoretical analysis of the similarity-based edge reconstruction attack (SERA), which was previously studied only empirically by He et al. (USENIX Security 2021) and Duddu et al. (2020). It extends the noisy aggregation (NAG) privacy guarantees from Wu et al. (2023) and Sajadmanesh et al. (USENIX Security 2023), which covered specific architectures, to a broader family including GCN, GAT, SAGE, and GIN. The paper also departs from the prevailing narrative that homophily drives SERA's effectiveness by showing that sparsity alone suffices, and it introduces a provable failure regime on dense stochastic block models that prior empirical work had not characterized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that sparsity—not feature-edge correlation—is the critical determinant of SERA's success reshapes how practitioners should assess privacy risk in GRL deployments: even graphs with no homophily pattern are vulnerable if sufficiently sparse. The dense-SBM failure bound delineates a clear boundary where SERA becomes ineffective, which is important for calibrating when formal privacy mechanisms are truly needed. Open questions include whether the polylogarithmic feature-dimension requirement in Theorem 4.1 is tight, whether SERA can serve as a reliable privacy auditing tool given its demonstrated blind spots on dense graphs, and how these results extend to non-linear GNNs beyond the linear architecture analyzed theoretically.
