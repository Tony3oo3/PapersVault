## Problem

The train-test paradigm, the standard method for validating ML models, implicitly assumes data is collected in a way that permits valid inference about generalization error. However, modern AI systems (recommender systems, LLM-based QA) rely on passively collected data from complex social systems where the sampling distribution S differs from the target distribution T and follows heavy-tailed, power-law patterns. This paper asks whether model validation can be valid at all under these conditions and establishes formal impossibility results showing it cannot, for the vast majority of the system, under any risk estimator.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a sample graph (bipartite or hypergraph) of observed interactions whose node degrees follow regularly-varying power-law distributions, a rank assumption k bounding the complexity of the underlying system, and a target distribution T (typically uniform over all possible interactions). It produces not a model or prediction but an epistemic verdict: a formal test validity/invalidity determination for the inference setting, a quantification of the fraction of nodes for which validation is valid as a function of k (decaying as k^{-α}), and explicit k-core conditions that must be satisfied. The output is structured as necessary conditions rather than sufficient ones, reflecting the passive-data setting where control over collection is absent.

## Method

The core mechanism connects matrix completion theory to test validity through graph k-connectivity. Lemma 1 establishes that if the sample graph is not k-connected (where k is the rank assumption on system complexity), the set of possible worlds consistent with observations forms a non-empty vector space, meaning infinitely many equally-valid explanations exist that differ arbitrarily on unobserved entries. Lemma 2 then leverages the generalized Pythagorean theorem for Bregman divergences to show that for any risk estimator—including counterfactual estimators like Horvitz-Thompson—the probability of bounding the gap between estimated and true risk is exactly zero. Theorem 1 bridges k-connectivity to power-law degree distributions, proving the fraction of nodes with valid test settings decays as n_i · u(k) · k^{-α_i}, where α_i is the tail index. Corollary 2 demonstrates that naive scaling requires at least (|X|/2)^{α+1}/(α·x_min^α) samples and benchmarks must cover nearly all nodes, making both approaches prohibitively inefficient. Empirical validation on MOVIELENS 100k fits 100 rank-50 matrices to observed data and shows they differ by 77%+ NAE on 50% of unobserved entries, with validity varying sharply across demographic groups.

## Results

The paper establishes that under passive data collection from complex social systems with power-law degree distributions, the train-test paradigm is test-invalid for the vast majority of nodes. Theorem 1 shows the fraction of nodes with valid test settings decays as E[V_i] ≤ n_i · u(k) · k^(−α_i) in the rank k of the system. For Book Crossing parameters (α=2.38, |X|=10⁵), validity coverage drops from 100% at rank 8 to 58.8% at rank 10, 11.3% at rank 20, and just 0.2% at rank 100. On MOVIELENS 100k, fitting 100 rank-50 matrices to observed data yields pairwise NAE of 23–49% for 50% of unobserved entries, and for rank-60 models test-validity holds for 67% of 'technicians' but only 14% of 'homemakers'. Corollary 2 quantifies that scaling requires ≥2×10²¹ samples and benchmarks need >9.9×10⁶ additional data points to restore validity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical impossibility paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no traditional baselines. The results are positioned as a strengthening of the No Free Lunch theorems [Wolpert, 1997]: while NFL assumes full ontological neutrality (an induction-hostile universe), this paper shows test invalidity even under non-trivial inductive assumptions (low-rank structure) when data is passively collected from social systems. Compared to prior matrix completion work (Liu et al. 2019, Meka et al. 2009, Cheng et al. 2018), the key distinction is that those works establish conditions for unique completability or learning, whereas this paper proves that no risk estimator—including counterfactual and Horvitz-Thompson estimators—can be valid when the sample graph lacks sufficient k-connectivity relative to the system's rank. The MOVIELENS 100k experiments confirm the theoretical predictions: 100 rank-50 matrices all fit observed data with error <10⁻³ yet differ substantially on unobserved entries, demonstrating that no subset of the benchmark can serve as a valid test set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper strengthens the No Free Lunch theorems (Wolpert 1997) by showing that model validation is invalid even under non-trivial inductive assumptions (low-rank structure) when data is passively collected from social systems with heavy-tailed degree distributions, rather than requiring the induction-hostile assumption-free setting of classical NFL. The technical machinery draws on matrix completion under deterministic sampling (Liu et al. 2019) for the S-isomerism and vector-space structure of possible worlds, and on Bregman divergence geometry (Dhillon & Tropp 2008) for the risk-inequality argument. Conceptually, it connects to the underspecification/model-multiplicity literature (D'Amour et al., Black et al.) and to validity theory from psychology, but departs by providing formal impossibility results grounded in the combinatorial structure of sample graphs rather than merely documenting the existence of multiple consistent models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that the train-test paradigm is invalid for the vast majority of nodes in realistic social systems—even for counterfactual estimators designed to handle S≠T—challenges a foundational assumption underlying virtually all of modern AI evaluation. The corollary that naive scaling and limited benchmarks are prohibitively inefficient to fix this (requiring ~10²¹ samples in one illustrative case) rules out the most common proposed remedies. Open questions include how to design targeted data collection that increases k-connectivity, how to extend results to higher-arity relations and bounded output domains, and whether participatory data curation can practically close the validity gap. The fairness implications (different demographic groups have vastly different validity coverage, e.g., 67% vs. 14% for rank-60 models) raise urgent questions about deployed recommender systems.
