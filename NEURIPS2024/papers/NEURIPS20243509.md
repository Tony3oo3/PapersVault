## Problem

Existing contrastive-learning-based Meta-RL context encoders fail to distinguish tasks that require different optimal skills (e.g., Push vs. Pick&Place), treating all same-task trajectories as equivalent regardless of the behaviour mode. Additionally, standard K-sample MI estimators like InfoNCE suffer from the log-K curse, where the achievable MI is bottlenecked by the number of negative samples, making them impractical in sample-limited RL settings where collecting large negative sample spaces across many tasks is infeasible.

## Input / Output

The context encoder (an LSTM with a 128-dim hidden state projected to a 6-dim embedding) consumes the current episode's trajectory (states, actions, rewards up to time t) and produces a context embedding c that conditions the SAC policy. The policy then takes the current state s_t together with c to output an action. For contrastive training, positive and negative samples are both drawn from the same task's replay buffer, ranked by cumulative return: top-ranked trajectories serve as positive samples (generated by the optimal skill) and bottom-ranked ones as negatives (generated by suboptimal skills), with a momentum encoder ψ* producing target embeddings for stability.

## Method

The paper introduces Skill-aware Mutual Information (SaMI), defined as I(c;π_c;τ_c) = I(c;τ_c) − I(c;τ_c|π_c), a three-variable interaction information objective that is provably smaller than I(c;τ_c) and thus easier to optimise, focusing the encoder on skill-discriminative information. To estimate SaMI with finite samples, they propose SaNCE, a K-sample estimator where both positive and negative trajectories are sampled from the current task (generated by different skills π_c⁺ and π_c⁻), reducing the required sample space by a factor of |c| compared to InfoNCE, which draws negatives from all tasks. A soft variant of the SaNCE loss gates the contrastive term by the L2 distance between positive and negative embeddings, preventing the loss from dominating early in training when all returns are low. The full framework integrates SaNCE into existing Meta-RL algorithms (TESAC → SaTESAC, CCM → SaCCM) alongside the standard RL loss, with the context encoder updated by gradients from both objectives and a momentum-updated target encoder for contrastive stability.

## Results

On Panda-gym, SaCCM achieves 0.57±0.26 success rate on moderate test and 0.36±0.35 on extreme test, outperforming CCM (0.49/0.29) and TESAC (0.31/0.22). On the modified MuJoCo benchmark, SaMI-based methods show the largest gains in skill-diverse environments: e.g., on Crippled Hopper extreme test, SaCCM reaches 2059±221 return vs. CCM's 1009±289, and on HumanoidStandup extreme test, SaCCM achieves 141750±27426 vs. CCM's 94991±15258. All SaMI improvements over their non-SaMI counterparts are statistically significant (p<0.05 paired t-tests). SaNCE maintains performance with a |c|-times smaller negative sample space, demonstrating robustness to reduced buffer and batch sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SaTESAC and SaCCM consistently outperform all five baselines across both benchmarks, with the largest margins in extreme generalisation settings. Against CCM (the strongest contrastive baseline), SaCCM improves extreme-test success rate from 0.29 to 0.36 on Panda-gym and extreme-test return from 1009 to 2059 on Crippled Hopper (MuJoCo). Against TESAC (RL-loss-only context), the gap widens substantially in extreme settings (e.g., 0.36 vs. 0.22 on Panda-gym extreme). PEARL, which uses probabilistic context variables without contrastive learning, shows the weakest generalisation (0.11 extreme success on Panda-gym). DOMINO and CaDM are compared in Appendix G under the same MuJoCo setup; the paper notes that SaMI does not introduce the independence assumptions used by DOMINO, making it more suitable for correlated environmental features. The key differentiator is that SaMI-based methods excel specifically in environments requiring diverse skills (Crippled variants, Panda-gym with varying mass/friction), while in single-skill environments (e.g., Hopper, Ant) the advantage over CCM is smaller or absent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the contrastive-learning-based Meta-RL line (CCM, CaDM, DOMINO) by introducing skills as a third variable in the mutual information objective, moving from the standard two-variable MI between context embeddings and trajectories to a three-variable interaction information (SaMI). It departs from InfoNCE's K-sample estimator by proposing SaNCE, which restricts negative sampling to the current task's skill space rather than all tasks, reducing the required sample space by a factor of |c|. The practical framework builds directly on TESAC and CCM architectures, integrating SaNCE as an additional contrastive loss alongside the RL loss, while the momentum encoder design follows the CURL/MoCo pattern.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The log-K curse is a genuine practical bottleneck in Meta-RL where collecting large numbers of negative samples across many task combinations is expensive, and SaNCE's |c|-fold reduction in sample space offers a concrete, well-motivated remedy. However, the skill identification mechanism (ranking trajectories by return within a task) is a simplification that may not capture semantically meaningful skill boundaries, and the paper acknowledges that correlated environmental features remain untested. Open questions include whether the emergent skill clusters are robust to different reward structures, whether the approach scales to continuous skill spaces, and whether the moderate performance gains (e.g., ~8% absolute in Panda-gym moderate test) persist in more complex, high-dimensional real-world robotic settings.
