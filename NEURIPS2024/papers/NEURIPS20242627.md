## Problem

Neural architecture search is energy-intensive, with single NAS runs consuming thousands of GPU-hours and emitting hundreds of thousands of pounds of CO2. While recent work has reduced search time, it treats carbon emissions uniformly regardless of when and where computation occurs, conflating energy efficiency with carbon efficiency. CE-NAS addresses this gap by exploiting temporal variations in grid carbon intensity to schedule energy-intensive and energy-efficient NAS phases at different times.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242627/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes hourly carbon intensity traces (sourced from ElectricityMap at 1-hour resolution), a multi-objective search space definition (e.g., NASNet or EfficientNet parameterized spaces), a carbon budget that serves as the search termination condition, and a fixed pool of available GPUs. It produces a searched architecture selected from the Pareto frontier (optimizing accuracy against latency or parameter count), an hourly discrete GPU allocation ratio (K=8 levels) splitting resources between sampling and evaluation, and hourly carbon intensity forecasts from the time-series transformer. The RL agent's state additionally encodes the current hypervolume of found architectures and the number of searched candidates, linking allocation decisions to search progress.

## Method

CE-NAS decouples NAS into two phases with different energy profiles: energy-efficient sampling (generating new architectures via qNEHVI Bayesian optimization and estimating their accuracy through a few-shot supernet) and energy-intensive evaluation (actually training candidate architectures to convergence). A time-series transformer (encoder-decoder with 64 encoder and 96 decoder layers, embedding size 48, context length 8760) forecasts hourly carbon intensity, outperforming prior SOTA forecasters by 2–4 MAPE points. An actor-critic RL agent (4 fully connected layers, hidden sizes 100/150/200/100) takes the remaining carbon budget, number of searched architectures, current hypervolume, and predicted carbon traces as state, and outputs a discrete GPU allocation ratio (K=8) that shifts more GPUs toward sampling during high-carbon hours and toward evaluation during low-carbon hours. LaMOO recursively partitions the search space via MCTS to focus on promising subspaces, and architectures in the ready-to-train queue are prioritized by dominance number so that those closest to the Pareto frontier are trained first when low-carbon windows open.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (NasNet search space), CE-Net-P1 achieves 97.35% top-1 accuracy (2.65% error) with only 1.68M parameters and a carbon cost of 38.53 lbs CO₂, while CE-Net-P2 reaches 97.84% top-1 accuracy (2.16% error) with 3.30M parameters at the same carbon cost. On ImageNet, CE-Net-G2 achieves 80.6% top-1 accuracy (19.4% error) with 0.78 ms TensorRT FP16 latency on NVIDIA V100, consuming 909.86 lbs CO₂. On HW-NAS-Bench, CE-NAS reduces carbon emissions by up to 7.22× compared to vanilla LaMOO while achieving a hypervolume (HV ≈ 4100) within 0.034% of vanilla LaMOO (HV ≈ 4117). On NasBench301, CE-NAS achieves the second-best hypervolume with the second-lowest relative carbon emission, outperforming the SOTA heuristic GPU allocation strategy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On CIFAR-10, CE-Net-P2 (2.16% error) outperforms all vanilla NAS baselines (best: AmoebaNet-B-small at 2.50%) and all one-shot baselines (best: P-DARTS at 2.50%) in top-1 accuracy, while CE-Net-P1 (2.65% error) matches NASNet-A's accuracy with 49% fewer parameters (1.68M vs. 3.3M). The carbon cost of CE-NAS (38.53 lbs) is comparable to one-shot methods (21.70–65.87 lbs) but 50–1000× lower than vanilla NAS (1514–43972 lbs). On ImageNet, CE-Net-G2 (19.4% error, 0.78 ms) beats the best one-shot baseline OFA-Net (20.0% error, 1.16 ms) in both accuracy and latency, with a carbon cost (909.86 lbs) only ~2× higher than the best one-shot (278.15 lbs for PC-DARTS) but 3–48× lower than vanilla NAS (2768–44177 lbs). On HW-NAS-Bench, CE-NAS achieves a hypervolume within 0.034% of vanilla LaMOO while consuming 3.24×–7.22× less carbon, and outperforms the heuristic GPU allocation strategy [4] at every carbon budget tested. On NasBench301, CE-NAS surpasses the heuristic strategy in hypervolume at comparable carbon cost, and at equivalent carbon budgets achieves 4× lower carbon than one-shot and vanilla LaMOO for comparable search quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

CE-NAS builds on LaMOO for multi-objective search space partitioning, qNEHVI for Bayesian architecture sampling, and few-shot supernet evaluation, but its core contribution is the integration of a carbon-aware scheduling layer that prior NAS work entirely lacked. The paper explicitly distinguishes carbon efficiency from energy efficiency (citing Bashir et al.), noting that prior NAS methods minimize GPU-hours without accounting for temporal grid carbon intensity variations. It also draws on systems research on carbon-aware scheduling for training and inference workloads, but is the first to apply such temporal carbon-awareness specifically to the NAS search process, where the workload naturally decomposes into energy-efficient sampling and energy-intensive evaluation phases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NAS is a major and growing source of carbon emissions (a single NAS run can emit as much as five cars over their lifetime), and this paper demonstrates that a 7.22× carbon reduction is achievable with negligible search-quality loss by simply respecting temporal carbon intensity. The practical impact is significant for labs and cloud providers running NAS at scale, as the framework requires no hardware changes—only a scheduling policy conditioned on publicly available grid data. Key limitations include the restriction to temporal (not spatial) carbon optimization, the RL agent being trained for a specific NAS algorithm combination (LaMOO + qNEHVI), and the reliance on a fixed carbon budget as a termination condition. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other NAS paradigms (e.g., evolutionary or RL-based NAS), how it scales to multi-region GPU clusters, and whether spatial carbon arbitrage can be incorporated without prohibitive data-transfer costs.
