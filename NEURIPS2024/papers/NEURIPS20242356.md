## Problem

As lifelong benchmarks grow to hundreds of millions of samples to resist overfitting, the O(|D||M|) cost of re-evaluating every model on every sample becomes practically infeasible (e.g., 180 GPU days for 31,000 models on 1.69M samples). The paper asks whether one can predict sample-wise accuracy for new models (or difficulty for new samples) by evaluating only a tiny subset, while keeping approximation error low and memory overhead minimal.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a binary accuracy cache matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{m×n} built from prior model evaluations, plus either a new model to be tested on n' ≪ n uniformly sampled points or new samples to be tested on m' ≪ m models. It produces a full-length sample-wise prediction vector y_{m+1} ∈ {0,1}^{1×n} with a strict threshold structure [1_k^T, 0_{n-k}^T], meaning all samples up to position k are predicted correct and all beyond are predicted incorrect. The entire pipeline requires only two 1D arrays of length n (the sort-sum vector and the current search output), totaling under 100 MB even at billion-sample scale.

## Method

Sort & Search (S&S) is a two-stage framework inspired by Computerized Adaptive Testing. In the Sort stage, samples are ranked from easy to hard by sorting columns of the accuracy cache by their row-sum (number of models that classified them correctly); alternatives include confidence-based sorting and a recursive two-stage correction for tied difficulties. In the Search stage, n' points are sampled uniformly from the sorted order, a dynamic-programming algorithm (DP-Search) finds the optimal row-wise threshold in O(n) time via a prefix-sum trick, and that threshold is extrapolated from n' to n to construct the full prediction vector. The same procedure handles new-sample insertion by transposing the cache (A → A^T), so models are ranked by aggregate accuracy instead. A key theoretical result proves DP-Search returns the globally optimal threshold for any fixed permutation, and an error decomposition shows the residual (aleatoric) error stems from imperfect generalization of the single global ranking to new models, not from the sampling strategy—guiding future work toward multi-rank generalization rather than better search.

## Results

Sort & Search (S&S) reduces lifelong model evaluation cost by ~1000x, from 180 GPU days to 5 GPU hours on a single A100-80GB GPU, while maintaining low sample-level mean absolute error (MAE). On Lifelong-CIFAR10 (31,250 models, 1.69M samples) and Lifelong-ImageNet (167 models, 1.98M samples), MAE decays exponentially with sampling budget n', reaching 0.15–0.17 with only n'=100 samples (a 10,000x compute saving). For sample difficulty estimation (insert_D), S&S achieves MAE < 0.15 by evaluating only m'=64 out of 31,250 models (10^4x savings). Total storage overhead is <100 MB (0.0166% of input data).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares S&S against a single baseline: CopyNearest&Expand, a scalable adaptation of Vivek et al. [91] (Anchor Points) that removes the Sort component and uses random sampling with nearest-neighbor expansion. S&S wins on all three axes: (1) accuracy — 1% lower MAE at n'=8,192, meaning ~19k more correctly classified samples on average; (2) convergence speed — S&S reaches its asymptotic error at n'=1,024 while the baseline requires n'=32,768 (a 32x larger budget); (3) robustness to fewer sort models — at n'=512, S&S with only 10 sort models still outperforms the baseline using 50 sort models. The paper also contrasts S&S with an oracle dataset-level accuracy prediction (which achieves E_agg=0 but sample-level MAE of 0.4–0.5), showing S&S without oracle access achieves 0.15–0.17 MAE with just 100 samples, demonstrating the inadequacy of aggregate accuracy metrics for lifelong evaluation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of lifelong benchmarking (ever-expanding test pools inspired by Dynabench and dynamic sampling) and efficient model evaluation (inspired by Computerized Adaptive Testing). It departs from concurrent instance-level evaluation methods like Anchor Point Sampling [91] and IRT-Clustering [69], which require O(|D|²) memory and cannot scale beyond a few thousand samples, by achieving O(|D| log |D|) complexity through a sort-and-search decomposition. It also distinguishes itself from dataset-level ranking methods (Model Spider, LOVM, Flash-Eval) by operating at the sample level, which is necessary to amortize costs across a growing accuracy cache. The paper further argues that the standard aggregate-accuracy prediction metric is fundamentally flawed (Theorem 2.1), motivating the sample-level formulation that prior efficient-evaluation work has not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 1000× compute reduction (180 GPU days → 5 GPU hours) makes lifelong benchmarking practically feasible for the first time, and the two introduced benchmarks (1.69M and 1.98M samples) provide immediate large-scale testbeds. The error decomposition revealing that nearly all residual error is aleatoric (ranking generalization) rather than epistemic (insufficient sampling) is a clear signal that future work should focus on generalizing beyond a single global difficulty ordering rather than on better sampling strategies. A key open question is whether the single-ranking assumption holds for tasks beyond image classification (e.g., retrieval, captioning), where model cliques with divergent difficulty perceptions might emerge. The method's reliance on a binary accuracy cache also limits direct applicability to real-valued or multi-label tasks without thresholding.
