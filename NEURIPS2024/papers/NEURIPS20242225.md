## Problem

While off-policy value-based RL agents are known to suffer feature rank collapse and capacity loss under non-stationarity, this connection to representation dynamics has been overlooked in on-policy policy optimization methods like PPO. A crucial open question is why PPO's trust region—designed to constrain policy updates via probability-ratio clipping—fails to prevent the performance collapse that accompanies representation deterioration. This paper provides the first systematic study of feature rank and capacity loss in PPO and identifies the mechanism by which representation collapse renders the trust region ineffective.

## Input / Output

For Atari (ALE), the agent consumes 84×84 grayscale pixel observations with 4-frame stacking and outputs discrete actions; for MuJoCo, it takes continuous state vectors and outputs actions sampled from a state-dependent TanhNormal distribution whose mean and log-variance both condition on the learned representation. The method specifically tracks the pre-activations (not post-activation outputs) of the penultimate layer as the representation whose dynamics drive collapse, since dead ReLU neurons cannot propagate gradients through their activations, making pre-activation norm a more sensitive diagnostic than feature norm.

## Method

The core contribution is Proximal Feature Optimization (PFO), an auxiliary L2 loss that penalizes the squared distance between the pre-activations of the current policy π_θ and the old policy π_old that collected the batch, effectively extending PPO's trust region from output probabilities into the feature space. The key mechanistic insight is that as feature rank collapses, representations of different states become colinear, causing gradients across states to align—this makes PPO's per-sample clipping ineffective because an update on one state pushes the probability of another state beyond its clip limit. A formal toy setting with 1D representations (φ(y) = αφ(x)) demonstrates this: for α ≥ 0, a gradient step on (x, a₁) necessarily moves π_θ(a₁|y) past its clipped ratio, and for α < 0, it drives the probability below its initial value, showing the trust region constraint is structurally unenforceable under rank collapse. The paper further corroborates the analysis with interventions—sharing the actor-critic trunk and adapting Adam's second-moment decay—that regularize representations and reduce the excess probability ratio, confirming that controlling feature dynamics mitigates performance collapse.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that PPO agents suffer from feature rank collapse in 5/6 ALE games and 7/8 MuJoCo tasks, with pre-activation norms increasing consistently throughout training. Performance collapse is observed in 3/6 ALE games and 3/4 MuJoCo tasks, coinciding with rank collapse and a breakdown of the PPO trust region (probability ratios significantly exceeding clipping limits). The proposed PFO regularization reduces pre-activation norm, dead neurons, and capacity loss while increasing feature rank, and most importantly mitigates performance collapse on ALE/Phoenix and ALE/NameThisGame, with a slight increase in upper-tail returns. Sharing the actor-critic trunk acts as a regularizer in dense-reward environments but is detrimental in sparse-reward settings (Gravitar), where the value network's rank collapse propagates to the policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead evaluates interventions against the standard PPO-Clip baseline across ALE and MuJoCo. PFO (applied to penultimate or all pre-activations) provides the most consistent improvements: it lowers the excess probability ratio (especially the upper tail), reduces dead neurons and capacity loss, increases feature rank, and prevents performance collapse on ALE/Phoenix and ALE/NameThisGame. Sharing the actor-critic trunk is beneficial in dense-reward environments (Phoenix, NameThisGame) where it concentrates ranks at low but non-trivial values and improves returns, but is harmful in the sparse-reward Gravitar environment where the value network's rank collapse destroys the shared policy representation. Adam interventions (resetting moments per batch or accelerating second-moment decay) reduce feature norm and increase rank on ALE and lower the excess ratio, but are insufficient to prevent collapse and also perform poorly on Gravitar. Overall, PFO is the only intervention that consistently improves both representation quality and trust-region behavior across environments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representation dynamics literature from value-based RL (Kumar et al., 2021; Lyle et al., 2022) to on-policy policy optimization, specifically PPO. It builds on the loss-of-plasticity observations in PPO (Dohare et al., 2021; Abbas et al., 2023) by providing the missing mechanistic link: feature rank collapse and capacity loss. The trust region breakdown analysis connects to prior observations of ratio violations in PPO-Clip (Wang et al., 2020; Engstrom et al., 2020) but identifies representation colinearity as the systematic cause rather than optimizer artifacts. The proposed PFO loss is conceptually distinct from DR3 (Kumar et al., 2022) and RD (Ma et al., 2023), which target implicit regularization in offline RL, whereas PFO extends the trust region to feature space in an on-policy setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PPO is one of the most widely deployed RL algorithms, and this paper provides the first diagnostic framework (feature rank, capacity loss, pre-activation norm) for understanding its performance collapse, along with a simple practical fix (PFO). The finding that the trust region becomes ineffective precisely when representations degrade is a critical caveat for practitioners relying on PPO-Clip's safety guarantees. Limitations include restriction to small models without normalization layers, and the generalization to larger architectures (Transformers, RNNs) or different environments remains open. A deeper theoretical understanding of why non-stationarity drives representation deterioration in policy networks specifically is still lacking.
