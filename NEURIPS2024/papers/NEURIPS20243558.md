## Problem

Iterative approximation solvers for combinatorial optimization are limited to a narrow receptive field, accessing only a small fraction of the solution space per iteration and thus prone to local minima. Existing remedies (large neighborhood search, variable neighborhood search) only modestly expand this scope. The paper instead asks whether information from distant regions can be actively propagated to the solver, rather than expanding the solver's reach.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a general pseudo-Boolean target function f(s) (quadratic, polynomial, or mixed-continuous), a step size γ, a σ schedule {σ_t} (where σ_t = √τ_t controls the heat diffusion degree), and an iteration count T. Each iteration consumes a single uniform sample x_t ~ Unif[0,1]^n to estimate the heat-diffused gradient, making it far cheaper than Monte Carlo gradient estimation which requires M samples. Outputs are binary configurations s_T for standard PBO, ternary weight matrices for quantized networks, indicator vectors plus OLS coefficients for variable selection, and vertex cover sets for constrained problems.

## Method

HeO extends the parameter space from [0,1]^n to R^n and solves the unbounded heat equation ∂_τ u = Δ_θ u with initial condition u(0,θ) = h(θ), where h(θ) = E_{p(s|θ)}[f(s)]. By backward uniqueness of the heat equation, u(τ,θ) shares the same global minima as h(θ) for all τ > 0, so gradient descent on u(τ_t, θ) at varying τ_t cooperatively minimizes the original objective. For multilinear polynomial f(s), the heat equation admits a closed-form solution u(τ,θ) = E_{x~Unif[0,1]^n}[f(erf((θ−x)/√τ))], enabling efficient single-sample gradient estimation via autograd. The update rule θ_{t+1} = Proj_[0,1]^n(θ_t − γ∇_θ u(τ_t, θ_t)) projects back to the hypercube each step, and the non-monotonic τ_t schedule is permissible because all transformed functions share the same optima—a cooperative optimization property absent in quantum adiabatic or bifurcation-based methods.

## Results

On the minimum vertex cover problem across 7 large-scale real-world graphs (up to 58.8M vertices), HeO consistently finds smaller cover sets than FastVC, e.g., 312,531 vs. 338,724 on socfb-B-anon (7.7% reduction) and 387,730 vs. 421,123 on socfb-A-anon (7.9% reduction). On max-cut problems from the Biq Mac Library, HeO achieves the lowest average relative loss and the fewest bottom-2 worst results among 7 algorithms. On 3-SAT instances from SATLIB, HeO finds more complete satisfiable solutions than both 2-order and 3-order OIM, particularly for 175–250 variable instances where 2-order OIM fails entirely. On 400-dimensional variable selection, HeO outperforms both Lasso and L0.5 regression in indicator accuracy and test MSE across all sparsity and noise settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HeO outperforms all 12 baselines across every experimental setting. On the toy neural-network minimization (Fig. 2), HeO with momentum dominates MCGE [28], simulated annealing [33], and Hopfield neural network [34] in both target function value and uncertainty reduction. On max-cut (Biq Mac Library, Fig. 3), HeO achieves the lowest average relative loss and the fewest bottom-2 worst results compared to LQA [10], aSB [12], bSB/dSB [13], CIM [35], and SIM-CIM [15]. On 3-SAT (SATLIB, Fig. 4), HeO surpasses both 2-order and 3-order OIM [16] in mean constraint satisfaction and probability of full satisfiability; for 175–250 variables, HeO finds more complete solutions than 3-order OIM while 2-order OIM finds none. On ternary neural network training (Fig. 5), HeO robustly exceeds MCGE with momentum across all dataset sizes and output dimensions. On 400-dimensional variable selection (Fig. 6), HeO beats both Lasso [40] and L0.5 regression [41] in indicator accuracy and test MSE without requiring prior knowledge of sparsity or noise level. On minimum vertex cover (Table 1), HeO finds strictly smaller covers than FastVC [44] on all 7 graphs, with the largest relative improvement (~7.9%) on socfb-A-anon.

## Relation to Prior Work

HeO is explicitly positioned as a stochastic Gaussian continuation (GC) method with projection, extending the GC framework [49] from non-convex optimization to combinatorial optimization. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant strategy of expanding the solver's receptive field (large neighborhood search, variable neighborhood search) by instead propagating information from distant regions to the solver via heat diffusion, leveraging the backward uniqueness of the heat equation to guarantee optima preservation. The paper also distinguishes itself from denoising diffusion models (which require training and strict reverse-time scheduling), quantum adiabatic/bifurcation methods (whose smoothed optima can shift), and randomized smoothing (which lacks the optima-preservation guarantee), establishing a 'cooperative optimization' paradigm where non-monotonic τ schedules remain valid.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HeO provides a unified, training-free gradient-based framework applicable across a wide spectrum of combinatorial problems (QUBO, PUBO, ternary, mixed discrete-continuous, constrained) without requiring problem-specific quadratization or auxiliary variables, which is practically significant for high-order and mixed-variable settings. The cooperative optimization property—where the solver can freely transition between different diffusion times without degrading performance—opens the door to adaptive and non-monotonic schedules that could further accelerate convergence. Key limitations include inefficiency for integer linear programming and routing problems (due to Bernoulli encoding constraints), no theoretical guarantee of global convergence, and the current single-sample-per-iteration design that may limit precision on very hard instances. Open questions include whether preconditioning via a positive-definite matrix A in the heat equation, or hybridization with Metropolis-Hastings sampling, can extend applicability and improve convergence guarantees.
