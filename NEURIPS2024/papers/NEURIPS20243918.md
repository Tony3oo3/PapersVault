## Problem

The widely used Todorov (2005) algorithm for solving stochastic optimal control under multiplicative and internal noise relies on an unbiasedness assumption (E[x_t|x̂_t] = x̂_t) that provably does not hold, leading to suboptimal control and filter gains whenever internal noise is present or before full algorithmic convergence. This paper identifies the breakdown of the orthogonality principle as the root cause and demonstrates that the resulting suboptimality can reduce performance by up to 90% in sensorimotor reaching tasks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full LQMG system specification: linear dynamics matrices (A, B), observation matrix H, multiplicative noise scaling matrices (C, D), additive and internal noise covariances (Ω_ξ, Ω_ω, Ω_η), time-varying quadratic cost matrices (Q_t, R_t), and initial joint moments (μ_1, Σ_1) over a finite horizon T. It produces time-varying control gains L_t ∈ R^{p×m} and filter gains K_t ∈ R^{m×k} that define the linear control law u_t = L_t x̂_t and the Kalman-type update, along with the closed-form expected accumulated cost E[J]. No Gaussianity assumption on the joint state-estimate distribution is required, since the first two moments are sufficient statistics for the quadratic cost.

## Method

The key insight is to drop the unbiasedness assumption entirely and instead compute the expected cost E[J] in closed form by propagating only the first and second moments of the joint variable (x_t, x̂_t) forward in time via linear update equations (Eqs. 14–15), which hold regardless of the underlying distribution. The GD algorithm then minimizes E[J] with respect to all L_t and K_t via numerical gradient descent. For computational efficiency in high-dimensional settings, the FPOMP algorithm replaces numerical optimization with an analytical fixed-point iteration: at each step, it forward-propagates moments at fixed gains, then backward-solves the first-order optimality conditions (∂E[J]/∂L_t = 0, ∂E[J]/∂K_t = 0) to update gains in closed form, repeating until convergence. A geometric analysis via eigenvector decomposition of the moment-dynamics matrix reveals that the optimal solution jointly modulates L_t and K_t to increase the angle between eigenvectors, effectively decorrelating internal fluctuations from the latent state dynamics—a mechanism absent from the Todorov algorithm.

## Results

In a four-dimensional sensorimotor reaching task, the GD algorithm reduces the expected accumulated cost E[J] by up to 90% compared to the TOD algorithm when internal noise constitutes approximately 10% of total noise (σ_η = 0.05). The GD solution produces control gains that decrease monotonically with increasing internal noise, yielding smoother control signals, slower movements, and significantly reduced trial-to-trial variability (FA/RV ≈ 0.5 at σ_η = 0.05). In a high-dimensional task (m=10, p=4, k=10), GD continues to outperform TOD with the same qualitative trends. Computationally, the FPOMP analytical counterpart matches GD's optimal solutions exactly while running in minutes (comparable to TOD), versus ~4 hours for numerical GD on the 4D task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the TOD algorithm from Todorov (2005), the current state-of-the-art for LQMG optimal control. GD outperforms TOD on every tested scenario whenever internal noise is non-zero (σ_η > 0), with the performance gap widening as internal noise increases: in the 1D problem, GD achieves strictly lower E[J] for all σ_η > 0 (Fig. 2a), and in the 4D sensorimotor task the cost reduction reaches 90% at σ_η = 0.05 (Fig. 3a). The FPOMP analytical algorithm reproduces GD's optimal control and filter gains exactly (Figs. 2b,c) while being computationally comparable to TOD. Even in the absence of internal noise, GD outperforms TOD when the algorithm has not fully converged (e.g., with fixed suboptimal filter gains, Fig. 8), confirming that TOD's unbiasedness assumption is fundamentally flawed. Qualitatively, TOD's control gains show little sensitivity to internal noise magnitude, whereas GD's gains decrease appropriately, reflecting the correct intertwining of control and estimation that the orthogonality principle fails to capture under internal noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Todorov's (2005) seminal extension of the classic LQAG (Linear-Quadratic-Additive-Gaussian) optimal control framework to the LQMG model, which incorporates multiplicative (signal-dependent) noise in both motor output and sensory feedback, plus additive internal noise in the estimation process. The key departure is identifying a fundamental flaw in Todorov's derivation: the assumption of unbiased estimation (E[x_t|x̂_t] = x̂_t) does not hold, particularly when internal noise is present, violating the orthogonality principle that underpins the optimal Kalman filter. Rather than patching the derivation, the authors reformulate the problem by assuming only linearity of the control law, then compute the expected cost via closed-form moment propagation and minimize it numerically (gradient descent) or analytically (the FPOMP fixed-point algorithm), eliminating the need for the invalid unbiasedness condition entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Todorov (2005) algorithm is the de facto standard for inverse optimal control in sensorimotor neuroscience, underpinning a large body of behavioral modeling work (BCI design, motor adaptation, psychophysics). Demonstrating that it produces suboptimal solutions—by up to 90% in cost when internal noise is realistic—means that prior behavioral inferences based on this algorithm may be qualitatively wrong (e.g., predicting faster, more variable movements than the true optimum). Open questions include: whether the non-adaptive (state-independent) filter assumption remains a meaningful limitation under multiplicative noise, whether the FPOMP algorithm can be extended to the full multiplicative-noise case in multiple dimensions, and whether the predicted behavioral signatures (slower movements, reduced trial-to-trial variability) are actually observed in human data. The paper also does not formally prove convergence to a global minimum, though no local minima were found empirically.
