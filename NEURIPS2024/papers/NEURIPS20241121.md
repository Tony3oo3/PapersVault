## Problem

Neural Collapse theory establishes that the optimal final classifier converges to a Simplex Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF), yet prior approaches either fix the classifier to a predetermined ETF direction or learn it via gradient descent—neither accelerates convergence because the backbone must still align features to a fixed or slowly-evolving direction. The missing piece is dynamically identifying the *nearest* simplex ETF to the current feature geometry at every iteration, so the classifier is always optimally positioned relative to the features rather than requiring the network to discover the correct rotation.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 96×96 for STL-10, 224×224 for ImageNet) and produces class labels via softmax over logits. Unusually, the final classifier weights are not trainable parameters but are implicitly defined at each forward pass as W = M·U*, where U* is the solution to a Riemannian optimization over the Stiefel manifold that finds the simplex ETF direction closest to the current penultimate-layer feature means. Features are L2-normalized onto a hypersphere, and the bias is set to −M·U*·h_G to account for global feature centering.

## Method

At each training iteration, per-class feature means are computed (smoothed via an exponential moving average for stochastic-batch stability), centered, and normalized to unit Frobenius norm. A Riemannian trust-region solver (pyManopt) then minimizes the Frobenius distance between these normalized feature means and U·M̃ over the Stiefel manifold St_C^d, augmented with a proximal term to the previous U* to guarantee a unique solution and prevent oscillation between degenerate ETF directions. The resulting U* implicitly defines the classifier weights W = M·U*, eliminating the need to learn the last layer via gradient descent. Crucially, this inner optimization is encapsulated in a Deep Declarative Network node, so gradients from the cross-entropy loss propagate back through the Riemannian solution via the implicit function theorem, providing a second gradient path that steers backbone features toward the ETF geometry. The overall training is a bilevel problem: the outer loop minimizes cross-entropy with respect to backbone parameters θ, while the inner loop re-solves for U* at every step.

## Results

The Implicit ETF method achieves the best test top-1 accuracy at epoch 50 across most datasets, demonstrating significantly faster convergence: on ImageNet (ResNet-50) it reaches 63.40% vs. 52.64% (Standard) and 58.85% (Fixed ETF); on CIFAR-100 (ResNet-50) it reaches 48.38% vs. 45.91% and 45.37%. By epoch 200, all methods converge to similar performance levels, with Implicit ETF achieving 65.36% on ImageNet (vs. 60.02% Standard, 61.47% Fixed ETF) and 90.98% on CIFAR-10 (VGG-13, vs. 90.34% Standard, 72.48% Fixed ETF). The method also exhibits the smallest variance across five random seeds, indicating improved training stability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: a Standard learnable classifier and a Fixed ETF classifier (canonical simplex ETF direction, following Zhu et al. [76]). The Implicit ETF method decisively outperforms both at epoch 50 on ImageNet (63.40% vs. 52.64% Standard and 58.85% Fixed ETF, a +10.8 pp gain over Standard), CIFAR-100/ResNet-50 (48.38% vs. 45.91% and 45.37%), and CIFAR-10/VGG-13 (88.30% vs. 86.70% and 70.99%). By epoch 200, the Fixed ETF baseline suffers significant degradation on VGG-13 architectures (e.g., 72.48% on CIFAR-10 vs. 90.98% for Implicit ETF), while the Standard method remains competitive on smaller datasets. The one exception is STL-10/ResNet-50, where Fixed ETF slightly edges out Implicit ETF at epoch 200 (64.33% vs. 62.75%), though Implicit ETF still leads at epoch 50. Overall, the method's primary advantage is convergence speed rather than final accuracy, with all methods reaching similar asymptotic performance on most datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural Collapse line of work, which established that final classifier layers converge to Simplex Equiangular Tight Frames (ETFs) at the terminal phase of training. Prior approaches (Zhu et al., Yang et al.) fixed the classifier to a predetermined simplex ETF direction, reducing trainable parameters but leaving the backbone to do all the geometric alignment work. Here, the authors replace the fixed ETF with a dynamically computed nearest simplex ETF via a Riemannian optimisation on the Stiefel manifold, and embed this inner problem in a Deep Declarative Network node (Gould et al.) to enable end-to-end gradient flow. The method is thus a principled generalisation of the fixed-ETF classifier: the proximal term in the Riemannian objective recovers the fixed-ETF solution as a limiting case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical benefit is a consistent and meaningful convergence speedup (e.g., ~15% higher train accuracy at epoch 50 on CIFAR100/ResNet50) with markedly reduced variance across seeds, which is valuable for practitioners who want faster, more stable training without architectural changes. A key open question is the role of the DDN gradient stream: on small-scale datasets it appears optional, but on ImageNet the authors could not compute it due to memory constraints, leaving unclear whether the full bilevel formulation provides additional gains at scale. The computational overhead of the Riemannian solve and DDN backward pass (up to 70 GB for UFM-200) limits immediate adoption in very large settings, and the authors flag efficient DDN gradient computation as future work.
