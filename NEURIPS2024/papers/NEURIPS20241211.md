## Problem

Estimating the local intrinsic dimension (LID) of a data point is a longstanding problem with applications in outlier detection, adversarial example identification, and AI-generated content analysis, but existing estimators are either model-free and prohibitively expensive at scale, or model-based yet require training multiple generative models, rely on inferior architectures like normalizing flows, or remain computationally intractable in high dimensions. No prior method leverages the most powerful generative models—diffusion models—with a single pre-trained model, and none scale to the resolution of Stable Diffusion (~10⁶ pixel dimensions).

## Input / Output

FLIPD takes a single data point x (an image or synthetic sample), a pre-trained score-based diffusion model, and a scalar time parameter t₀ close to 0, and returns a scalar LID estimate. For high-resolution images (e.g., 512×512 from LAION-Aesthetics), the method operates in the latent space of Stable Diffusion (4×64×64 = 16,384 dimensions) rather than pixel space, using only k=1 Hutchinson sample for the trace approximation. The estimator is fully differentiable, enabling backpropagation through LID estimates, and requires no ODE solver or additional training—only a single forward pass through the score network.

## Method

FLIPD builds on LIDL's insight that the slope of log-density versus log-noise-standard-deviation estimates LID(x)−D, but replaces LIDL's m separately trained normalizing flows with a single pre-trained diffusion model by deriving a closed-form link (Equation 12) between the Gaussian-convolved density ρ(x,δ) and the DM's marginal density p(x,t). The key technical contribution is applying the Fokker-Planck equation to the forward SDE to obtain an explicit formula for ∂/∂δ log ρ(x,δ) = σ²(t(δ))[tr(∇s(ψ(t(δ))x, t(δ))) + ‖s(ψ(t(δ))x, t(δ))‖²], which directly yields the regression slope LIDL would estimate, eliminating the need for an ODE solver entirely. The final estimator FLIPD(x,t₀) = D + ν(t(δ₀); ŝ, x) requires only one evaluation of the score function and its Jacobian trace (approximated via the Hutchinson estimator with k≪D random vectors), giving a cost of Θ(kF) versus Θ(KF + D²K) for the competing NB estimator. A theoretical soundness result (Theorem 3.1) proves that in the linear (affine subspace) case, the estimator converges to the true LID as δ→−∞, and the method exhibits a multiscale property where different t₀ values capture different levels of geometric detail.

## Results

On synthetic LID benchmarks, FLIPD achieves a perfect concordance index (1.00) across all multi-manifold datasets, meaning it always correctly ranks data by true LID even when absolute estimates are imperfect. In terms of MAE, FLIPD is the best model-based estimator, particularly as dimension increases (e.g., MAE of 1.30 on U₁₀+U₃₀+U₉₀⊆ℝ¹⁰⁰ vs. 61.6 for NB and 8.46 for LIDL). On natural images, FLIPD achieves Spearman correlations of 0.82–0.88 with PNG compression size, far exceeding model-free baselines (0.01–0.44). FLIPD is also ~10× faster than NB on 28×28 images and ~100× faster on 32×32 images, and is the first LID estimator tractable at Stable Diffusion scale (0.20s per image vs. >9×10³s for NB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Normal Bundle (NB) estimator [Stanczuk et al., 2022], FLIPD dominates on both accuracy and speed: FLIPD achieves MAE of 1.30–3.93 on ℝ¹⁰⁰ mixtures versus NB's 61.6–74.2, and a perfect concordance index (1.00) versus NB's 0.34; FLIPD is also ~10–100× faster (0.10–0.13s vs. 1.6–10.8s per image). Against LIDL [Tempczyk et al., 2022], FLIPD wins on MAE in most scenarios (e.g., 1.81 vs. 8.87 on ℕ₁₀+ℕ₂₅+ℕ₅₀⊆ℝ¹⁰⁰) and achieves perfect CI (1.00) versus LIDL's 0.70–0.99, while requiring only a single pre-trained DM rather than 8 separately trained normalizing flows. Against model-free baselines ESS [Johnsson et al., 2014] and LPCA [Fukunaga & Olsen, 1971], FLIPD wins on MAE in most high-dimensional and non-linear settings (e.g., 12.8 vs. 14.5 and 219 on 𝒰₉₀₀⊆ℝ¹⁰⁰⁰), though LPCA and ESS can match or beat FLIPD on MAE in simpler low-dimensional or affine cases; however, FLIPD consistently achieves perfect concordance index where these methods do not. On natural images, FLIPD's Spearman correlation with PNG compression (0.82–0.88) substantially exceeds ESS (0.02–0.44) and LPCA (−0.01–0.41), while NB is competitive on MNIST/CIFAR-10 but degrades sharply on FMNIST (0.48) and SVHN (0.57). MLE [Levina & Bickel, 2004] and FIS [Albergante et al., 2019] are additionally compared in the appendix and are outperformed by FLIPD on pointwise LID estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LIDL (Tempczyk et al., 2022), which estimated LID by training multiple normalizing flows at different noise levels and regressing log-density against log-noise-standard-deviation. The key conceptual departure is recognizing that a single pre-trained diffusion model already encodes all noise levels, and that the Fokker-Planck equation provides a closed-form expression for the rate of change of log-density, eliminating the need for multiple ODE solves or multiple trained models. It also departs from the NB estimator (Stanczuk et al., 2022), which estimated LID via SVD of stacked score vectors and scales poorly in high dimensions, by reducing the computation to a single Hutchinson trace estimate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLIPD is the first LID estimator tractable at the scale of Stable Diffusion (~10^6 pixel dimensions), and is orders of magnitude faster than competing methods, making LID estimation practical for large-scale image and text analysis. Its differentiability opens new avenues for using LID as a regularizer or loss component during training. A notable limitation is sensitivity to network architecture (MLP vs. UNet) on natural images, and the theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) currently holds only for linear manifolds, leaving the nonlinear case as an open question. The paper also reveals that model-free estimators degrade severely as ambient dimension increases, motivating the use of generative-model-based approaches for high-dimensional data.
