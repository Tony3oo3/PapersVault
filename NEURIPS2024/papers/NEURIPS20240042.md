## Problem

Estimating counterfactual treatment responses over long horizons requires capturing long-term dependencies in time-varying confounders, but existing RNN-based models (RMSN, CRN, G-Net) struggle with long-range interactions, while transformer-based alternatives (Causal Transformer) are computationally expensive and hard to interpret. Additionally, prior models do not enforce invertibility of the learned representation, which undermines the identification assumptions underlying counterfactual estimation. This paper addresses both gaps by combining contrastive predictive coding for long-term dependency capture with an InfoMax principle for representation invertibility, all within a lightweight GRU backbone.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a patient's full history process H_{t+1} = [V, X_{≤t+1}, W_{≤t}, Y_{≤t}] — a sequence of static covariates, time-varying covariates (e.g., clinical measurements), past binary/multi-class treatments, and past outcomes — along with a hypothetical future treatment sequence ω_{t+1:t+τ}. It outputs the expected counterfactual outcome E(Y_{t+τ}(ω_{t+1:t+τ}) | H_{t+1}) for each horizon τ = 1, …, 10 (or 15), produced autoregressively by a GRU decoder without teacher forcing. The encoder operates on a single time step per batch element, and the decoder generates the full outcome trajectory sequentially, making prediction time scale linearly with τ rather than quadratically as in transformer-based baselines.

## Method

Causal CPC uses a two-stage training procedure over a simple 1-layer GRU backbone. In the encoder pretraining stage, local features Z_t are extracted per time step and summarized into a context C_t via an autoregressive GRU; the CPC loss (InfoNCE) is minimized across τ future horizons to maximize shared information between C_t and future local features, while an InfoMax contrastive loss between randomly split past/future subsequences of the history enforces that C_t is 'reconstructable' (i.e., the representation retains sufficient information to invert the encoding). In the decoder fine-tuning stage, a GRU decoder predicts factual outcomes autoregressively, and an adversarial game is played: a treatment classifier is trained to predict W_{t+1} from the representation, while the representation is updated to minimize the CLUB (Contrastive Log-ratio Upper Bound) of mutual information between the representation and treatment, provably yielding a treatment-invariant representation at the Nash equilibrium (Theorem 5.4). This information-theoretic balancing replaces the gradient-reversal or CDC losses used by prior methods, and the entire pipeline avoids transformers, achieving comparable or superior accuracy with roughly 7× faster prediction than Causal Transformer.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic MIMIC-III dataset (sequence length 100), Causal CPC achieves the lowest RMSE at most prediction horizons, reaching 0.77±0.10 at τ=10 compared to Causal Transformer (0.88±0.17), CRN (1.17±0.35), RMSN (1.18±0.13), and G-Net (1.49±0.16). The performance gap widens substantially at longer horizons: at τ=10, Causal CPC outperforms the next-best method (CT) by ~12% in RMSE. On the cancer simulation (PK-PD model, γ=1, sequence length 60), Causal CPC achieves NRMSE of 1.26±0.08 at τ=10, outperforming CT (1.35±0.16), CRN (1.43±0.26), and G-Net (1.70±0.25). Causal CPC also demonstrates superior computational efficiency, with prediction time of 4±1 min versus 30±3 min for CT and 35±3 min for G-Net.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Causal CPC outperforms all five baselines at most prediction horizons (τ≥3) on both the semi-synthetic MIMIC-III and cancer simulation datasets, with the advantage growing at longer horizons. Against Causal Transformer (CT) [48], the closest competitor, Causal CPC achieves 12% lower RMSE at τ=10 on MIMIC-III (0.77 vs. 0.88) and 7% lower NRMSE on cancer simulation (1.26 vs. 1.35), while also being ~7.5× faster at prediction (4 min vs. 30 min). Against CRN [7], Causal CPC wins at all horizons except τ=1 on MIMIC-III where CRN is marginally better (0.27 vs. 0.32), but the gap widens dramatically at τ=10 (0.77 vs. 1.17). Against G-Net [39] and RMSN [41], Causal CPC consistently dominates across all horizons, with G-Net showing the largest degradation at long horizons (RMSE 1.49 at τ=10). MSM [64] is excluded from most comparisons due to prohibitively high prediction errors (NRMSE >3.0 at τ=10 on cancer simulation). The ablation study confirms that both the CPC (InfoNCE) and InfoMax regularization terms contribute meaningfully, with removing InfoMax causing the largest performance drop (NRMSE 0.74 vs. 0.62 on MIMIC-III).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) from the self-supervised representation learning literature into the causal inference setting, a domain where it had not previously been applied. It builds on the adversarial balancing framework of CRN and Causal Transformer but replaces their gradient-reversal or CDC-based balancing with an information-theoretic objective (CLUB upper bound on MI between representation and treatment), providing a Nash-equilibrium guarantee (Theorem 5.4) that prior methods lacked. The InfoMax regularization for encoder invertibility draws on Deep InfoMax but is adapted to the sequential, multi-view structure of longitudinal process histories. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against the recent trend toward transformer-based causal models (Causal Transformer), arguing that a simple GRU backbone with well-designed contrastive and information-theoretic regularizations can match or exceed transformer performance on long-horizon counterfactual prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that long-horizon counterfactual regression does not require computationally expensive transformer architectures, with Causal CPC achieving comparable or better accuracy at roughly 1/7th the prediction time of Causal Transformer. This is particularly relevant for clinical deployment where multiple counterfactual trajectories must be evaluated per patient. Key limitations include reliance on sequential ignorability (tested via a falsifiability experiment), inconsistent short-horizon performance, and evaluation on relatively small datasets (≤1000 training sequences). Open questions include extension to continuous treatments, uncertainty quantification for longitudinal causal predictions, and whether the contrastive regularization advantage persists at larger data scales where transformers may recover their edge.
