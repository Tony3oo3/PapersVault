## Problem

In online learning, a decision maker repeatedly selects actions under an adversarial loss sequence, and the standard minimax regret is Θ(√T) in full feedback. This paper asks how much regret can be reduced when the learner is additionally allowed to issue at most k best-action queries to an oracle that reveals the optimal action at that step — a model motivated by expensive human reviews in content moderation. The key open question is the exact minimax regret as a function of both T and k, and whether a sublinear number of queries yields only additive or genuinely multiplicative gains.

## Input / Output

At each time step t, the learner observes a loss vector ℓ_t ∈ [0,1]^n (full feedback) or only the losses at the k query steps (label-efficient feedback), and must select one of n actions. The learner is given a query budget k ≤ T; when a query is issued, the oracle reveals argmin_i ℓ_t(i) before the learner commits, and the learner selects that action. The output is a sequence of action selections, and the performance metric is worst-case regret against the best fixed action in hindsight. In the label-efficient variant, no loss information is available at non-query steps, making the feedback structure fundamentally different from standard bandit or full-feedback settings.

## Method

The core algorithm (Algorithm 1) combines the Hedge (multiplicative-weights) algorithm with a uniform random query schedule: k time steps are sampled uniformly, and at those steps the oracle-revealed best action is selected while at all other steps Hedge plays its usual distribution. The key analytical insight is that the additive loss term (k/T)·L_T^min from query steps interacts with the negative term in Hedge's standard bound to permit a more aggressive learning rate η = max{√(log n/T), k/T}, converting what would be an additive O(k) improvement into a multiplicative regret reduction to O(min{√T, T/k}). For label-efficient feedback, a Bernoulli-query variant of Hedge with rescaled losses achieves O(min{T/√k, T²/k²}), improving over the standard Θ(T/√k) label-efficient rate when k ≥ Ω(T^{2/3}). Tight lower bounds are established via Yao's minimax theorem on a two-arm stochastic instance, using the Bretagnolle-Huber inequality to bound the KL divergence between the two hypothesis distributions and showing no algorithm can beat the stated rates.

## Results

In the full feedback model, the paper establishes a tight minimax regret of Θ(min{√T, T/k}) for k best-action queries, improving over the standard Θ(√T) rate without queries. In the label-efficient feedback model (feedback only at query times), the tight regret is Θ(min{T/√k, T²/k²}), which improves over the standard Θ(T/√k) label-efficient rate when k ≥ Ω(T^{2/3}). The key finding is that even a sublinear number of queries (e.g., k = T^{2/3}) yields a multiplicative improvement in the regret rate (from √T to T^{1/3} in full feedback), rather than merely an additive O(k) reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against standard online learning algorithms without any query access. In full feedback, the standard Hedge algorithm achieves Θ(√T) regret; with k = T^{2/3} queries, this paper's algorithm reduces regret to T^{1/3}, a multiplicative gain of T^{1/6}. In the label-efficient setting, the standard label-efficient Hedge (Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006) achieves Θ(T/√k) regret where k is the number of feedback steps; this paper shows that k = T^{3/4} best-action queries (with feedback only at those steps) reduce regret to O(√T), a √T improvement. The paper also distinguishes itself from Bhaskara et al. (2023b), who study comparison queries over small subsets of actions at every time step (yielding O(1) regret for 2-arm comparisons), whereas here queries are over all n actions but limited to k total uses. The lower bounds (Theorems 3.3, 3.4) are tight and hold even in the stochastic i.i.d. setting, confirming that no algorithm can beat these rates regardless of strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces best-action queries into online learning, a query type distinct from prior work on correlated vector hints (Dekel et al., 2017; Bhaskara et al., 2021b) and comparison queries over action subsets (Bhaskara et al., 2023b). The algorithms are built by augmenting the classical Hedge algorithm (Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006) with uniform random querying, and in the stochastic i.i.d. setting, by combining Follow-The-Leader (Auer et al., 2003) and Explore-Then-Commit (Perchet et al., 2015) with query access. The key departure from prior hint/query models is that best-action queries are purely ordinal (revealing the argmin action) and the learner is constrained to a fixed budget k, whereas prior work either allowed unlimited queries or operated over strongly convex domains where logarithmic regret was achievable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tight minimax bounds establish that a sublinear number of best-action queries (k = T^α for α > 1/2) yields a multiplicative improvement in regret rate rather than merely an additive O(k) gain, which is a non-trivial structural insight about how oracle information interacts with multiplicative-weights learning. The lower bounds hold even in the stochastic i.i.d. setting, ruling out the possibility that the adversarial analysis is overly pessimistic. Open questions include extending to bandit feedback, partial monitoring, and feedback graphs (where the key Hedge analysis lemma does not apply), as well as studying noisy oracles that identify the best action only with probability 1/n + δ rather than perfectly.
