## Problem

When multiple agents with different reward functions must share a single policy in a Markov decision process, the natural approach of summing rewards and optimizing the aggregate is sensitive to arbitrary affine rescalings of individual rewards, making it ill-defined. The paper asks how to aggregate heterogeneous ordinal preferences over policies into a collective policy in a way that is invariant to such rescalings, drawing on social choice theory to provide principled, fairness-guaranteeing aggregation rules.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multi-objective MDP (finite state set, action set, transition kernel) together with n reward functions, one per agent, and normalizes each so that expected returns lie in [0,1]. It outputs a point in the state-action occupancy polytope — equivalently, a stochastic policy — that satisfies a chosen fairness or voting criterion. Because the space of alternatives is continuous (the polytope), the method reasons about volumes of subsets rather than counting discrete candidates, and all algorithms are expressed in terms of calls to a polytope volume oracle.

## Method

The key insight is to reinterpret ordinal preferences over policies as volumes of subsets of the state-action occupancy polytope: an agent's 'rank' of a policy is the fraction of the polytope's volume that the agent prefers to it. For fairness, the paper extends the proportional veto core (via sequential hyperplane cuts eliminating 1/n of the polytope per agent) and proves that a (1/e)-quantile fair policy always exists — a tight bound that is twice as strong as the discrete analogue — using Grünbaum's inequality on the polytope centroid. For voting, it adapts α-approval and Borda count by replacing discrete rank counts with the CDF of each agent's expected-return distribution over the polytope; since exact computation is NP-hard (via reductions from Maximum Independent Set and MAX-2SAT), the paper formulates both rules as mixed-integer linear programs with one binary variable per agent (or per ε-bucket for Borda), yielding practical solutions. All mechanisms return a Pareto-optimal policy among those satisfying the chosen criterion, and the entire pipeline relies on a fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme for polytope volume as a subroutine.

## Results

In the 5-symmetric-agent scenario, max-quantile achieves the lowest Gini index (0.0188 ± 0.0022), indicating the most equitable distribution of normalized returns, while Borda achieves the highest Nash welfare (0.5356 ± 0.0062). In the 10-agent random-subset scenario, max-quantile again achieves the lowest Gini index (0.0625 ± 0.0067), and Borda leads on Nash welfare (0.6801 ± 0.0261). The utilitarian rule performs worst on fairness, with a Gini index of 0.4392 in the 5-agent setting and Nash welfare of only 0.0502, confirming its sensitivity to reward scale differences. The paper's theoretical guarantee that a (1/e)-quantile fair policy always exists (Theorem 2) is empirically validated, with the max-quantile algorithm finding policies well above this worst-case bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six aggregation rules: utilitarian (maximize sum of returns), egalitarian (maximize minimum return lexicographically), α-approval (α = 0.8, 0.9), Borda count, and max-quantile fairness. On the Gini index (fairness), max-quantile clearly outperforms all other rules in both scenarios, achieving 0.0188 vs. 0.0225 for Borda and 0.4392 for utilitarian in the 5-agent setting. On Nash welfare, Borda and max-quantile are nearly tied in the 5-agent scenario (0.5356 vs. 0.5355), while Borda leads in the 10-agent scenario (0.6801 vs. 0.6474). The utilitarian rule is the worst performer on both fairness metrics in the 5-agent setting, confirming the paper's argument that cardinal welfare maximization is sensitive to affine reward transformations. The 90%-approval rule provides a strong middle ground, with Gini indices close to Borda and Nash welfare competitive with max-quantile. Overall, the experiments identify max-quantile as the most appealing rule for fairness, while Borda offers a slightly better welfare-fairness tradeoff.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges social choice theory and multi-objective reinforcement learning by reinterpreting ordinal voting and fairness rules through the volumetric geometry of the state-action occupancy polytope. It directly extends Chaudhury et al.'s (ICML 2024) volumetric proportional veto core from federated learning to MDP policy aggregation, and generalizes Babichenko et al.'s (STOC 2024) discrete quantile fairness to the continuous polytope setting. Crucially, it departs from the dominant MORL and fair-RL literature (Ogryczak et al., Siddique et al., Mandal & Gan, Fan et al., Ju et al.) which relies on cardinal social welfare functions sensitive to affine reward transformations, instead adopting ordinal methods that are invariant to such rescalings—a property the authors argue is essential given the impossibility of interpersonal utility comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, affine-invariant framework for aggregating heterogeneous human preferences into a collective policy in sequential decision-making, directly addressing a core challenge in pluralistic AI alignment. The theoretical improvement from (1/2e) to (1/e) quantile fairness in the continuous setting, and the elegant equivalence between q-quantile fairness and q-approximation of Borda score, offer clean guarantees that discrete analogues lack. Key open questions include scaling beyond thousands of agents/states/actions, extending to continuous state or action spaces, handling online/unknown environments, and addressing the strategic manipulation concerns raised by the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem in this volumetric setting.
