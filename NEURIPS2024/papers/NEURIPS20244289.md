## Problem

Existing membership inference attacks (MIAs) against LLMs suffer from two critical practical limitations: reference-based attacks like LiRA require a reference dataset closely matching the training distribution (which adversaries cannot realistically obtain), and both reference-free and reference-based attacks rely on overfitting as the membership signal, which is typically mitigated by regularization in deployed LLMs. This leads to high false-positive rates and AUC scores barely above random guessing in realistic fine-tuning scenarios where models exhibit memorization but not overfitting.

## Input / Output

The adversary needs access to a Query API (returning generated texts and logits/loss for a given prompt) and a Fine-tuning API (to fine-tune the pre-trained base model), both commonly provided by commercial LLM providers or available for open-source models. The method consumes a target text record, its probability under the target LLM, the probability under a self-prompt reference model, and N pairs of symmetrical paraphrased texts (generated via T5-based semantic paraphrasing or Gaussian-noise embedding perturbation). The output is a calibrated probabilistic variation score Δp̃(x) = p̃_θ(x) − p̃_θ̂(x), thresholded to produce a binary member/non-member decision.

## Method

SPV-MIA combines two modules: (1) Practical Difficulty Calibration (PDC), which constructs a reference dataset by prompting the target LLM itself with short text chunks and collecting its generated outputs, then fine-tuning a reference model on this self-prompt data—eliminating the need for the actual training set; and (2) Probabilistic Variation Assessment (PVA), which replaces the raw probability signal with a second-order directional derivative metric (approximated via symmetrical paraphrasing) that detects whether a text sits at a local maximum in the model's probability distribution, capturing memorization rather than overfitting. The theoretical foundation draws on the observation that LLM memorization manifests as augmented probability concentration around member records, formalized through the Hessian matrix and the second partial derivative test. The final attack score is the difference in probabilistic variation between the target and self-prompt reference models. The paper further shows that the Neighbour Attack is a special case of probabilistic variation when the symmetry constraint is relaxed, providing a unified theoretical explanation for its effectiveness.

## Results

SPV-MIA achieves an average AUC of 0.924 across four LLMs (GPT-2, GPT-J, Falcon-7B, LLaMA-7B) and three datasets (Wikitext-103, AG News, XSum), substantially outperforming the best baseline LiRA-Candidate (avg. AUC 0.797) by approximately 30%. At the stricter TPR@1%FPR metric, SPV-MIA reaches an average of 46.9%, compared to 15.8% for LiRA-Candidate and below 4% for all reference-free attacks. The method also demonstrates robustness: even with only 1,000 API queries or 8-token prompts, AUC remains above 0.9, and DP-SGD with ε=15 still leaves SPV-MIA at 0.774 AUC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPV-MIA dominates all six baselines across every LLM-dataset pair. Against the strongest reference-based baseline LiRA-Candidate (which assumes access to a domain-specific dataset for its reference model), SPV-MIA improves average AUC from 0.797 to 0.924 (+16 absolute points, ~30% relative gain), while requiring only short text chunks rather than a full auxiliary dataset. Against the reference-free baselines (Loss Attack, Neighbour Attack, DetectGPT, Min-K%, Min-K%++), the gap is even more dramatic: these methods cluster around 0.57–0.64 AUC (barely above random), whereas SPV-MIA reaches 0.92–0.98. At the stricter TPR@1%FPR metric, SPV-MIA achieves 38.7–61.1% versus 10.9–15.8% for LiRA-Candidate and 1–4% for reference-free attacks. Notably, reference-free methods degrade on larger LLMs (e.g., Loss Attack drops from 0.614 on GPT-2 to 0.577 on GPT-J for Wiki), while SPV-MIA maintains or improves with scale, confirming that stronger models are more vulnerable to memorization-based attacks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reference-based MIA framework of LiRA (Mireshghallah et al., 2022) and the difficulty calibration principle of Watson et al. (2022) by replacing the ideal same-distribution reference dataset with a self-prompt dataset generated by the target LLM itself, making the approach feasible in practical settings. It departs from the probability-based membership signal used by all prior MIAs (which implicitly requires overfitting) by introducing a probabilistic variation metric grounded in the memorization theory of van den Burg & Williams (2021), approximated via symmetrical paraphrasing. The paper also provides a theoretical reformulation of the Neighbour Attack (Mattern et al., 2023) as a relaxed special case of probabilistic variation, unifying two previously separate lines of work under a common Hessian-based framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that existing MIAs against LLMs are fundamentally misaligned with practical deployment: they assume overfitting (which is typically avoided) and require an identical-distribution reference dataset (which is infeasible to obtain). By reframing the membership signal around memorization—which is inevitable and persists throughout training—and by making the reference model constructible via self-prompting, SPV-MIA reveals that privacy risks in fine-tuned LLMs are substantially higher than previously measured. Key limitations include restriction to causal language models (not MLMs or Seq2Seq), dependence on two API accesses (query + fine-tuning), and the finding that DP-SGD with moderate privacy budgets (ε=15–60) still leaves AUC above 0.77, suggesting current defenses are insufficient. Open questions include adaptation to other LLM architectures and whether the memorization-based signal generalizes to pre-training data detection at scale.
