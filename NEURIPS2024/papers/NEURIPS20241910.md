## Problem

Generating multiple diverse yet high-quality responses from foundation models remains difficult: temperature sampling trades accuracy for diversity and is incompatible with greedy decoding, while a single fine-tuned model produces near-identical outputs. The growing availability of large-scale synthetic instruction datasets (e.g., OSS-Instruct, Platypus) is underexploited, since fine-tuning one model on the full dataset yields diminishing returns beyond ~15% of the data. The paper asks whether this surplus synthetic data can be repurposed to elicit genuinely diverse responses without quality loss.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single user text prompt (e.g., a code-generation query or an NLU question) and produces K diverse responses (K=8 in experiments) by randomly selecting one of K LoRA adapters and generating one output from it. The training input is an existing synthetic instruction dataset (75K code QA pairs from OSS-Instruct or ~25K QA pairs from Platypus) plus M hand-written test queries (12 per domain, 8 selected by variance) that serve as partitioning anchors. Outputs are full code solutions or free-form text continuations, evaluated with pass@k for code and diversity/KL metrics for NLU.

## Method

SPA (Synthesize-Partition-Adapt) partitions a synthetic instruction dataset into K subsets using data attribution scores, then trains one LoRA adapter per subset. Concretely, the base model is first fine-tuned on the full synthetic set with LoRA; influence function scores (via conjugate-gradient Hessian-vector products) are then computed for every training point against M hand-written test queries, yielding an N×M influence matrix. A ranking heuristic assigns each training example to the test query with the highest influence score, producing K disjoint subsets that capture distinct behavioral aspects of the data. Each subset trains an independent LoRA adapter (rank 16, 400 steps), and at inference a random adapter is sampled to generate one response, enabling diversity even under greedy decoding. The framework is attribution-agnostic (BM25 lexical overlap is also evaluated) and exploits the observation that the marginal utility of additional synthetic data for a single adapter saturates quickly, making the surplus data available for specialization.

## Results

On HumanEval, SPA with influence function achieves a diversity score of 0.85 and average KL divergence of 0.017, the highest among all methods, while maintaining a pass@1 of 50.15% comparable to the single-adaptation baseline (50.02%). On MBPP, the same method reaches a diversity score of 0.78, average KL of 0.020, and pass@5 of 73.68%, again the best in diversity and sample quality. All multi-adaptation methods use greedy decoding (τ=0), whereas the single-adaptation baseline requires τ=0.1 to produce any diversity. On NLU tasks (BBH, GPQA, MMLU, WinoGrande), SPA with influence function consistently outperforms both lexical overlap and random partitioning in diversity score and average KL divergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four methods: a single LoRA adaptation trained on the full dataset (Single, τ=0.1), multiple adaptations on randomly partitioned subsets (Random, τ=0), multiple adaptations partitioned by BM25 lexical overlap (Lexical, τ=0), and the proposed SPA with influence function (Influence, τ=0). SPA with influence function wins on all diversity metrics: it achieves 0.85 diversity vs. 0.78 (Lexical) and 0.69 (Random) on HumanEval, and 0.78 vs. 0.71 and 0.64 on MBPP. It also achieves the highest pass@5 (69.05% on HumanEval, 73.68% on MBPP), indicating that greater diversity does not come at the cost of sample quality. The pass@1 scores are essentially tied across all methods (50.02–50.30 on HumanEval, 60.15–60.65 on MBPP), confirming that partitioning does not degrade per-sample accuracy. Lexical overlap consistently outperforms random partitioning, suggesting that even simple attribution signals help, but influence function provides a meaningful additional boost of roughly 7–9 percentage points in diversity score over the next-best method.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPA repurposes influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) — originally a data attribution tool for understanding model behavior — as a partitioning signal for synthetic instruction-tuning data, a use case not previously explored. The framework combines this with LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2021) to train multiple specialized adaptations, departing from the dominant paradigm of using inference-time sampling (temperature, top-k, top-p) to elicit diversity. Conceptually, SPA shifts the diversity problem from the decoding stage to the post-training stage, leveraging the observation that diminishing returns in fine-tuning on ever-larger synthetic datasets (Fig. 2) create an opportunity to partition data and train multiple models rather than one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is significant as synthetic instruction datasets continue to grow: rather than wasting compute fine-tuning a single model on redundant data, practitioners can partition and train multiple LoRA adapters that collectively offer diverse yet high-quality outputs, even under greedy decoding. Key limitations include the substantial offline cost of influence function computation (hours per column on A100 GPUs) and the serving overhead of maintaining multiple LoRA adapters in production. Open questions include whether more efficient attribution methods (TRAK, K-FAC) can reduce the partitioning cost, whether more principled clustering (beyond the ranking heuristic) yields better partitions, and whether the approach generalizes to domains where synthetic data is less abundant or less diverse.
