## Problem

Most CFR variants (CFR+, PCFR, DCFR) that accelerate convergence require full game tree traversal, making them inapplicable under Monte Carlo sampling conditions needed for large-scale games like Texas Hold'em (10^162 information sets). The paper asks how to design an MC-compatible algorithm that exploits the high proportion of dominated strategies in large games to converge faster than existing MCCFR variants.

## Input / Output

The algorithm operates on an extensive-form game tree with information sets, consuming the current game state, player behavioral strategies, and chance node probabilities at each node. At each information set, it accumulates counterfactual values Q_t^i(I,a) over iterations and outputs a pure best-response strategy (arg max over Q values) rather than a mixed regret-matching distribution. The final output is the time-averaged strategy profile converging to a Nash equilibrium, with exploitability as the convergence metric in two-player settings and mutual battle rewards in multiplayer settings.

## Method

MCCFVFP replaces CFR's regret-matching strategy update with a best-response strategy while retaining the counterfactual value framework. Specifically, it accumulates counterfactual values Q_t^i(I,a) = Q_{t-1}^i(I,a) + π_{σ_t}^{-i}(I)·u^i(I, σ_t|_{I→a}) (omitting the average payoff term that doesn't affect arg max), then sets the next strategy to σ_{t+1}^i(I) = arg max_a Q_t^i(I,a), yielding a pure strategy. This pure-strategy property makes π_{σ_t}^{-i}(I) binary (0 or 1), dramatically increasing naive pruning efficiency so that only O(|S|^{1/N}) nodes need traversal versus O(|S|) for CFR. Per information set, the computation drops from 6x−2 additions and 3x multiplications (CFR) to just 2x+1 additions (~2/9 the cost). Convergence to NE is guaranteed via Blackwell approachability, and the algorithm is combined with external sampling MCCFR to form the full MCCFVFP procedure.

## Results

In two-player Texas Hold'em (89k information sets), MCCFVFP achieves an exploitability of 0.289 BB/100 after 7246s of training, compared to 0.358 BB/100 for MCCFR at the same time — a ~20% improvement. In 6-player Texas Hold'em (338k information sets), MCCFVFP gains 0.935 BB/100 over MCCFR in head-to-head battles after 32s of training, substantially exceeding Pluribus's reported 3.2 BB/100 advantage over human players. Across Kuhn-extension, Leduc-extension, and Princess-and-Monster games, MCCFVFP consistently converges 20–50% faster than ES-MCCFR in wall-clock time, while requiring only ~2/9 of the per-information-set computation. As game scale increases, sampling-based algorithms (MCCFVFP, MCCFR) surpass full-traversal methods (CFR+, DCFR, PCFR) in convergence speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MCCFVFP is compared against six baselines: CFR, CFR+, DCFR/DMCCFR, PCFR, ES-MCCFR, and MCCFR. In small-scale games (vanilla Kuhn), full-traversal methods like DCFR and PCFR can outperform both MCCFR and MCCFVFP, but as game scale grows (e.g., 15C5P3L Kuhn, 15C5P Leduc), sampling-based MCCFVFP clearly surpasses all full-traversal baselines. Against its primary baseline ES-MCCFR, MCCFVFP wins in every tested scenario: in two-player Texas Hold'em it reaches 0.289 BB/100 exploitability vs. 0.358 for MCCFR at 7246s (~20% faster), and in 6-player Texas Hold'em it gains 0.935 BB/100 in head-to-head battles after 32s of training. The per-node computational advantage (2/9 the cost of MCCFR per information set) means that even when MCCFVFP touches a similar number of nodes, its wall-clock time is roughly half that of MCCFR. In the 30–40s training regime that mirrors Pluribus's setup, MCCFVFP's 0.932 BB/100 advantage over MCCFR in 6-player Hold'em is particularly notable given that Pluribus itself only achieved 3.2 BB/100 against human opponents. The paper does not report any scenario where MCCFVFP loses to MCCFR, though it acknowledges theoretically that in 'tangled games' (high proportion of non-dominated strategies) the advantage could diminish.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-established lines of work: Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR) and its Monte Carlo variant (MCCFR) for solving extensive-form imperfect information games, and Fictitious Play (FP) with its best-response strategy. The key conceptual departure is replacing regret matching (RM) with a pure best-response (BR) strategy within the CFR framework, yielding CFVFP. The authors justify this substitution through Blackwell approachability, showing that FP's BR strategy satisfies the same convergence guarantees as RM. The 'clear game' taxonomy (high proportion of dominated strategies) provides a new lens for understanding why BR outperforms RM in large-scale games, a distinction not previously formalized in the CFR literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 20–50% convergence speedup over MCCFR is practically significant for training blueprint strategies in large-scale games like Texas Hold'em, where training budgets are enormous (billions of nodes). The 2/9 per-node computational cost reduction is a concrete engineering win. However, the central assumption—that large-scale games are 'clear games' with a high proportion of dominated strategies—is a conjecture supported by anecdotal evidence (Pluribus, DeepMind's spinning-top analogy) rather than rigorous proof, and the convergence rate O(L|A|/√T) is theoretically worse than RM's O(L√|A|/T) in the worst case. Open questions include whether the advantage persists in genuinely 'tangled' games, compatibility with deep-network-based CFR variants, and the authors' proposed warm-start strategy that switches from MCCFVFP to other algorithms after dominated strategies are pruned.
