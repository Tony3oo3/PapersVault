## Problem

Conformal prediction guarantees valid marginal coverage but offers no control over conditional coverage or interval length, and existing score functions (Local, CQR) impose structural constraints that limit how well intervals adapt to heteroskedasticity. Prior work addressing these gaps (e.g., CF-GNN, ConTr) requires retraining or fine-tuning the base model, which is computationally expensive and demands access to model internals. This paper asks whether one can improve conformal intervals post-training, using only model predictions, without modifying the trained model.

## Input / Output

The procedure consumes a pre-trained model's predictions on a dataset of (X, Y) pairs, a base conformity score function (Local or CQR type), and a target miscoverage level α; it does not require access to model parameters or the training algorithm. It outputs a boosted conformity score function E^(τ)(·,·) and, after standard split-conformal calibration, conformalized prediction intervals that preserve the original marginal coverage guarantee. The boosting operates on tabular data with features X ∈ ℝ^p and continuous labels Y ∈ ℝ, and the number of boosting rounds τ is selected via k-fold cross-validation on the training set.

## Method

The key insight is to define generalized score families — F for Local-type scores |y−μ(x)|/σ(x) and H for CQR-type scores max{μ₁(x)−y, y−μ₂(x)}/σ(x) — that strictly contain the original scores and provably include the oracle score achieving exact conditional coverage or minimum-length HDR intervals. Within these families, gradient boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM) iteratively updates the score components (μ, σ) or (μ₁, μ₂, σ) by minimizing a task-specific differentiable loss: for conditional coverage, a log-sum-exp approximation of the maximum within-group deviation measured by a contrast tree; for length, a smooth approximation of average interval length using a differentiable Harrell-Davis quantile estimator. The number of boosting rounds is selected by cross-validation that mimics the calibration step, preventing overfitting of the score function. Because boosting is applied only to the training set, the resulting intervals retain the standard split-conformal marginal coverage guarantee (Proposition 3.1), and the procedure is fully post-training, requiring only model predictions rather than model retraining.

## Results

On conditional coverage (Table 1, α=10%), boosting the Local score reduces maximum deviation from target coverage by up to 63.17% (meps-19: 15.36% → 5.66%) and 47.71% (community: 25.76% → 13.47%), while boosting CQR yields negligible gains (≤7.29%) since CQR already targets conditional coverage. On average interval length (Table 2, α=10%), boosting Local achieves reductions of up to 52.74% (blog: 2.056 → 0.972) and 27.03% (facebook-1: 1.896 → 1.383), while boosting CQR reduces length by up to 18.55% (meps-21: 2.585 → 2.105) and 16.35% (meps-19: 2.554 → 2.136). Marginal coverage is preserved at the target 90% level across all boosted intervals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Local baseline [16], the boosted procedure delivers dramatic gains on conditional coverage: maximum deviation drops by 48–63% on the meps and community datasets, and by 18–49% on bike and concrete. On interval length, boosted Local outperforms unboosted Local by 12–53% across all six reported datasets. Against the CQR baseline [18], the picture is more nuanced: boosting CQR yields only marginal conditional-coverage improvements (≤8.6%) because CQR already implicitly targets this property, but it does reduce average interval length by 10–19% on the meps and facebook datasets. Notably, after boosting, the Local procedure's conditional coverage becomes comparable to (and in two cases slightly better than) the boosted CQR procedure, closing the gap that was up to 3× before boosting. The paper also notes that boosted Local intervals can be substantially longer than boosted CQR intervals when optimizing for coverage (e.g., community: 10.76 vs. 1.74), reflecting the trade-off between the two objectives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the split conformal prediction framework by introducing a post-training gradient boosting stage that iteratively refines the conformity score function, rather than modifying the underlying model as in CF-GNN [28] or ConTr [26]. It builds on the locally adaptive (Local) score [16] and conformalized quantile regression (CQR) [18, 23] by embedding them within broader generalized score families (F and H) that are provably expressive enough to contain oracle scores. The loss functions for conditional coverage leverage contrast trees [21] to identify worst-case subgroups, while smooth Harrell-Davis quantile estimators [1] and sigmoid approximations make the otherwise non-differentiable conformal calibration step amenable to gradient-based optimization via XGBoost [12] or LightGBM [15].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The procedure is practically attractive because it operates entirely on model predictions without requiring access to or modification of the trained model, making it applicable to any black-box predictor. Theoretical guarantees (Propositions 5.1, A.2) confirm that the generalized score families can recover both exact conditional coverage and minimum-length oracle intervals asymptotically. Key open questions include simultaneously optimizing length and conditional coverage with user-specified trade-offs, extending to classification and fairness-aware settings, and whether alternative gradient-based architectures (e.g., neural networks) could outperform tree-based boosting. A notable limitation is that optimizing for one objective (e.g., length) can degrade the other (e.g., conditional coverage), as seen in the blog dataset where length optimization worsened coverage deviation.
