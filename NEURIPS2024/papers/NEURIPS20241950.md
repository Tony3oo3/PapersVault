## Problem

Existing deep feature-based image descriptors are inflexible: they either depend on human-labeled opinion scores or are designed to evaluate fixed image characteristics. The paper identifies an unexplored property of deep features—their varying response to degradation across different image content and textures—and asks whether this response can serve as a flexible, task-adaptable image descriptor without any training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241950/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a set of text prompt pairs (e.g., 'A blurry photo with low-quality' vs. 'A sharp photo with high-quality') that define the degradation type. For IQA, it outputs a scalar quality score computed as the mean cosine distance between original and text-degraded CLIP ViT-B/32 features across a task-specific degradation set D. For restoration, DDR is used as a differentiable loss term during training, and the output is a restored image at 128×128 resolution. No pixel-domain degradation is ever synthesized; all degradation modeling occurs in the feature space.

## Method

DDR quantifies the change in CLIP image features when a text-driven degradation is fused in the feature domain. A pair of prompts (degraded and clean descriptions) is encoded by CLIP's text encoder, and their difference yields a degradation direction vector. An AdaIN-style adaptation aligns the mean and variance of this vector to match the image feature statistics, bridging the text-image modality gap. The adapted degradation is then added directly to the image feature to produce the degraded feature, and DDR is the cosine distance between original and degraded features. For BIQA, DDR is averaged over a degradation set {color, noise, blur, exposure}; for restoration, DDR is maximized as an unsupervised objective alongside a PSNR reconstruction loss, with the degradation set tailored to the task (e.g., {color, content, blur} for deblurring and SISR). The entire pipeline is zero-shot for IQA and requires no additional training parameters for the descriptor itself.

## Results

On the opinion-unaware blind image quality assessment (OU-BIQA) task, DDR achieves the highest SRCC on 7 out of 8 datasets, with particularly strong gains on in-the-wild data: 0.6613 on LIVE in-the-wild (vs. 0.5060 for the best baseline ContentSep) and 0.8009 on CID2013 (vs. 0.7698 for NPQI). In image deblurring, adding DDR as an auxiliary loss to PSNR improves PSNR by at least 0.16 dB across both NAFNet and Restormer on GoPro and RealBlur (e.g., 33.34 vs. 33.17 dB for NAFNet on GoPro). For real-world SISR, DDR yields the best PSNR of 27.31 dB (NAFNet) and 28.17 dB (Restormer), outperforming all feature-domain loss alternatives (LPIPS, CTX, PDL, FDL).

## Baselines & Comparisons

In OU-BIQA, DDR (zero-shot, no training) outperforms all nine baselines on 7 of 8 datasets; the sole exception is the LIVE dataset where dipIQ (0.9378) and ILNIQE (0.8975) exceed DDR (0.8793), likely because LIVE contains only synthetic degradations where handcrafted features are well-calibrated. The largest margins appear on in-the-wild datasets: DDR surpasses ContentSep by 0.155 on LIVE in-the-wild and by 0.031 on CID2013. In image deblurring (Tables 4–5), DDR as an auxiliary loss consistently beats all four feature-domain losses (LPIPS, CTX, PDL, FDL) on both NAFNet and Restormer across GoPro and RealBlur; for example, NAFNet+DDR achieves 33.34 dB PSNR on GoPro vs. 33.17 (PSNR-only), 33.17 (PSNR+LPIPS), and 32.79 (PSNR+CTX). The same pattern holds for SISR, where DDR yields 27.31 dB (NAFNet) and 28.17 dB (Restormer), exceeding the best feature-domain loss (LPIPS) by 0.03 and 0.04 dB respectively. Notably, DDR is the only method that improves over PSNR-only training on every single model–dataset combination tested, whereas several feature-domain losses (e.g., CTX, FDL) actually degrade performance in some settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two distinct lines of work: (1) text-driven feature augmentation from TextMania (Moon et al., CVPR 2023), which fuses language-guided attributes into visual features, and (2) AdaIN (Huang & Belongie, ICCV 2017), whose mean-variance alignment is repurposed here to 'stylize' text-driven degradation representations to match image feature statistics. Conceptually, DDR departs from prior deep-feature-based image descriptors (which either regress to human-labeled scores or measure fixed characteristics) by instead measuring the *change* in features under controlled degradation, and from prior degradation-representation methods for restoration (which require task-specific training) by using CLIP text prompts to flexibly specify degradation type without any learned parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DDR offers a training-free, zero-shot image quality metric that outperforms all existing OU-BIQA methods across eight datasets, and simultaneously serves as a plug-and-play unsupervised loss for restoration tasks — a dual utility that is rare for a single descriptor. Key limitations include the reliance on CLIP's visual encoder, which may underrepresent low-level degradation cues (the authors note that larger CLIP variants actually perform worse), and the manual selection of degradation prompts per task. Open questions include whether fine-tuning the feature extractor on degradation-specific tasks would close the gap, and whether learnable degradation tokens could automate prompt selection for arbitrary downstream tasks.
