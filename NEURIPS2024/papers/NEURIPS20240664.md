## Problem

When a language model is fine-tuned on outputs from another LLM, it is currently impossible to reliably detect this contamination without knowing the exact training text, and existing methods (membership inference, active IP protection) either require supervised access or lack valid statistical guarantees. This paper asks whether watermarked text—originally designed for AI-generated text detection—leaves a detectable 'radioactive' trace in any model fine-tuned on it, enabling the original model owner to prove contamination with provable confidence.

## Input / Output

The detector consumes a suspect LLM (either open-weight for logit access or closed via API), a corpus of watermarked text generated by the original model A, and the watermark secret key s. In open-model 'reading mode,' watermarked text is forwarded through B and next-token predictions are scored; in closed-model mode, B is prompted with watermarked instructions and generated completions are scored. The output is a p-value (or log₁₀(p)) computed from a binomial (KGW) or gamma (AK) distribution test over millions of de-duplicated (k+1)-tuples, where a value below 10⁻⁵ indicates statistically significant radioactivity.

## Method

The core protocol scores tokens from the suspect model B using the original watermark's scoring function W_score, but amplifies the weak radioactivity signal through three mechanisms: (1) a 'reading mode' for open models that forwards Alice's watermarked text through B and scores next-token predictions using input tokens as watermark windows, focusing analysis on contexts that match fine-tuning conditions; (2) a filter φ that restricts scoring to k-grams likely present in the contaminated training data; and (3) scoring orders of magnitude more tokens (100k–1.5M) than standard watermark detection. A key theoretical contribution is Proposition 1, which proves that 'B was not trained on Alice's watermarked data' is a subset of the null hypothesis H₀ (binomial score distribution) provided scored (k+1)-tuples are pairwise distinct and no watermark window repeats within the attention span. This de-duplication is empirically critical: without it, p-values collapse to <10⁻³⁰ under H₀ (false alarms), while with it, empirical p-values match the theoretical 0.5 mean under the null.

## Results

In the open-model/unsupervised setting, the proposed radioactivity detection achieves a p-value below 10⁻⁵ when only 5% of Bob's fine-tuning data is watermarked, and below 10⁻¹⁰ at 10% watermarked data. In the supervised setting, detection reaches p < 10⁻³⁰ (open-model) and p < 10⁻¹⁰ (closed-model) even with just 1% watermarked data. Watermarking does not degrade fine-tuned model quality: benchmark scores (NQ, TriviaQA, GSM8k, HumanEval, MMLU) remain within ~1 point of the non-watermarked baseline (Table 2). Detection confidence scales with supervision degree d, going from log₁₀(p) = −5.8 at d = 0.1% to < −30 at d = 10% (Table 3).

|---|---|

| 5% | −16.0 ± 2.6 |
| 10% | < −30 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against Membership Inference Attacks (MIA), which can only detect radioactivity in the open-model/supervised setting and fail entirely when the supervision degree d drops below ~2% (Figure 10b). In contrast, the proposed watermark-based radioactivity detection works in all four settings (open/closed × supervised/unsupervised), maintaining p < 10⁻⁵ even at d = 0.1% in the open-model case. Against the IP protection method of He et al. [2022a,b] (synonym-based watermarking), the paper demonstrates that their statistical test is fundamentally broken at scale: on non-watermarked instruction-answers, He et al.'s method yields p-values as low as 8.8 × 10⁻¹³ (Table 9), indicating severe false positives that make the test unusable. The Zhao et al. [2023] distillation-detection watermark also lacks grounded statistical guarantees and requires an empirically set threshold, whereas the proposed method provides provably valid p-values through careful de-duplication (Table 4 confirms p-values are ~0.5 under H₀ with de-duplication, versus < 10⁻³⁰ without it in the closed-model setting).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the image-domain concept of 'radioactivity' (Sablayrolles et al., 2020) to LLM watermarks, showing that decoding-based watermarks (KGW, Aaronson-Kirchner) leave detectable residual signals in models fine-tuned on watermarked text. The detection protocol is built directly on the scoring functions of Kirchenbauer et al. [2023a] and Aaronson and Kirchner [2023], but repurposes them from output-text detection to model-contamination detection with rigorous de-duplication rules for valid p-values. It departs from membership inference and IP-protection literature by working in the unsupervised setting (where the adversary doesn't know which specific outputs were used) and by providing provable statistical guarantees that prior active-protection methods lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLM fine-tuning increasingly relies on synthetic data from other models (Self-Instruct, Alpaca-style pipelines), the ability to detect such contamination with p < 10⁻⁵ even at 5% watermarked proportion has direct implications for IP protection and regulatory transparency. Key limitations include: the method only applies to hash-based watermarks (not semantic or multi-bit schemes), closed-model detection requires ~10k API queries, and a second fine-tuning on clean data only halves (not eliminates) the signal. Open questions include whether defenses like differential privacy or rephrasing can fully remove radioactivity, and how the approach generalizes to non-hashing watermark families.
