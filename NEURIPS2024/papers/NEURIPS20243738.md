## Problem

Existing evaluations of LLM reasoning measure answer accuracy but cannot distinguish genuine causal reasoning from mere replication of statistical patterns in training data. This paper asks whether LLMs can perform counterfactual reasoning consistent with a known causal structure, and introduces the probability of necessity (PN) and probability of sufficiency (PS) as formal, quantitative measures to answer that question.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243738/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires a pre-specified reasoning graph (a structural causal model over boolean variables), factual and counterfactual natural language prompts, and multiple LLM responses per prompt (typically 10 replicates). It produces PN and PS estimates with full bootstrap distributions (500 resamples), factual/counterfactual inconsistency rates (FIR/CIR), and γ-overlap metrics that quantify how tightly the LLM's estimated PN/PS concentrate around the true values. A key constraint is that the reasoning graph must be known a priori and the framework is restricted to boolean-valued variables.

## Method

The paper formalizes LLMs as abstract machines using the HEX framework, where solving a problem is a composition of an abstraction mapping (prompt), an internal query, and an output mapping. It then defines counterfactual queries that set a variable X to an alternative value x′ and ask the LLM to predict the resulting Y_{X=x′}, operationalized through specially structured counterfactual prompts. True PN and PS are computed by sampling from the original and intervened causal models, while LLM-estimated PN and PS are computed from the LLM's factual and counterfactual responses using the monotonicity identification formula. The core test is whether the LLM's estimated PN/PS distributions (obtained via 500 bootstrap resamples) overlap with the true values, with a theoretical criterion (Lemma 1) establishing that an LLM is β-counterfactual consistent with the causal model if and only if its HEX diagram commutes for all counterfactual queries. Empirically, GPT-4 achieves near-perfect PN/PS alignment on the Div6 problem, while GPT-2 and GPT-3.5-turbo show substantial counterfactual inconsistency, suggesting reasoning emerges only partially with model scale.

## Results

For the Div6 problem (true PN=1, PS=0.50), GPT-4 achieves the closest estimates with PN=0.984 and PS=0.505, and is the only model whose bootstrap density estimates overlap with the true PN/PS values. GPT-3.5-turbo performs nearly flawlessly on direct (factual) questions but exhibits a counterfactual error rate exceeding 25%, leading to substantially degraded PN/PS estimates. GPT-2 shows the weakest reasoning overall, though it achieves an accurate PN for the EvenSum problem while its PS estimates remain notably off. Across all three problems (Div6, EvenSum, CandyParty), negative PN/PS values appear for GPT-2 and GPT-3.5-turbo in several instances, indicating fundamental inconsistencies between their factual and counterfactual outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between three GPT-family models (GPT-2, GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4) against the ground-truth PN and PS computed analytically from the structural causal model. GPT-4 is the only model that achieves overlap between its estimated PN/PS densities and the true values, and specifically for the Div6 problem (PN=0.984 vs. true 1.0; PS=0.505 vs. true 0.50). GPT-3.5-turbo performs well on factual queries but degrades sharply on counterfactual queries (>25% error rate), causing its PN/PS estimates to diverge substantially from the true values. GPT-2 is the weakest performer, producing negative PN/PS estimates in several bootstrap samples for multiple problems, though it does achieve an accurate PN for the EvenSum problem. The paper also briefly re-runs the Div6 problem with Llama (7B, 13B) and Phi (1.5) in Appendix J, finding results consistent with the GPT-family trend: smaller/older models show weaker counterfactual reasoning. Overall, the results indicate that reasoning ability (as measured by PN/PS fidelity) improves with model scale within the GPT family but remains far from perfect even for GPT-4 on the harder problems (EvenSum, CandyParty).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two prior threads: the HEX framework [9], which formalizes LLMs as abstract machines processing queries over states via natural language interfaces, and Pearl's causal hierarchy [24, 25, 31], which defines probabilities of necessity (PN) and sufficiency (PS) as counterfactual quantities requiring both observational and interventional data. The authors explicitly position their work as departing from prior LLM reasoning evaluations [15, 13, 28, 4] that rely on accuracy or chain-of-thought metrics, arguing that none have used PN and PS as the primary objects of computation. The key conceptual move is treating an LLM as a 'counterfactual data simulator' whose outputs under hypothetical prompts can be compared against ground-truth counterfactuals generated from a known causal model, thereby operationalizing Pearl's assertion that only machines handling correlations, interventions, and counterfactuals possess human-comparable reasoning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, quantitative way to distinguish genuine generalization-by-reasoning from mere pattern replication, which is critical as LLMs are increasingly deployed for high-stakes decision-making. The empirical finding that even GPT-4 achieves ideal PN/PS overlap only on the simplest problem (Div6) while failing on harder ones (EvenSum, CandyParty) underscores that reasoning does not yet robustly emerge with scale. Key limitations—requiring access to a known reasoning graph, restricting to boolean variables, and depending on specific prompt formulations—constrain immediate applicability, and the open question of whether future models will close the counterfactual gap remains unresolved. The authors note the framework could extend to vision, robotics, and social sciences, but this remains speculative.
