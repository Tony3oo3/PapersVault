## Problem

The reversal curse was previously characterized as a generalization failure based solely on completion tasks, suggesting LLMs cannot understand the equivalence between A and B. This paper asks whether the failure is truly a lack of understanding or an artifact of the evaluation format and document structure. No prior work examined how the structure of training facts interacts with LLMs' internal problem-solving mechanisms to produce the observed asymmetries across task types.

## Input / Output

Training uses synthetic biographical facts (30 name-description pairs per subset, each augmented with 30 paraphrases) injected via standard knowledge injection with loss computed over the entire input document. Evaluation employs two formats: open-ended questions scored by ROUGE-1 recall against ground-truth descriptions/names, and 4-option MCQs where correctness is determined by regex matching of the selected option letter. For mechanistic analysis, the paper additionally extracts CoT reasoning traces and computes per-layer saliency scores quantifying information flow from name and description token spans to the answer position.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a diagnostic framework revealing an inherent 'thinking bias' in decoder-only LLMs: a consistent tendency to initiate fact recall using names rather than descriptions when processing biographical queries. This bias is validated through two complementary approaches: (1) CoT prompting that elicits explicit fact-recalling steps, where the frequency of names as subjects of recalled facts is quantified across models and datasets; and (2) saliency analysis of attention matrices (I_l = |Σ_h A_{h,l} ⊙ ∂L/∂A_{h,l}|) measuring relative information flow from name spans versus description spans to the answer token across all layers, showing S_nt consistently surpasses S_dt in mid-to-late layers. The bias explains the puzzling MCQ results: models trained on '[Name] is [Description]' generalize to D2N MCQs because their name-first recall aligns with the document structure, while '[Description] is [Name]' fails because the model cannot recall descriptions from names alone. Multiple mitigation strategies—extended training up to 20 epochs, mix training with QA examples, QA finetuning with CoT, and autoregressive blank-infilling—fail to overcome this bias, establishing it as an intractable property of next-token-prediction-pretrained architectures.

## Results

On the NameIsDescription subset, finetuned models achieve 91.5–97.4% ROUGE-1 recall on N2D open-QA and 65.3–78.3% accuracy on MCQs, demonstrating strong knowledge application. On the DescriptionIsName subset, open-QA D2N reaches 84.6–94.2% (near-perfect recall), yet MCQ accuracy collapses to 22.3–31.4%—essentially random guessing for 4-option questions. This stark contrast between open-QA and MCQ performance on the same subset is the paper's central empirical finding, revealing that LLMs can recall facts in free-form generation but fail to apply them when forced to select from options, unless the training document structure aligns with the model's inherent name-first thinking bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method paper, so it does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares (1) task formats: open-QA vs. MCQ on the same finetuned models, revealing that the reversal curse visible in open-QA (D2N NID ≈ 0–4%) largely disappears in MCQ format for NameIsDescription facts (D2N MCQ ≈ 65–78%) but persists for DescriptionIsName facts (MCQ ≈ 22–31%, near the 25% random baseline). (2) Document structures: NameIsDescription vs. DescriptionIsName, showing that all six models generalize effectively only when names precede descriptions in training documents. (3) Model scale: extending to LLaMA2-70B-chat and LLaMA3-70B-Instruct (Table A7) confirms the same pattern—MCQ on DescriptionIsName remains at ~24–27%—ruling out capacity as the explanation. (4) Mitigation strategies: longer training (up to 20 epochs), mix training with QA examples, and autoregressive blank-infilling all fail to lift DescriptionIsName MCQ above random, underscoring the intractability of the thinking bias through training alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reversal curse investigation initiated by Berglund et al. [2] beyond the original completion-task setting to open-ended QA and multiple-choice evaluation, revealing a fundamentally different picture of LLM generalization. It draws on the saliency interpretation framework of Simonyan et al. [40] to probe internal attention patterns, and tests the autoregressive blank-infilling mitigation strategy from Lv et al. [29] as one of several attempts to overcome the observed limitations. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a 'thinking bias' hypothesis—that LLMs have an inherent tendency to initiate fact recall from names rather than descriptions—which reframes the reversal curse as a backward-recall deficiency tied to document structure rather than a pure generalization failure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that models can answer open-QA D2N questions near-perfectly yet perform at random on the corresponding MCQs (when trained on 'Description is Name' facts) challenges the straightforward assumption that knowledge acquisition implies knowledge application, and points to a structural bottleneck in how LLMs retrieve and apply facts. The demonstrated intractability of this bias across multiple mitigation strategies (longer training, mix training, QA finetuning, ABI) suggests that data structure during knowledge injection is a first-order design consideration, with practical implications for RAG pipelines and continual learning. Open questions include whether the bias generalizes beyond biographical facts to other entity-relation types, whether it persists in closed-source frontier models, and whether a systematic data restructuring methodology can be developed to align training documents with the models' preferred recall direction.
