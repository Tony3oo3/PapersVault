## Problem

State-of-the-art toxicity detectors (e.g., LlamaGuard, OpenAI Moderation API) achieve low TPR at the very low FPR thresholds required in production, where toxic prompts are rare and even a 1% false-positive rate causes unacceptable disruption. These detectors also impose extra inference cost and latency by requiring a separate model to be queried for every prompt. The paper asks whether toxicity can be detected at negligible cost by exploiting information already present in the LLM's own output.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a text prompt and the raw first-response-token logits (a vector of ~36,000 values for Llama2) produced by the LLM being served, rather than the prompt text itself or a generated response. It outputs a single scalar toxicity score from the sparse logistic regression, which is thresholded to yield a toxic/benign decision. Because only the first-token logits are needed, detection can occur before any response tokens are generated, making it compatible with streaming APIs.

## Method

MULI's key insight is that well-aligned LLMs exhibit elevated logits for refusal-associated tokens (e.g., 'Sorry', 'Cannot', 'I') at the first response position for toxic prompts, even when the model ultimately responds substantively rather than refusing. The detector applies a sparse logistic regression with lasso regularization on a transformed logit vector: the transformation f* computes per-token log-odds (ln(p) − ln(1−p)) from the softmax of the first-token logits, then normalizes to a fixed mean and standard deviation estimated on training data. Training is supervised on labeled toxic/benign prompts, optimizing binary cross-entropy plus an ℓ₁ penalty, which yields a highly sparse weight vector concentrated on a small set of informative tokens. Because the logits are already computed as part of normal LLM inference, MULI adds essentially zero overhead compared to running a separate detection model.

## Results

On ToxicChat, MULI achieves 97.72% balanced accuracy, 91.29 AUPRC, and 42.54% TPR at 0.1% FPR, substantially outperforming LlamaGuard (5.25% TPR@0.1% FPR) and the OpenAI Moderation API (6.08% TPR@0.1% FPR). On LMSYS-Chat-1M, MULI reaches 98.23 AUPRC and 66.85% TPR at 0.1% FPR, compared to 7.29% for LlamaGuard and 63.74% for the OpenAI Moderation API. The largest gap appears at very low FPR thresholds: at 0.01% FPR, MULI detects 24.03% of toxic prompts on ToxicChat versus only 1.38% (LlamaGuard) and 2.76% (OMod). MULI also outperforms GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini used as zero-shot classifiers under the same FPR constraints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MULI outperforms both SOTA baselines—LlamaGuard and the OpenAI Moderation API—on every metric across both primary datasets. The most striking gap is at TPR@FPR 0.1%: MULI achieves 42.54% on ToxicChat versus 5.25% for LlamaGuard and 6.08% for OMod, an 8× improvement over LlamaGuard. On LMSYS-Chat-1M, the OpenAI Moderation API narrows the gap (63.74% vs. 66.85% TPR@0.1% FPR), but MULI still leads on AUPRC (98.23 vs. 97.62) and TPR@0.01% FPR (53.62% vs. 56.95%—actually OMod slightly edges MULI here). LlamaGuard is consistently the weakest baseline, falling 20+ points behind MULI on AUPRC and 35+ points behind on TPR@0.1% FPR on ToxicChat. The paper also reports that MULI beats GPT-4o (71.8% TPR at 1.4% FPR) and GPT-4o-mini (51.7% TPR at 1.0% FPR) used as zero-shot toxicity classifiers on ToxicChat.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of toxicity detection that relies on a separate, fine-tuned LLM (e.g., LlamaGuard) or a dedicated API (OpenAI Moderation) to classify prompts. Instead, it repurposes the queried LLM's own first-response-token logits as the detection signal, applying a sparse logistic regression on top — a classical linear classifier operating on information already produced during normal inference. The conceptual lineage traces to the safety-alignment literature (RLHF, refusal behavior), but the key move is treating the LLM's internal 'hesitation' (elevated probability of refusal tokens) as an extractable, free feature rather than discarding it after sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MULI offers a near-zero-overhead alternative to dedicated toxicity detectors that achieves dramatically better TPR at the low-FPR operating points that matter in production (42.5% vs. 5.25% at 0.1% FPR on ToxicChat), and it can block toxic prompts before any response is generated, making it compatible with streaming APIs. Key open questions include robustness to adversarial jailbreaks and to models whose alignment has been fine-tuned away, as well as whether performance holds across demographic subgroups — none of which are evaluated here. The finding that MULI's effectiveness correlates with the base model's security score suggests a useful diagnostic: the LLM's own safety alignment doubles as a free detection resource, but this coupling also means MULI inherits the fragility of alignment.
