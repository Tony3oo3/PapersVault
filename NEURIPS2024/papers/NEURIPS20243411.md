## Problem

Despite the success of foundation models in NLP and vision, no Graph Foundation Model (GFM) has achieved strong cross-task and cross-domain performance because a transferable vocabulary for graphs remains undefined. Existing subgraph-based approaches are computationally expensive to extract and suffer from GNNs' inability to detect certain motifs within subgraphs, while graphon-theoretic approaches rely on assumptions that are hard to satisfy in real-world cross-domain settings.

## Input / Output

The method takes graphs with node feature vectors as input and implicitly encodes L-layer computation trees via a single message-passing GNN (no explicit subgraph extraction). Pre-training produces a discrete tree vocabulary of K tokens (default 128) learned via vector quantization; fine-tuning then uses this frozen vocabulary to output node class labels, link prediction scores, or graph class labels by unifying all tasks as computation tree classification. The tree vocabulary remains fixed during fine-tuning to preserve encoded general knowledge, and both a prototype classifier and a linear classifier are combined at inference.

## Method

GFT rethinks transferable graph patterns as computation trees—subtree structures that emerge naturally from unfolding the message-passing process—and proves a recursive stability bound showing that similar computation trees yield similar GNN embeddings. During pre-training, a computation tree reconstruction objective (combining feature, topology, and semantic reconstruction losses) is jointly optimized with vector quantization to learn a discrete tree vocabulary, augmented by an orthogonal regularizer to prevent token collapse and improve expressiveness. During fine-tuning, all graph tasks (node, link, graph-level) are reformulated as computation tree classification: task-specific trees are constructed (e.g., merging source/target node trees for link prediction), their embeddings query the frozen tree vocabulary, and predictions are made via a prototype classifier (class prototypes built from same-class tree tokens) combined with a linear classifier. This design eliminates explicit subgraph extraction (reducing complexity from O(|V|³) to O(L·(|E|·d+|V|·d²))), provides theoretical transferability guarantees, and mitigates negative transfer by aligning pre-training and fine-tuning through the shared tree vocabulary.

## Results

In the pre-training and fine-tuning setting, GFT achieves an average accuracy of 79.92% across eight datasets spanning node, link, and graph-level tasks, exceeding the best baseline (GAT at 75.44%) by over 4.5% on average. The largest gains appear on link prediction (WN18RR: 91.91% vs. 80.16% for GAT, +11.75%) and graph classification (HIV: 72.67% vs. 65.54%, +7.13%). In few-shot settings (1–5 shots), GFT outperforms the best GFM baseline (OFA) by 5–10% across all evaluated datasets, e.g., 89.13% vs. 83.64% on FB15K237-10-way 5-shot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GFT outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset and task type. Against the strongest supervised baseline GAT (75.44% avg), GFT gains +2.4% on node classification (e.g., 78.62 vs. 76.24 on Cora), +11.8% on WN18RR link prediction (91.91 vs. 80.16), and +7.1% on HIV graph classification (72.67 vs. 65.54). Against the best self-supervised baseline GIANT (74.11% avg), the gap widens on graph-level tasks (HIV: 72.67 vs. 65.44; PCBA: 77.90 vs. 61.49). Among existing GFMs, GFT surpasses Prodigy (in-context learning) and OFA (supervised pre-training) by 5–10% in few-shot settings, e.g., 36.29% vs. 25.51% on Arxiv-40-way 5-shot and 89.13% vs. 83.64% on FB15K237-10-way 5-shot. Notably, GFT's advantage is most pronounced on link and graph-level tasks, where subgraph-based GFMs struggle due to GNN expressiveness limitations in detecting critical substructures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from two dominant lines of work on graph transferability: graphon-theory approaches (Ruiz et al., Cao et al.) that rely on stringent distributional assumptions, and subgraph-based GFMs (Prodigy, OFA, UniGraph) that require explicit ego-graph extraction and suffer from 1-WL expressiveness limitations. It repurposes the computation tree — a structure already implicit in every message-passing GNN layer — as the fundamental transferable unit, combining it with vector quantization (Van Den Oord et al., 2017) to form a discrete tree vocabulary. The key conceptual departure from prior GFMs is that computation trees need no explicit extraction (they emerge naturally from the GNN forward pass) and are fully learnable by the same encoder that produces them, sidestepping both the efficiency and expressiveness problems of subgraph-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GFT provides a theoretically grounded, computationally efficient alternative to subgraph-based GFMs, with a clean unification of node-, link-, and graph-level tasks under a single tree-classification formulation. The demonstrated mitigation of negative transfer through vocabulary alignment addresses a practical pain point in cross-domain pre-training. However, the analysis is restricted to message-passing GNNs (excluding graph transformers and higher-order expressive GNNs), experiments cover only homophily graphs, and the model has not been tested at the scale of truly large graphs (OGB-LSC). Open questions include whether the computation tree vocabulary generalizes to in-context or zero-shot settings, and whether the approach extends to architectures where the message-passing unrolling is not naturally tree-structured.
