## Problem

Neural VRP methods suffer severe performance degradation on clean instances with crafted perturbations, yet existing work focuses almost exclusively on the attack side while defense remains underexplored. Vanilla adversarial training for VRPs introduces an undesirable trade-off between standard generalization and adversarial robustness, primarily due to insufficient model capacity. This paper tackles the defense side by asking how to effectively synergize multiple models to achieve favorable performance on both clean and adversarial instances within a reasonable computational budget.

## Input / Output

The framework takes VRP instances defined over a graph with node coordinates (continuous, in unit square) and, for CVRP, integer node demands (1–9) as input. During training, each clean instance is expanded into a set of local adversarial variants (one per model) and a single global adversarial variant, all of which are valid problem instances. At inference, the neural router is discarded; the M trained models each produce a tour, and the best-cost tour among them is returned as the final solution.

## Method

CNF adversarially trains M copies of a pretrained autoregressive construction model (e.g., POMO) in a collaborative min-max loop. In the inner maximization, it generates both local adversarial instances (each model attacks itself) and global adversarial instances (at each gradient step, the best-performing model w.r.t. the current perturbation is targeted), yielding more diverse and stronger attacks that exploit the ensemble effect. In the outer minimization, an attention-based neural router takes the instance set and a cost matrix as input, outputs a logit matrix, and assigns TopK instances to each model to guarantee load balancing. The router is optimized via REINFORCE with a reward equal to the improvement in collaborative (best-of-M) performance after each training step, learning to route instances to the models that benefit most. At inference, the router is discarded and the best solution across all M models is returned.

## Results

On TSP100, CNF(3) achieves 0.118% optimality gap on clean instances (Uniform) and 0.236% on adversarial instances (Fixed Adv.), outperforming all baselines on both metrics simultaneously. On CVRP100, CNF(3) reaches 1.073% (Uniform) and 0.730% (Fixed Adv.), again the best among all methods. The untrained POMO(1) baseline suffers catastrophic degradation on adversarial instances (35.8% gap on TSP100, 3.98% on CVRP100), while vanilla AT (POMO_AT) improves adversarial robustness but sacrifices clean performance (e.g., 0.365% vs. 0.144% on TSP100 Uniform). CNF(3) also achieves the best OOD generalization, e.g., 0.193% on Rotation(100) and 1.383% on Uniform(200) for TSP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CNF(3) dominates all baselines on both clean and adversarial metrics across TSP and CVRP. Against vanilla AT (POMO_AT(3)), CNF improves TSP100 adversarial gap from 0.295% to 0.236% while also improving clean gap from 0.255% to 0.118%, eliminating the accuracy-robustness trade-off. Against the hardness-aware curriculum defense (POMO_HAC(3)), CNF achieves a lower adversarial gap (0.236% vs. 0.344% on TSP100) and a lower clean gap (0.118% vs. 0.135%). Against diversity training (POMO_DivTrain(3)), CNF wins on both metrics (0.118%/0.236% vs. 0.255%/0.297% on TSP100). The greedy routing variant (CNF_Greedy(3)) is consistently worse than the full CNF with the neural router, confirming the router's contribution. On CVRP100, CNF(3) achieves the best adversarial gap (0.730%) and clean gap (1.073%), outperforming POMO_DivTrain(3) (0.754%/1.254%) and POMO_HAC(3) (0.829%/1.085%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the adversarial training (AT) min-max formulation of Madry et al. (ICLR 2018) and applies it to neural VRP solvers, primarily POMO (Kwon et al., NeurIPS 2020). It departs from prior VRP robustness work (Zhang et al. 2022, Geisler et al. 2022, Lu et al. 2023), which focused on designing perturbation models (the attack side) and relied on vanilla AT for defense, by introducing a collaborative ensemble-based AT framework with a learned neural router for instance distribution. The paper also explicitly distinguishes its setting from image-domain adversarial robustness methods (e.g., TRADES), which depend on imperceptible perturbations and ground-truth labels that are unavailable or inapplicable in discrete combinatorial optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable gap by showing that vanilla AT for neural VRP solvers induces an undesirable accuracy-robustness trade-off, and that a collaborative multi-model framework with a learned router can mitigate this while also improving out-of-distribution generalization on both synthetic and benchmark instances. The OOD generalization improvement as a byproduct of adversarial defense is a useful empirical signal for the NCO community. Key limitations include the increased training cost (85 hrs vs. 32 hrs for vanilla AT with 3 models) and the reliance on a pretrained model as warm-start; open questions include certified robustness guarantees, more efficient collaborative training (e.g., parameter sharing), and extending the framework to other COPs and improvement-based heuristics.
