## Problem

Large multimodal models still struggle to understand humor derived from juxtaposition in comics, where two panels create a nonlinear narrative with inherent contradictions that demand social reasoning and deep semantic interpretation. Prior computational humor research has focused on single-panel cartoons, leaving the more complex case of multi-panel contradictory narratives largely unexplored. This paper asks whether current state-of-the-art VLMs and LLMs can accurately comprehend the interplay between juxtaposed panels and the underlying philosophy they convey.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241493/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample is a captionless two-panel comic image (from Anton Gudim's 'YES, BUT' series) paired with four annotated components of varying length: a literal description (~80 words), a contradiction explanation (~31 words), an underlying philosophy (~24 words), and a title (~6 words). For VLMs the raw comic image is the sole input, while LLMs receive a generated literal description in place of the image. The two generation tasks (literal description, contradiction) produce free-form text, and the two reasoning tasks (philosophy selection, title matching) are 4-way multiple-choice questions whose negative options are hard distractors crafted by annotators to be contextually plausible.

## Method

The paper introduces YESBUT, a 348-comic benchmark constructed through a progressive human-AI collaborative annotation pipeline in which GPT-4 generates initial drafts of each component (from easy to hard: description → contradiction → philosophy → title) that eight human annotators then refine, followed by a cross-verification stage to filter ambiguous or biased samples. Four tasks are designed at increasing difficulty—literal description writing, contradiction generation, underlying philosophy selection, and title matching—progressing from surface narrative comprehension to deep nonlinear reasoning about the juxtaposition. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot across 11 VLMs and 3 LLMs using three distinct prompts per task to reduce prompt bias, with automatic metrics (BERTScore, ROUGE-2, GPT-based scoring, accuracy) supplemented by human evaluation on 30 samples. Ablation studies probe whether providing oracle human-written descriptions or decomposing the task into a description-then-reasoning pipeline improves deep reasoning, revealing that accurate surface-level narrative understanding is a prerequisite for grasping the underlying contradiction, yet a significant gap remains even with oracle inputs.

## Results

On the YESBUT benchmark, GPT-4 achieves the highest scores on literal description (BERT 88.32, R-2 87.46, GPT-eval 3.76) and contradiction generation (BERT 87.64, R-2 83.21, GPT-eval 4.03). For the deep reasoning tasks, Claude-3 leads on underlying philosophy selection with 84.10% accuracy, while LLaVA-1.6-34B achieves the best title matching score of 63.31%. Even the best-performing models remain far from human-level performance, with title matching accuracy below 65% across all evaluated models, underscoring the difficulty of nonlinear narrative reasoning in juxtaposed comics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Commercial VLMs (GPT-4, Claude-3) consistently outperform open-sourced VLMs across all four tasks. GPT-4 leads on both generation tasks (literal description and contradiction), while Claude-3 achieves the highest philosophy selection accuracy (84.10%), narrowly beating GPT-4 (82.76%). Among open-sourced VLMs, LLaVA-1.6-34B is the strongest, achieving the best title matching score (63.31%) and competitive philosophy accuracy (78.83%), while InstructBLIP variants (13B and 7B) perform the worst on deep reasoning tasks (30.75% and 25.86% philosophy accuracy, respectively). Notably, LLMs (Llama-3-8B, Mistral-7B) fed with LLaVA-generated descriptions achieve philosophy and title scores comparable to mid-range open-sourced VLMs, suggesting that narrative text alone can partially substitute for direct image understanding. However, even the best model (Claude-3 at 84.10% philosophy, LLaVA-1.6-34B at 63.31% title) falls well short of human performance, and title matching remains the hardest task for all models, with a 15–20 point gap versus philosophy selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of computational humor and comic understanding research (Hessel et al. 2023, Yang et al. 2024), which has focused on single-panel cartoons and deep semantics, to the specific and more demanding case of two-panel juxtaposition with contradictory narratives. It draws on comics theory (juxtaposition as a narrative device) and repurposes it as a formal evaluation axis for VLMs, introducing a multi-level task hierarchy (literal description → contradiction → philosophy → title) that no prior benchmark has structured around the nonlinear reasoning between panels. The empirical evaluation builds on standard zero-shot VLM/LLM evaluation protocols but applies them to a domain where the required reasoning is explicitly non-sequential and culturally grounded.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a concrete and measurable gap: even GPT-4 and Claude-3 achieve only ~84% on philosophy selection and ~63% on title matching, and the finding that oracle descriptions substantially boost performance while model-generated descriptions can actually hurt (Table 3) localizes the bottleneck to visual-to-narrative translation rather than downstream reasoning. This suggests that improving VLMs' ability to faithfully extract narrative structure from images is a prerequisite for any progress on deep semantic understanding of visual media. Open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond Anton Gudim's stylistically consistent two-panel format, whether the 348-sample size is sufficient for robust conclusions, and how nonlinear reasoning architectures (multi-agent debate, test-time compute scaling) might close the gap.
