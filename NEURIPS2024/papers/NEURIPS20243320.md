## Problem

The effectiveness of general stochastic or conditional policies is often not identifiable from observational data due to unobserved confounding, yet existing partial-identification results are restricted to atomic interventions on small discrete systems and offer no practical estimation tools for finite samples with continuous or high-dimensional covariates. This paper bridges that gap by deriving tighter analytical bounds that exploit causal graph structure and by constructing scalable estimators with formal convergence guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243320/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a causal diagram G over observed variables, a policy specification π that maps covariates C ∈ R^d to a probability distribution over action variables X (encompassing deterministic, stochastic, and conditional interventions), and a finite observational sample D ~ P(V). It produces a pair of analytical bounds (ψ^ℓ, ψ^u) on E_{P_π}[Y] expressed as expectations of weighted functions of the observational distribution, which are then estimated from data via cross-fitted nuisance models. The outcome Y is assumed bounded in [0,1], and the bounds are guaranteed to contain the true policy effect for all SCMs compatible with G and P(V).

## Method

The paper introduces two graphical criteria—partial adjustment sets and partial conditional instrumental sets—that exploit d-separation in manipulated causal graphs to tighten the natural policy bounds (a generalization of Manski–Robins bounds to general policies). Algorithm 1 systematically combines these criteria in a recursive procedure (omitting irrelevant interventions, finding adjustment sets, then finding instrumental sets) with O(k(k²+m)) runtime. For estimation, the authors derive two equivalent target functionals: a probability-weighting form involving a ratio γ = π̄_R · 1_z(Z)/P(T,Z|W\Z,C) and a sequential-regression form involving nested conditional expectations μ₀, μ₁, μ̃₁, μ₂. The final DML estimator combines both via K-fold cross-fitting, achieving n^{-1/2} convergence whenever nuisances converge at n^{-1/4}, and is doubly robust (unbiased if either the probability ratio or the outcome regression is correctly specified).

## Results

The paper evaluates three estimators (T^PW, T^REG, T^DML) across four synthetic settings and a real-world obesity dataset. Under correct nuisance estimation (Setting 1), all three estimators converge to the true bounds as sample size increases. Under misspecification of either γ or μ (Settings 2–3), T^DML remains accurate while T^PW and T^REG degrade, confirming its doubly-robust property. When decaying noise at rate n^{-1/4} is added to all nuisances (Setting 4), T^DML achieves the fastest convergence rate, outperforming both T^PW (unstable at small n due to ratio denominators near zero) and T^REG. On the real obesity dataset, Alg. 1 produces tighter bounds than the Natural Policy Bounds (Prop. 2), and policies promoting healthier lifestyles (larger α) are expected to reduce mean BMI from 29.3, though substantial uncertainty remains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimation approaches: T^PW (probability-weighting), T^REG (sequential regression), and the proposed T^DML (double machine learning). T^DML dominates in robustness: when γ is misspecified (Setting 2) or μ is misspecified (Setting 3), T^DML maintains low error while T^PW and T^REG each fail in their respective misspecification scenario. In the noisy nuisance setting (Setting 4, noise ~ Normal(n^{-1/4}, n^{-1/4})), T^DML converges faster than both competitors across all four data-generating mechanisms, including a high-dimensional setting with W ∈ R^{100}. For bound tightness, the paper compares the Natural Policy Bounds (Prop. 2, most conservative), bounds using only partial adjustment sets (Prop. 3), bounds using only partial instrumental sets (Prop. 4), and the combined Alg. 1. Figure 5 shows that Alg. 1 consistently yields the narrowest bound width, with the gap over Prop. 2 widening as sample size increases. No external external baselines from the literature are directly compared numerically; the comparisons are internal to the proposed framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the partial identification literature from atomic interventions (Manski, Robins, Balke-Pearl) to general stochastic and conditional policies, introducing graphical criteria—partial adjustment sets and partial instrumental sets—that exploit d-separation in manipulated causal graphs to tighten bounds beyond the natural policy bounds. The estimation framework adapts the double machine learning toolkit (Chernozhukov et al.) to the bounding problem, combining probability-weighted and regression-based characterizations of the same bound via cross-fitting. The σ-calculus for stochastic interventions (Correa & Bareinboim) provides the formal machinery for manipulating policy-induced distributions in the proofs, while the ListSep algorithm (van der Zander) is adapted to enumerate all valid partial adjustment sets. The authors explicitly position their approach as complementary to sensitivity-analysis methods (Tan, Rosenbaum models), which quantify unobserved confounding through odds ratios rather than structural assumptions encoded in a causal graph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first estimation framework for bounding the effect of general policies (not just atomic interventions) given a causal diagram, making partial identification practical for high-dimensional, continuous-outcome settings relevant to precision medicine and public policy. Key limitations include the requirement of a correct causal graph, the conjectured undecidability of selecting the optimal bound expression from multiple valid ones, and the absence of finite-sample confidence intervals (only asymptotic rates are provided). The honest discussion of bound width—driven primarily by the data distribution rather than the graph—tempts expectations about practical informativeness. Open questions include extending the framework to sequential/multi-period policies and characterizing finite-sample uncertainty for the DML estimator.
