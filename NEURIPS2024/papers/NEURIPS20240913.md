## Problem

In temporal clinical settings, EHR data is continuously recorded while chest X-rays are captured infrequently due to radiation and cost constraints, creating an asynchronicity gap. Existing multimodal fusion methods rely on a 'carry-forward' strategy that uses the last available CXR, which may be hours or days outdated for rapidly deteriorating ICU patients. This paper asks whether generating an updated, patient-specific CXR at prediction time can close this temporal gap and improve downstream clinical predictions.

## Input / Output

The LDM stage takes a reference CXR image (grayscale, encoded to a 4×28×28 latent via a pre-trained VAE with 8× compression) and the irregular EHR time series (17 clinical variables + age/gender, hourly-sampled) spanning the interval between the reference CXR and the target time. The prediction stage additionally consumes the complete 48-hour EHR window and the last available CXR (encoded by ResNet-34). Outputs are a generated latent CXR (3136-dim vector) fused via self-attention to produce multi-label phenotype predictions (25 disease classes) and binary in-hospital mortality predictions.

## Method

DDL-CXR operates in two stages. In the LDM stage, a UNet-based latent diffusion model generates an up-to-date CXR representation in VAE latent space, conditioned on the reference CXR latent (concatenated channel-wise with the noisy target latent to preserve anatomy) and a Transformer-encoded EHR sequence (injected via cross-attention at spatial transformer layers). A key training innovation is a contrastive objective: a perturbed EHR representation (mixed with Gaussian noise) is fed to the UNet, and the loss penalizes the model if the perturbed-condition generation remains close to the target, forcing the network to genuinely exploit EHR information. An auxiliary task trains the EHR encoder's CLS token to predict CheXpert abnormality labels, further aligning EHR features with imaging semantics. In the prediction stage, the generated latent CXR, the last available CXR, and the full 48h EHR are each encoded (Transformer, ResNet-34, Transformer) and fused through a self-attention layer for final clinical prediction.

## Results

On the MIMIC-IV/MIMIC-CXR benchmark, DDL-CXR achieves the best performance across all four metrics: 0.470 AUPRC and 0.740 AUROC for phenotype classification, and 0.523 AUPRC and 0.822 AUROC for in-hospital mortality prediction. The AUPRC gains are particularly notable, with relative improvements of 2.4% over the best baseline (DrFuse, 0.459) for phenotyping and 3.56% over GAN-based (0.505) for mortality. When the time gap between the last CXR and prediction time exceeds 36 hours, DDL-CXR's mortality AUROC reaches 0.830 versus 0.806 for the best baseline (GAN-based), confirming that generating an updated CXR is most beneficial when the last image is most outdated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDL-CXR outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric. For phenotype classification, it leads DrFuse (0.459 AUPRC, 0.729 AUROC) and GAN-based (0.453, 0.728) by 1.1 and 1.7 AUPRC points respectively, while the single-modal Uni-EHR baseline trails by 3.6 AUPRC points. For mortality prediction, DDL-CXR surpasses the strongest competitor GAN-based (0.505 AUPRC, 0.816 AUROC) by 1.8 AUPRC and 0.6 AUROC points, and outperforms the EHR-only Uni-EHR (0.498, 0.815) by 2.5 AUPRC points. The carry-forward multimodal methods (MedFuse, DrFuse, DAFT, MMTM) all underperform the single-modal Uni-EHR on mortality AUPRC, suggesting that simply fusing an outdated CXR can be counterproductive. The advantage of DDL-CXR grows with the time gap δ: at δ≥36h, its mortality AUROC (0.830) exceeds the best baseline (GAN-based, 0.806) by 2.4 points, whereas at δ<12h the gap narrows to 2.1 points (0.867 vs 0.846). In the per-phenotype AUPRC breakdown (Table 3), DDL-CXR achieves the best average rank (2.28 vs 3.88 for DrFuse), winning on 19 of 25 disease labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the latent diffusion model (LDM) framework of Rombach et al. for image generation, adapting it to the clinical domain by conditioning on a patient's prior CXR (for anatomy) and EHR time series (for disease progression). It departs from the standard 'carry-forward' strategy used in clinical multimodal fusion (MedFuse, DrFuse, DAFT, MMTM), which simply reuses the last available CXR, by instead generating an updated latent CXR at prediction time. The contrastive learning objective for the LDM—penalizing the model when given perturbed EHR conditions—is a novel training signal not seen in prior conditional medical image generation work (Packhäuser, Weber, Gu), which relied on pathology labels or radiology reports rather than longitudinal EHR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a clinically significant and underexplored problem: the temporal misalignment between continuously recorded EHR and sparsely acquired CXR in ICU settings, where over 70% of patients show CXR changes within 24 hours. The 'generation-as-fusion' paradigm—where the diffusion process itself captures cross-modal interaction—offers a conceptually appealing alternative to post-hoc feature-level fusion. However, the approach is validated only on MIMIC-IV/CXR, lacks radiologist evaluation of generated images, and shows a performance gap at intermediate time intervals (24–36h), leaving open questions about generalizability to other imaging modalities, longer time horizons, and real-world deployment where generation fidelity must meet clinical standards.
