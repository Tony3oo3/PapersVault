## Problem

Current enzyme annotation systems (EC, GO, KO) classify enzymes by protein family or expert-derived reaction categories rather than by their specific catalyzed reactions, leading to ambiguities and inconsistencies. No large-scale benchmark exists for directly predicting the mapping between individual enzymes and their exact catalyzed reactions, and prior datasets (ESP: 18K pairs, EnzymeMap: 46K pairs) are an order of magnitude smaller than what is needed for robust evaluation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes protein sequences (for ESM/SaProt PLM initialization), AlphaFold-predicted 3D protein structures, and substrate/product molecular graphs augmented with force-field-optimized 3D conformations. The task is framed as bidirectional retrieval: given a reaction, rank all candidate enzymes by catalytic likelihood, or given an enzyme, rank all candidate reactions. Negative samples are constructed by selecting top-1000 sequence- or SMILES-similar but non-catalyzing partners, rather than using all non-annotated pairs, to keep the search space tractable.

## Method

Reactions are encoded via a multi-view pipeline: 2D/3D molecular graphs (with atom positions) are processed by MAT or UniMol GNNs, and substrate-to-product 'transitions' are captured through cross-attention between substrate and product node embeddings, yielding a single reaction vector. Enzymes are encoded by first initializing residue features with two protein language models (ESM for sequence, SaProt for structure-aware tokens via FoldSeek alignment), then applying a Frame Averaging Neural Network (FANN) to produce SE(3)-invariant residue representations from the 3D structure. A 4-layer MLP encoder processes each modality's embedding to a 256-d space, and a 4-layer MLP decoder scores the concatenated enzyme-reaction pair. The model is trained with supervised binary classification on positive and similarity-constructed negative pairs, and evaluated via retrieval metrics (Top-k accuracy, Mean Rank, MRR) across three splits that progressively increase difficulty by introducing unseen enzymes or unseen reactions.

## Results

On the enzyme-similarity split (enzyme→reaction retrieval), the best neural network (MAT-2D + ESM with Bi-RNN decoder) achieves 81.5% top-1 accuracy, 2.71 mean rank, and 0.886 MRR, substantially outperforming BLAST (33.3% top-1, 7.08 mean rank, 0.502 MRR). On the time-based split, the best model (UniMol-3D + ESM + GNN) reaches 35.9% top-1 accuracy and 0.495 MRR for enzyme→reaction retrieval. The reaction-similarity split is markedly harder: top-1 accuracy drops to ~11% for enzyme→reaction and ~13% for reaction→enzyme, while BLAST collapses to near 0% (0.20% and 0.00% top-1 respectively), confirming that sequence similarity alone cannot generalize to unseen reactions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is BLASTp (sequence alignment), a classical annotation method. On the enzyme-similarity split, neural networks decisively outperform BLAST: the best Bi-RNN model (MAT-2D + ESM) achieves 81.5% top-1 accuracy versus BLAST's 33.3%, and 0.886 MRR versus 0.502. On the reaction-similarity split, the gap is extreme — BLAST drops to 0.20% top-1 (enzyme→reaction) and 0.00% (reaction→enzyme), while neural networks still reach 11.0% and 13.5% respectively, indicating that sequence similarity alone cannot generalize to unseen reactions. On the time-based split, BLAST and neural networks are roughly comparable (BLAST: 35.8% top-1, 0.531 MRR; best NN: 39.1% top-1, 0.530 MRR), likely because some enzyme-reaction pairs reappear across the temporal boundary. Overall, the results demonstrate that learned representations capture enzyme-reaction compatibility far more effectively than sequence homology, particularly when generalizing to novel enzymes or reactions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the enzyme-reaction retrieval formulation introduced by CLIPZyme [51] and the enzyme-substrate prediction of ESP [37], but departs from both in key ways: it replaces CLIPZyme's pseudo-transition-state graph with cross-attention between substrate and product atom representations, and it incorporates both 2D graph and 3D conformational data alongside structure-aware protein language models (SaProt) and SE(3)-equivariant GNN encoding (FANN). Unlike prior work that predicts coarse EC/GO annotations or relies on sequence similarity (BLAST), ReactZyme directly models the mapping between a specific enzyme and its exact catalyzed reaction, positioning the task as a large-scale retrieval problem over 178K enzyme-reaction pairs drawn from SwissProt and Rhea.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ReactZyme benchmark is the largest enzyme-reaction dataset to date (178K pairs vs. 18K for ESP and 46K for EnzymeMap) and introduces three principled evaluation splits that isolate different generalization challenges. The reaction-similarity split, where test reactions are unseen, remains largely unsolved (best top-1 accuracy ~11%), highlighting a critical gap in current models' ability to generalize to novel chemistry. The paper also demonstrates that 3D structural information and equivariant encoding provide meaningful gains over 2D-only representations—a finding not observed in prior EC classification tasks—suggesting that geometric reasoning is important for capturing catalytic specificity. Open questions include how to construct better negative samples and how to improve generalization to truly unseen reactions, potentially through contrastive learning.
