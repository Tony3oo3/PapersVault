## Problem

Precision matrix estimation in small sample settings (n < p/10) is unreliable because sample sizes fall below the theoretical lower bound Ω(d² log p) for graph recovery. Existing meta-learning approaches for this task impose restrictive assumptions (new-task edges must be subsets of auxiliary-task edges) and rely on slow solvers like BigQuic that require O(p²)–O(p³) time per iteration, making them impractical when only a handful of samples are available.

## Input / Output

The method consumes K auxiliary datasets (each n_k × p, with n_k = p/2 in experiments) and a single new-task dataset (n × p, with n as low as p/20), all assumed to follow multivariate sub-Gaussian distributions. It produces a sparse p × p precision matrix Ω_new for the new task, the shared meta-knowledge matrix θ, and a sparsity pattern G constructed as the union of supp(θ) and the soft-thresholded support of the new-task sample covariance. The precision matrix is recovered by solving the dual of a maximum-determinant matrix completion problem constrained to the chordal embedding of G.

## Method

FasMe is a two-stage meta-learning framework. In the meta-training stage (meta-teacher), it decomposes each auxiliary precision matrix as Ω^(k) = θ + θ_r^(k) and jointly estimates the common substructure θ and task-specific residuals via a multi-task CLIME-style constrained ℓ₁ minimization (K‖θ‖₁ + ρΣ‖θ_r^(k)‖₁), solved column-by-column as independent linear programs in parallel. In the meta-testing stage (meta-student), it constructs the sparsity pattern G = supp(θ) ∪ supp(S_η(Σ_new)), computes its chordal embedding via symbolic Cholesky factorization, and solves the MDMC dual problem using a Newton Conjugate Gradient algorithm that converges in O(p log ε⁻¹) time and O(p) memory—dramatically faster than BigQuic's O(p²) per-iteration cost. The key theoretical contribution is proving that only O(log p / K) samples per auxiliary task and O(log |G|) samples for the new task suffice for consistent support recovery under sub-Gaussian and irrepresentable conditions.

## Results

On synthetic Random graph data (n=50, p=1000), FasMe achieves MCC=0.659 and F-norm error=22.63 in 0.061s, outperforming all four baselines on both accuracy and speed. At the larger setting (n=100, p=2000), FasMe reaches MCC=0.661 in 0.994s, while the next-best method Meta-IE (BigQuic) achieves only MCC=0.281 in 96.85s. On real-world data, FasMe attains the minimum negative log-determinant Bregman divergence (−56.31 on fMRI, −64.35 on ChIP-Seq), substantially outperforming the best baseline (Meta-IE at −291.66 and −200.45 respectively). FasMe is at least 10× faster than all baselines across all tested configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FasMe dominates all four baselines on both accuracy (MCC, F-norm) and runtime across every tested configuration. Against QUIC, FasMe improves MCC from 0.043 to 0.659 (n=50, p=1000) while being ~50× faster (0.061s vs 3.028s). Against Neighborhood Selection, FasMe nearly doubles MCC (0.659 vs 0.287) and is ~140× faster. Against Meta-IE (BigQuic), the closest meta-learning competitor, FasMe achieves 63% higher MCC (0.659 vs 0.403) and is ~450× faster (0.061s vs 27.370s); Meta-IE's performance degrades sharply as p increases because its support-union constraint becomes unreliable. gRankLasso performs worst on accuracy (MCC≈0.008) and exceeds the 30-minute time limit in all settings due to its automatic tuning mechanism failing in small-sample regimes. On real-world fMRI and ChIP-Seq data, FasMe achieves the lowest Bregman divergence (−56.31 and −64.35), roughly 5× better than the next-best baseline Meta-IE (−291.66 and −200.45).

## Relation to Prior Work

FasMe builds on three distinct lines of work: the CLIME constrained ℓ₁-minimization framework (Cai et al., 2011) for the meta-teacher's column-wise LP formulation, the Maximum Determinant Matrix Completion (MDMC) problem (Vandenberghe et al., 1998) for the meta-student's dual optimization, and chordal embedding (Dahl et al., 2008) to handle non-chordal sparsity patterns. It departs from the closest prior meta-learning method, Meta-IE (Zhang et al., 2021), in two key ways: replacing the support-union meta-knowledge (which restricts new-task edges to a subset of auxiliary edges) with a common-substructure decomposition, and replacing BigQuic's iterative Newton method with a recursive closed-form MDMC solver. The multi-task learning formulations of Honorio et al. and Ma & Michailidis inform the meta-teacher design, but FasMe's separation of meta-training from meta-testing avoids the joint retraining bottleneck those methods suffer from.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: precision matrix estimation for rare diseases or small-cohort neuroscience studies where n ≪ p, a regime where standard methods like GLasso provably fail. The theoretical sample complexity reduction to O(log p/K) per auxiliary task and O(log|G|) for the new task, combined with O(p log ε⁻¹) convergence time, makes the approach viable for high-dimensional genomic and neuroimaging data. Key limitations include the sub-Gaussian assumption, the requirement that auxiliary tasks share a common substructure with the target (supp(θ) ⊆ supp(Ω_new)), and the need for sufficient samples in auxiliary tasks. Open questions the authors identify include relaxing the sub-Gaussian assumption and further reducing meta-training sample requirements.
