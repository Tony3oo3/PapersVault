## Problem

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) improves generalization empirically, but its theoretical underpinnings remain incomplete: existing continuous-time approximations capture only second-order effects, and the critical alignment between SAM's perturbation direction and the top Hessian eigenvector is often poor in practice, limiting SAM's ability to actually reduce sharpness. The paper asks what drives SAM's implicit bias on the Hessian spectrum and how to make that regularization explicit.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes standard mini-batch gradients (as in SAM) plus intermittent Hessian-vector products computed via q=5 power-iteration steps every p=100 mini-batch steps to estimate the top eigenvector. The estimated eigenvector is decomposed into components parallel and perpendicular to the current gradient; only the perpendicular component is added to the perturbation direction. The output is the updated parameter vector, with the same interface as standard SAM (two forward/backward passes per step plus occasional HVP calls).

## Method

The paper derives a third-order stochastic differential equation for SAM (Theorem 4.1), showing that the drift coefficient contains a cubic term proportional to the gradient of the top Hessian eigenvalue, but only when the perturbation vector aligns with the leading eigenvector. Empirically, this alignment is shown to be poor during real training, explaining why SAM's sharpness regularization is weaker than theory predicts. To fix this, Eigen-SAM periodically estimates the top Hessian eigenvector via power iteration, decomposes it into gradient-parallel and gradient-perpendicular components, and augments the standard SAM perturbation with the perpendicular component scaled by a hyperparameter α. This explicitly steers the perturbation toward the direction of maximal curvature, making the third-order term in the SDE effectively minimize λ₁(∇²f). The approach adds only ~2+q/p to 2+2q/p times the cost of SGD (vs. 2× for SAM), and a PAC-Bayes bound (Theorem 3.1) justifies targeting the top eigenvalue for generalization.

## Results

Eigen-SAM consistently outperforms both SGD and SAM across all four image classification benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Fashion-MNIST, SVHN) and three architectures (ResNet-18, ResNet-50, WideResNet-28-10). The most notable gains are on CIFAR-100, where Eigen-SAM achieves 78.3% (ResNet-18), 79.7% (ResNet-50), and 82.8% (WideResNet-28-10), improving over SAM by 0.9%, 0.7%, and 0.8% respectively. On CIFAR-10, Eigen-SAM reaches 95.9% (ResNet-18), 96.2% (ResNet-50), and 96.8% (WideResNet-28-10), a 0.3–0.4% improvement over SAM. For ViT-B-16 fine-tuning, Eigen-SAM achieves 98.5% on CIFAR-10 and 89.8% on CIFAR-100, again surpassing SAM by 0.1–0.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SAM (Foret et al., 2021), the standard sharpness-aware minimization algorithm. Eigen-SAM wins against SAM on every dataset-architecture combination in Table 1, with the largest margin being +0.9% on CIFAR-100 (ResNet-18: 78.3% vs. 77.4%) and consistent +0.3–0.4% gains on CIFAR-10. The second baseline, Explicit Gradient Regularization (EGR; Barrett & Dherin, 2020), is used in Appendix A as a counterexample to the second-order SDE theory of Compagnoni et al. (2023); EGR achieves only 95.0%±0.2% on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18) versus SAM's 95.5%±0.1% and Eigen-SAM's 95.9%±0.2%, confirming that SAM's gains cannot be explained by gradient-norm regularization alone. A third counterexample, Reverse-SAM (using negative perturbation direction), scores 94.4%±0.2%, further below both SAM and Eigen-SAM, reinforcing that the direction of perturbation relative to the top eigenvector matters. Overall, Eigen-SAM dominates all baselines across all reported settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the second-order SDE for SAM derived by Compagnoni et al. (2023) to a third-order SDE, revealing that a cubic term involving the Hessian's top eigenvalue gradient is essential to SAM's implicit sharpness regularization. It builds on the continuous-time approximation framework of Li et al. (2017) and complements the Riemannian flow analysis of Wen et al. (2022) and the quadratic-loss dynamics of Bartlett et al. (2023), but operates under less restrictive assumptions (ρ = O(η^{1/3}) vs. exponentially smaller ρ). The proposed Eigen-SAM algorithm directly modifies SAM's perturbation vector by incorporating the orthogonal component of the estimated top Hessian eigenvector, making it a targeted algorithmic extension of the original SAM formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that perturbation-eigenvector alignment is systematically poor in practice challenges the theoretical narrative that SAM's implicit bias effectively minimizes the top Hessian eigenvalue, suggesting a gap between idealized analyses and real training dynamics. Eigen-SAM closes this gap with a simple, low-overhead modification (q/p ≈ 5% extra compute) that consistently improves generalization across CNNs and ViTs, though the margins (0.3–0.8%) are modest. Open questions include whether the alignment failure persists or worsens at larger scales (e.g., billion-parameter models), whether more frequent eigenvector estimation or adaptive α scheduling could yield larger gains, and whether the third-order SDE framework generalizes to other flatness-aware optimizers.
